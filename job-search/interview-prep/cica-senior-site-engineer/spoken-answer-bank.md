# Cica Senior Site Engineer Spoken Practice

This is material to speak through, then leave behind. Keep the shape of an answer and change the words each time. The companion [question and video source pack](question-and-video-source-pack.md) shows where every practice prompt came from.

## 1. Tell me about yourself

Most of my recent work has been inside active pharmaceutical facilities, leading equipment installations and facility modifications while operations continued around us. That meant getting the scope clear, coordinating the contractors and facility teams, making sure the utilities and work controls were ready, and staying with the job through turnover. Before that I managed engineered water-treatment builds at Evoqua, so I have also seen projects from the equipment-manufacturing side.

The reason this role caught my attention is that it pulls those pieces together. It is chemical systems, field execution, contractors, safety, and a customer who needs the system to work. The fab environment is the new piece for me. The work around it is familiar, and I have enough chemical-engineering background to learn the system without treating it like a black box.

## 2. Why Cica, and why make this move?

I am looking for work where I can own more of the site execution and stay close to the technical system. My recent roles gave me a broad mix of construction coordination, equipment work, shutdowns, and regulated-facility controls. Cica would put that experience around chemical delivery, which is a strong fit with where I started and with the kind of work I enjoy.

The other piece is timing because you are building the operation in Phoenix while the semiconductor market here is growing quickly, and I would be joining at a point where site judgment and a good way of running the work can have a visible effect. That is more interesting to me than stepping into a narrow role where the whole operating model has already been settled.

## 3. Why should we hire you without direct fab experience?

The fab is the learning curve. I would come in expecting to learn Cica's equipment, the customer's standards, and the semiconductor-specific controls quickly.

What transfers immediately is the site work. I know how to get control of a scope, read the design, work through field conditions, coordinate shutdowns, hold contractors to the approved plan, and bring the right technical people into a decision. I have worked across chemical manufacturing, engineered water systems, biotech, and pharmaceutical facilities. Each move required a new process and a new set of standards, while the responsibility for safe, organized execution stayed the same.

So I would be very direct about the learning curve, and just as direct about what I can own on day one. You would get somebody who can run the site side of the work while building depth in the system.

## 4. How do you verify an installation against a P&ID?

The way I look at it, the walkdown should never be my first encounter with the system. By then I have already been through the scope, the design reviews, the submittals, and whatever field decisions came up during construction. The walkdown is where I prove that the physical installation still matches the design intent.

I start with the current drawing and walk the flow path from the source to the point of use. I am matching tags and line information, but I am also looking at the things that become obvious only in the field: whether a valve can be reached, whether the flow direction makes sense, whether vents and drains are usable, and whether the installed routing creates a maintenance or safety problem. I use the layout or isometric for the physical routing because the P&ID alone cannot tell that whole story.

If I find a difference, I establish whether the field installation is wrong or whether the approved design needs a formal answer. A clear installation error gets corrected. A design conflict or field constraint goes through the RFI and change process, then the answer has to make it back into the field and the final record drawings. That last part matters. A decision sitting in an email has done nothing for the person operating the system later.

## 5. Walk me through RFIs, submittals, work plans, and PTWs

They answer different questions at different points in the job. The submittal answers, "Is this the equipment or material the design called for?" An RFI answers a real conflict or missing piece in the documents. The work plan tells me how the contractor intends to execute the activity in the actual facility. The PTW is the final site authorization under the conditions that exist that day.

The practical part is keeping them connected. If an RFI changes a tie-in, that can affect the drawing, the shutdown window, the work plan, the permit, and the people who need to be present. I want one clear answer, an owner, and proof that the crew received it before the work starts. On the day of the job I walk the area again because the paperwork may be a week old while the field keeps moving.

## 6. Tell me about a shutdown you coordinated

A recent example was a laboratory vacuum-line modification at Seagen. A biosafety cabinet needed vacuum service, and the original concept changed after we looked at the field condition. The final scope added another branch to the existing line, which meant touching an operating utility in a lab building.

The actual construction was small. The coordination was the project. We had the mechanical contractor, construction, facilities, safety, and the lab user involved, with a short early-morning window so service could be restored before the lab day began. I worked through the PTW and risk assessment, confirmed the final tie-in and restoration criteria, and stayed onsite through the work and signoff. We also documented existing cabinet damage before anybody touched it, which avoided a completely separate argument during closeout.

The job finished in the window and the user got a clean handoff. I use that story because it shows how I handle live-facility work: the dollar value can be small while the operational risk and the coordination load are real.

## 7. What causes you to stop work?

Anything that breaks the basis under which the work was approved. If the line identity is uncertain, the isolation cannot be verified, the crew is outside the work plan, or the permit conditions have changed, the job pauses. The same goes for an immediate exposure, containment, or life-safety concern.

My first move is to make the area stable and get the people who own the condition involved. Then we establish what changed and what has to be corrected before the job resumes. Stop-work authority works best when people see it as a way to regain control, rather than as theater or punishment. I want contractors raising the concern early because they trust that we will deal with it clearly.

## 8. What material would you use for an organic solvent?

I would start with the solvent and the service conditions because "organic solvent" by itself is too broad for a responsible material decision. I need to know the chemical, concentration, temperature, pressure, purity requirement, and whether the service involves storage, transfer, seals, or instrument components.

For high-purity chemical delivery, 316L stainless and fluoropolymers such as PFA or PTFE are common parts of the conversation, depending on compatibility and cleanliness requirements. I would check the approved specification and compatibility data across the full wetted path. The pipe can be perfectly compatible while a gasket, valve seat, pump component, or instrument creates the failure.

My role in the field is to understand the design basis well enough to catch a mismatch or an undocumented substitution before it disappears into the installation. For a final selection outside the established standard, I would bring in the process or materials owner and document the decision.

## 9. What was your role in inspection or qualification?

My role has usually been getting the project ready for the specialist and then owning the follow-through. I make sure the physical work has reached the right point, the drawings and test records are available, the system owner and contractors are present, and the equipment can be operated under the test conditions.

During the inspection or certification walk, I stay close enough to understand what failed and where; afterward I turn the findings into assigned work, bring the right trade or engineer back in, and coordinate the retest. The specialist owns the measurement or certificate, while I own readiness and closure on the project side.

That distinction is important in a regulated facility. I can speak in detail about the coordination, the field condition, and how we closed a deficiency, while the technical authority remains with the person qualified to issue the result.

## 10. How do you handle a contractor who is behind schedule?

First I want to know whether that activity is driving the milestone. A schedule can show a lot of red while one unresolved decision, access issue, or long-lead item is controlling the finish date. I compare the field condition with the schedule, speak with the superintendent, and isolate the constraint.

From there, the recovery has to change something real. Maybe we add a shift, split the work area, resequence around an access issue, pull an inspection forward, or escalate a decision that has been sitting with the owner. I put names and dates against that plan and watch it through short field milestones. "Work faster" is pressure. A recovery plan changes labor, sequence, access, or decision time in a way we can measure.

## 11. How do you communicate across technical teams and cultures?

I start with the decision the other person needs to make. The customer or an executive usually needs the condition, impact, recommendation, and timing. The engineer or contractor needs the drawing, the exact field condition, and the technical constraint. Both deserve the same truth, presented at the level they can use.

Across languages and disciplines, I rely heavily on marked-up drawings, photographs, short written recaps, and closed-loop confirmation. Idioms and long verbal explanations create room for two people to leave with different answers. If the technical depth sits with somebody else, I bring that person in and stay with the discussion until I can explain the decision accurately. I am comfortable using an SME. My responsibility is to make sure the field gets one clear direction.

## 12. Have you managed people or built a team?

I have managed a production shift of about 20 people at Evoqua, with daily responsibility for safety, staffing, work assignments, quality issues, and output. My later project roles have been more matrixed: contractors, vendors, engineers, facilities teams, and users who answer to different organizations but still need to move against one plan.

Building an entire semiconductor site organization from zero would be a larger step than either of those experiences. I would begin by defining the work and the coverage the site needs, then make authority and escalation very clear. From there, people need field onboarding, technical qualification, and a daily operating rhythm that shows where the risks and decisions are. I can own that structure, and I would use Cica's technical leadership closely while the local team develops.

## When an older example earns its way into the answer

Recent site work comes first. Reach back to GE or Dow only when the question is specifically about chemical-treatment design, wastewater, clarifiers, confined-space work, or early EHS experience and the older example is the cleanest direct evidence.

Use a short bridge: "This example is earlier in my career, but it is the most direct chemical-system example." Then answer the technical question and return to the present. The point is the relevance of the example, rather than a chronological tour of the resume.

## Recording drill

Pick one question, take ten seconds, and answer without reading. Keep a normal answer around one minute and a project story under two. Listen once and score five things from zero to two:

- Did the answer begin with the answer?
- Did it sound like speech?
- Was the example recent unless an older one had a clear technical reason?
- Could every ownership claim and technical detail survive a follow-up?
- Did the answer end cleanly?

Send the rough recording back for grading. The useful work will be identifying where the answer becomes vague, inflated, overly technical, or unlike your normal voice, then doing another pass from the same question.
