# TEFL curriculum benchmark & gap analysis — verified research

Deep-research pass, 2026-06-07. 5 search angles, 24 sources fetched, 116 claims extracted, 25 verified by 3-vote adversarial check: 24 confirmed, 1 killed. Companion to [business-research.md](business-research.md); feeds [course-generation-pipeline.md](course-generation-pipeline.md).

## 1. The benchmark: what a TEFL curriculum contains

**Regulated/prestige tier (primary specs, all 3-0 verified):**
- **CELTA** (Cambridge syllabus PDF): 5 topic areas — learners/teachers & context; language analysis & awareness; language skills (reading, listening, speaking, **writing**); planning & resources; developing teaching skills & professionalism. The "120 hours" is 120 *contact* hours plus a minimum ~80 self-study hours (~200 total), including **6 assessed teaching-practice hours with real learners**.
- **Trinity CertTESOL**: 5 assessed units — Teaching Skills; Language Awareness & Skills; The Learner Profile; Materials Assignment; The Unknown Language (trainee learns ~4h of a language foreign to them). Minimum 6 hours supervised assessed teaching practice.
- **Ofqual Level 5 (Gatehouse/i-to-i spec, V5 2022)**: 4 mandatory 45-GLH units — Classroom & Learners; Lesson Planning & Resources; Vocabulary/Grammar/Pronunciation; The Four Skills. 240 TQT. The Diploma adds One-to-One/Online and Business English units.

**Budget online tier (provider-stated, 3-0 verified as "what they claim to contain"):**
- **ITTT**: 20 units, ~50/50 grammar vs methodology (provider's own characterization).
- **TEFL Org Premier**: Methodology 50h, Grammar 30h, Video Observation 20h, Teaching Online 10h, Large Classes 10h.
- **Bridge**: 60h core (13 modules) + 40h grammar + 20h specialized (Young Learners + Business English).
- **i-to-i**: 5 thin units, assessed by quizzes/written assignments only.

**The must-have core (appears in essentially every course):** learners & teaching context, grammar/language analysis, the four skills *including writing*, phonology/pronunciation, lesson planning & resources, classroom management, error correction, evaluation/testing, coursebook & materials use.

**The two real differentiators:** (1) the regulated tier's **assessed live practicum** — budget courses universally omit it or sell it as a paid add-on (passive video observation at best); (2) breadth of learner-context coverage (young learners, business English, one-to-one) — the better budget courses include these, thin ones don't.

## 2. Gap analysis: teacherrecord vs the benchmark (3-0 verified)

⚠️ Correction (2026-06-07): the research pass was fed only modules mid1–mid15; the full map (course-structure-reference.md) also has mid16 (Writing, 6.8k words), mid17 (advanced grammar), mid18 (behaviour/storytelling/drama), and mid20 (limited resources/large classes/discipline/culture). So writing and classroom management are **thin, not absent** — the rest of the analysis stands.

Missing or thin:
- **Writing skills** — exists (mid16) but at 6.8k words it is the thinnest of the four skills; writing is a named, assessed Four-Skills component in every regulated spec.
- **Classroom management / discipline** — covered across mid18/mid20, though without the structured learning-outcomes treatment the regulated spec requires.
- **Teaching young learners** and **business English** — absent; precisely what better budget competitors (TEFL Org, Bridge) advertise.
- **Assessed practicum** — absent, like all budget courses (this is the structural tier difference, not a fixable content gap at this price point).

Over-weighted: **grammar** (a whole module plus grammar threaded through others), consistent with the budget-tier ~50% language-analysis pattern.

Wrong: the **"Learning Styles" unit (module-mid13)**. Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2009, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) — verified against the journal full text — found virtually no adequately-designed (crossover/aptitude-by-treatment) studies supporting style-matched instruction, and several adequate studies contradicting it: "there is no adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning-styles assessments into general educational practice." Subsequent literature reinforces this. **Our course should teach this as a known myth** (which doubles as a credibility differentiator over teacherrecord).

Caveat against overreach: the regulated specs use traditional proficiency bands (beginner/elementary/intermediate/advanced) for level differentiation; CEFR appears only as an entry-English requirement. So frame the teacherrecord gap as "no explicit level/proficiency framework," not "no CEFR."

## 3. Coursebooks (medium confidence — topic verified, copyright bottom-line is inference)

Across both tiers, published coursebook series (Interchange, Headway, English File) appear only as a *discussed topic* — "Course Books and Lesson Materials" (ITTT unit 14), CELTA's planning & resources area, Gatehouse's Lesson Planning & Resources unit. No evidence any cert course excerpts or licenses coursebook content. **Bottom line: an original 120-hr course needs no coursebook license.** Methods prose + original/public-domain examples (the ~124k-word State Dept corpus in `sources/`) suffice; coursebooks get a unit *about* selecting/using/adapting them. The user's Interchange copies are useful as personal reference for what coursebooks look like — not as source text. (The copyright inference and any brief-quotation fair-use question remain unverified legal questions if we ever want verbatim excerpts; default to never excerpting.)

## 4. Effectiveness uplift — verified citations (passes 2026-06-07)

Citation-verification pass (5 angles, 22 sources, 95 claims extracted, 24 confirmed 3-vote; publisher paywalls 403'd bots, so verification ran on Crossref/ERIC/institutional repositories — abstracts verbatim, full-text effect-size detail not always inspected). Verified and citable per-unit:

**Task-based language teaching (genuinely contested on magnitude, settled on direction).** Bryfonski & McKay (2019), "TBLT implementation and evaluation: A meta-analysis," *Language Teaching Research* 23(5):603-632, doi:10.1177/1362168817744389 — 52 studies, overall d = 0.93 (abstract verbatim). Superseded for course use by Xuan, Cheung & Liu (2025), "How effective is task-based language teaching...? A technical comment on Bryfonski and McKay (2019)," *LTR* 29(7), doi:10.1177/13621688221131127 — re-analysis g = 0.61, citing loose inclusion criteria, oversimplified outcome effect sizes, neglected moderators; Boers & Faez (2023, *LTR*, doi:10.1177/13621688231167573) independently skeptical. **Unit takeaway: TBLT works; teach the tighter g = 0.61 figure, present d = 0.93 as the disputed original.**

**Explicit vs implicit grammar instruction (settled direction, contested generalizability).** Norris & Ortega (2000), "Effectiveness of L2 Instruction," *Language Learning* 50(3):417-528, doi:10.1111/0023-8333.00136 — 49 studies: focused instruction yields large gains (~d 0.96), explicit > implicit, Focus-on-Form ≈ Focus-on-FormS (all abstract verbatim). Replicated by Spada & Tomita (2010), *LL* 60(2):263-308, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00562.x — 41 studies, explicit wins for BOTH simple and complex forms; and Goo, Granena, Yilmaz & Novella (2015), in Rebuschat (Ed.), *Implicit and Explicit Learning of Languages* (Benjamins SiBil 48), 443-482, doi:10.1075/sibil.48.18goo — 34 studies, explicit > implicit again. Counterweight: Kang, Sok & Han (2019), *LTR* 23(4):428-453, doi:10.1177/1362168818776671 — 35 years of form-focused instruction, only a MINOR explicit/implicit difference; moderators (outcome-measure type, proficiency, setting, intensity) carry the variance. **Unit takeaway: teach grammar explicitly without apology — four meta-analyses agree on direction — but flag that the advantage shrinks on communicative/implicit outcome measures (the measures favor explicit knowledge).**

**Oral corrective feedback (settled).** Lyster & Saito (2010), "Oral Feedback in Classroom SLA: A Meta-Analysis," *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 32(2):265-302 — 15 classroom studies (N=827): corrective feedback has significant, durable effects; prompts (eliciting self-correction) outperform recasts; longer treatments → larger effects. **Unit takeaway: correct errors — it works and lasts; prefer prompting students to self-correct over simply recasting the correct form.** ⚠️ Li (2010) is cited in the field as corroboration (~33 studies, d≈0.64) but was NOT independently verified — do not cite until confirmed.

**Corrective feedback, corroboration (verified via Crossref + abstract).** Li (2010), "The Effectiveness of Corrective Feedback in SLA: A Meta-Analysis," *Language Learning* 60(2):309-365, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x — 33 primary studies (22 published + 11 dissertations), designed as an update/complement to prior meta-analyses. Complements Lyster & Saito's classroom-focused result.

**Retrieval practice & spacing for vocabulary (settled).** Karpicke & Roediger (2008), "The Critical Importance of Retrieval for Learning," *Science* 319(5865):966-968, doi:10.1126/science.1152408 — foreign-language vocabulary (Swahili-English pairs): repeated retrieval produced a large delayed-recall advantage (~80% vs ~33-36% at one week, d = 4.03), while repeated *studying* after the first correct recall had no measurable effect (abstract verbatim: "Repeated studying after learning had no effect on delayed recall, but repeated testing produced a large positive effect"). L2-specific: Nakata (2015), *SSLA* 37(4):677-711, doi:10.1017/S0272263114000825 — spaced beats massed (large effect); expanding schedules show a limited-but-significant edge over equal spacing. Meta-analysis: Kim & Webb (2022), *Language Learning* 72(1):269-319, doi:10.1111/lang.12479 — 98 effect sizes, 48 experiments, N = 3,411, across spaced-vs-massed, longer-vs-shorter, and equal-vs-expanding contrasts. **Unit takeaway: vocabulary teaching should be built on testing/recall and spaced review, not re-presentation — re-reading a word list does almost nothing once it's been recalled correctly once.**

**Extensive reading (settled direction; mind the attribution).** Nakanishi (2015), *TESOL Quarterly* 49(1):6-37, doi:10.1002/tesq.157 — 34 studies, 3,942 participants: d = 0.46 (group comparisons) / d = 0.71 (pre-post). Jeon & Day (2016), *Reading in a Foreign Language* 28(2):246-265 — 49 studies, 5,919 participants: d = 0.57 (exp-vs-control) / d = 0.79 (pre-post). ⚠️ Keep the two sets of numbers separate — they're routinely conflated. Caution: Hamada (2020) argues selection bias inflates these (adjusted ≈ d 0.37); Sangers et al. (2025) is the most recent update (small-to-medium effects across domains — do not attribute the older numbers to it). **Unit takeaway: extensive reading reliably improves reading proficiency; teach it as a strong supplement, with honest effect sizes.**

**Comprehensible input / Krashen (teach with nuance).** The citable falsifiability critique is Gregg (1984), "Krashen's Monitor and Occam's Razar [sic — published typo]," *Applied Linguistics* 5(2):79-100, doi:10.1093/applin/5.2.79. **Unit takeaway: input is necessary for acquisition and Krashen's influence on communicative practice is real, but the strong Input Hypothesis is untestable as stated — present it as historically important, not settled science.**

**L1 use in the classroom (the orthodoxy shifted).** Hall & Cook (2012), "Own-language use in language teaching and learning," *Language Teaching* 45(3):271-308, doi:10.1017/S0261444812000067 — the state-of-the-art review documenting the field's re-evaluation away from the English-only monolingual assumption toward judicious own-language use. **Unit takeaway: English-mainly, not English-only — judicious L1 use is research-supported, the dogmatic ban is not.**

**Learning styles (settled, double-verified).** Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2009), *PSPI* 9(3):105-119 (see §2), reinforced by Willingham, Hughes & Dobolyi (2015), "The Scientific Status of Learning Styles Theories," *Teaching of Psychology* 42(3):266-271, doi:10.1177/0098628315589505.

All seven uplift topics now have verified, citable anchors. Remaining minor gap: Li (2010)'s overall effect size (d ≈ 0.64 is commonly cited) wasn't read from the full text — confirm before printing a number; same for Hamada (2020) and Sangers et al. (2025) full bibliographic detail if cited directly.
