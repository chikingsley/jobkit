## Section B — Review (2 points, 1 each)

**13. Explain how color-coded ink on the word chart supports students during the guided writing activity, according to the unit.**

   *Answer: Each part of speech is written in a different color, so a student can see immediately which word is a noun, which is a verb, and which is an adverb, doing the work that a grammar explanation might otherwise do without requiring students to hold abstract rules in memory while composing.*
   *Anchor: "unit 8.3 quiz"*

**14. The unit identifies two patterns that account for most weak lesson aims. Name both patterns and give a brief example of each.**

   *Answer: The first pattern is describing the activity rather than the outcome, for example writing 'students will play the card game' instead of specifying what language students will be able to use. The second pattern is writing at a level of generality no single lesson can address, for example 'students will develop their descriptive language skills across a range of topics,' which sets no observable endpoint for a forty-five-minute class.*
   *Anchor: "unit 10.1 quiz"*

## Section C — Scenario (5 points)

**15. Mr. Osei is planning a revision lesson for his adult intermediate class. A colleague suggests dividing the learners permanently by 'learning style': Daniel should receive only diagrams because he draws in his notebook, Yuki should work alone because she says she prefers quiet study, and Carlos should receive only verbal drills because he has struggled to retain vocabulary from reading. Drawing on the module's treatment of learner differences, learning strategies, and the learning-styles evidence, what should Mr. Osei say to his colleague, and what should he do instead?**

   *Answer: Mr. Osei should not adopt the colleague's matching approach. Although Daniel's diagram-drawing and Yuki's preference for solitary work are real and worth noticing, the research reviewed in the module shows that diagnosing a fixed style and then restricting instruction to fit that diagnosis does not produce the predicted learning benefit. This is the meshing hypothesis, and Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, and Bjork (2008) found no adequate evidence to support it. Willingham, Hughes, and Dobolyi (2015) went further, arguing that learning-styles theories should be treated as a popular but scientifically debunked idea rather than a basis for planning decisions. Acting on the colleague's advice would therefore mean building the lesson around a framework the evidence does not support.

Mr. Osei should instead vary instruction in response to actual learner needs without locking anyone into a single mode. For Carlos's vocabulary-retention difficulty, he can teach several cognitive strategies, such as visualization, association, chunking, and mnemonics, then help Carlos monitor which strategies work for particular tasks. He can build in a metacognitive pause that asks every learner to explain how they approached the task. He can also offer a sequence or choice of follow-up tasks, such as a concept map, a written summary, and partner discussion, without treating any task as a learner's permanent channel. This approach honors differences in proficiency, personality, motivation, preference, and strategy while avoiding unsupported labels. A full-credit response should reject diagnosed style matching with the correct research rationale and propose at least two concrete, non-labeling adaptations tied to the learners' actual needs.*
   *Anchor: "the sorting-and-matching procedure is the unsupported step. Learner differences are real"*
