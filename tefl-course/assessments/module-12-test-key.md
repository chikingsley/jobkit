## Section C — Scenario (5 points)

**15. Mr. Osei is planning a reading lesson for his mixed-level adult class. After observing his students for two weeks, he has noticed that Daniel always draws diagrams in his notebook, Yuki frequently asks to work alone, and Carlos often struggles to retain new vocabulary. A colleague suggests that Mr. Osei should diagnose each learner's dominant learning style — visual for Daniel, solitary for Yuki — and then design separate instructional tracks matched to those styles, so that Daniel receives only diagram-based tasks and Yuki always works independently. Mr. Osei is unsure whether to follow this advice. What would you recommend he do instead, and why?**

   *Answer: Mr. Osei should not adopt the colleague's matching approach. Although Daniel's diagram-drawing and Yuki's preference for solitary work are real and worth noticing, the research reviewed in the module shows that diagnosing a fixed style and then restricting instruction to fit that diagnosis does not produce the predicted learning benefit — this is the meshing hypothesis, and Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, and Bjork (2009) found no adequate evidence to support it. Willingham, Hughes, and Dobolyi (2015) went further, arguing that learning-styles theories should be treated as a popular but scientifically debunked idea rather than a basis for planning decisions. Acting on the colleague's advice would therefore mean building the lesson around a framework the evidence does not support.

What Mr. Osei should do instead is apply differentiated instruction — varying his teaching in order to create the best learning experience possible for individuals or small groups — without locking any learner into a single mode. For Carlos's vocabulary retention difficulties, Mr. Osei can explicitly teach cognitive strategies such as visualization, making associations, chunking, and using mnemonics, since successful learners draw on several of these at once depending on the task. He can also build in a metacognitive moment — pausing to ask students how they are approaching a task — so that learners like Yuki develop awareness of their own mental processes and can self-regulate over time. For motivation, Mr. Osei can offer a range of follow-up mini-tasks (e.g., drawing a concept map, writing a summary, discussing with a partner) so that students like Daniel can select a mode that suits their strengths without being permanently confined to it. This approach honours the real individual differences the module maps — proficiency level, personality, motivation, preferred strategies — while remaining grounded in what the research actually supports.*
   *Anchor: "diagnosing a fixed style and then restricting instruction to fit that diagnosis does not produce the predicted benefit"*
