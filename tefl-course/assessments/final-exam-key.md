### Module 2 — English Grammar for Teachers I

**6. Explain why the sentence 'She sings beautiful' is grammatically incorrect, identifying the word class of 'beautiful' and stating what word class is required instead, with a reason.**

   *Answer: In 'She sings beautiful', the word 'beautiful' is an adjective, but because it modifies the dynamic verb 'sings' rather than describing the subject, an adverb is required. The correct form is 'beautifully', which describes the manner of the action.*
   *Anchor: "sings is a dynamic verb, not a linking verb, so the modifier describes the manner of the action rather than the subject herself"*

**7. Explain why a compound-complex sentence requires more than just two main clauses to qualify as compound-complex, and give the minimum structural requirements for this sentence type.**

   *Answer: A compound-complex sentence must contain at least two main clauses AND at least one subordinate clause. Two main clauses alone produce only a compound sentence; the subordinate clause is the additional requirement that makes it compound-complex.*
   *Anchor: "it contains at least two main clauses and at least one subordinate clause"*

**8. The unit contrasts 'I lived in Rome' with 'I have lived in Rome' as a classroom minimal pair. Explain what meaning difference the unit says these two sentences convey, and why minimal pairs are recommended as a teaching technique.**

   *Answer: The first sentence presents the period of residence as a closed, bounded fact with no connection to the present, while the second presents it as part of the speaker's current biography, still relevant now. Minimal pairs are recommended because two sentences identical except for the verb form isolate the aspectual contrast precisely, allowing the meaning difference to emerge from context rather than from a definition.*
   *Anchor: "The first presents the period of residence as a closed, bounded fact... the second presents it as part of the speaker's current biography, relevant now."*

**9. A student says 'I am meeting my cousin tomorrow' to describe a pre-arranged event. According to the unit, why is this form more appropriate than 'I will meet my cousin tomorrow'?**

   *Answer: The present continuous is appropriate because it signals prior planning and arrangement. Using 'will' instead strips away the sense of prior planning and replaces it with a spontaneous decision or prediction made at the moment of speaking, which misrepresents the nature of the event.*
   *Anchor: "Using 'will' for a pre-arranged event strips away the sense of prior planning and replaces it with a decision or prediction made at the moment of speaking"*

**10. The unit describes three practical classroom approaches for responding to predictable L1-influenced errors. Name all three approaches in the order the unit presents them.**

   *Answer: The three approaches are: (1) anticipatory teaching, preparing for likely errors before they appear by building problem areas into the syllabus from the start; (2) error analysis during feedback, identifying the L1 rule learners are applying when marking written work and explaining the English rule that replaces it; and (3) contrastive explanation, pointing out clearly and briefly how English differs from the learner's L1 on a specific grammatical or phonological point.*
   *Anchor: "The first practical approach is anticipatory teaching... The second approach is error analysis during feedback... The third approach is contrastive explanation"*

### Module 3 — English Grammar for Teachers II

**11. Explain why a teacher should present semi-modals such as 'have to' as a distinct pattern rather than applying the same rules used for core modals like 'can' or 'must'.**

   *Answer: Semi-modals behave like ordinary verbs: they require the full infinitive marker and use do-support in questions and negatives, whereas core modals take a bare infinitive and form questions and negatives without do-support. Applying core modal rules to semi-modals produces errors, such as treating 'have to' as if it never takes a third-person singular inflection.*
   *Anchor: "applying the rules for can or must directly to have to produces errors"*

**12. A mixed conditional sentence reads: 'If she had taken that job in Berlin, she would be fluent in German by now.' Identify the time frame of each clause and explain what meaning this combination expresses that neither the second nor the third conditional alone could convey.**

   *Answer: The if-clause (past perfect) refers to a decision not made in the past; the main clause (would + bare infinitive) describes a present state that would exist today as a consequence. This combination expresses a past missed opportunity alongside its imagined present-day result simultaneously, which neither the second conditional (present/future hypothetical) nor the third conditional (closed past) can capture alone.*
   *Anchor: "The if-clause refers to a decision that was not made in the past; the main clause describes the present state that would exist today as a consequence"*

**13. A friend says to you: "I moved two weeks ago." You report this the following month. Rewrite the time expression 'two weeks ago' as it should appear in your reported sentence, and name the rule that governs the change.**

   *Answer: 'Two weeks ago' becomes 'two weeks before' (or 'two weeks previously'). The rule is that time adverbials shift to reflect the reporter's position in time, replacing 'ago' with 'before' or 'previously'.*
   *Anchor: "The construction "ago" is replaced by "before" or "previously," so "two weeks ago" becomes "two weeks before.""*

**14. The unit argues that recycling is what separates a vocabulary lesson from vocabulary acquisition. What does the unit say must happen for a phrasal verb to become available for spontaneous use?**

   *Answer: The item must accumulate repeated meaningful contact across different lessons and skills. The unit states that a phrasal verb met once and never seen again is unlikely to move into a learner's active repertoire, so teachers should return to the same items in different contexts—for example, in reading, speaking, and writing tasks across multiple lessons.*
   *Anchor: "That accumulation is what makes a word available for spontaneous use."*

**15. The unit recommends that learners keep a collocation notebook. What specific form should each entry take, and why does the unit say this format is preferable to recording bare word lists?**

   *Answer: Each entry should be a complete example sentence (e.g., 'We rely on the bus to get to school' rather than just 'rely on'). The unit says full sentences keep the verb-preposition or adjective-preposition combination intact, whereas bare lists invite learners to treat the preposition as an afterthought.*
   *Anchor: "Each entry should be a complete sentence; for rely on, a learner writes We rely on the bus to get to school rather than the bare phrase alone"*

**16. The unit recommends that teachers build a bank of short situational dialogs organized by communicative function. Explain, using the unit's own reasoning, why encountering the same functional expression across multiple different situational contexts is more valuable than encountering it only once.**

   *Answer: When learners encounter the same expression in several different situational contexts, the function becomes detached from any single context and available for genuine use, rather than being tied to one specific topic or scenario.*
   *Anchor: "the function becomes detached from any single context and available for genuine use"*

**24. The unit distinguishes lexical collocation from the grammatical patterning that function word bricks support. Explain the difference, and describe how a Word Bricks collection can give students practice with each.**

   *Answer: Collocations are fixed or strongly preferred word combinations, such as 'make a decision,' that cannot be predicted from individual word meanings alone. Function words support something different: grammatical patterning, sometimes called colligation, because they are the glue that binds content items into grammatically complete structures. A collection stocked with plenty of function bricks lets students rehearse that grammatical patterning as they build sentences (avoiding incomplete strings like 'cat drink water'), while lexical collocation can be layered on by asking students to pair verb and noun bricks that genuinely go together and reject combinations a proficient speaker would not produce.*
   *Anchor: "These are the glue words that bind content items into grammatically complete structures"*

   *Anchor: "identifies a topic that will interest their specific students and considers what language those students genuinely need; the planning begins with the learner"*

### Module 6 — Phonology and Pronunciation

**26. A student writes 'wether' instead of 'weather.' Using the concept introduced in the final section of the unit, explain why this error is not simply careless and what it reveals about the student's phonological processing.**

   *Answer: The student has correctly heard the spoken word but chosen the wrong written form because 'weather' and 'whether' are homophones — two words with identical pronunciation but different spellings. The error shows the student is processing the sound accurately; the confusion is graphic, not phonological. The teacher should address the module of the confusion rather than treating it as carelessness.*
   *Anchor: "'weather' and 'whether' are homophones, two words with identical pronunciation but different spellings, and the learner has chosen the wrong graphic form for a sound sequence they have heard correctly"*

**27. What distinguishes a diphthong from a monophthong, and what problem arises for a learner who holds the starting position of a diphthong without completing the glide?**

   *Answer: A monophthong is produced with the mouth holding a single steady position throughout the sound, while a diphthong involves the mouth gliding from one position to a second position within the same syllable. If a learner holds the starting position of a diphthong without completing the glide, they will produce something that sounds like a different word entirely.*
   *Anchor: "a learner who holds the starting position without completing the glide will produce something that sounds like a different word entirely"*

**28. The unit describes a student named Wei producing 'I want to go to the store' with seven syllables of nearly equal length. Identify the three natural stress beats a native English speaker would place in that sentence, and explain what happens to the unstressed words between those beats.**

   *Answer: The three natural stress beats are WANT, GO, and STORE. The unstressed function words 'to' and 'the' are compressed and reduced between those beats, keeping the rhythmic pulse steady rather than lengthening the overall utterance.*
   *Anchor: "The sentence has three natural stress beats: WANT, GO, and STORE. The words 'to' and 'the' shrink almost to nothing between those beats"*

**29. Explain why the unit presents minimal pairs as particularly useful for learners whose first language does not contain a sound contrast found in English, and what sequence of activities does the unit recommend for teaching with minimal pairs?**

   *Answer: Minimal pairs are especially useful because a student may produce two distinct English words identically without realising the difference affects comprehension, since the contrast simply does not exist in their first language. The unit recommends a structured sequence: first, listen-and-discriminate activities so students can reliably perceive the contrast, and then drilling for production once perception is established.*
   *Anchor: "a contrast does not exist in a student's first language... Separating perception from production matters because students often need to hear a distinction reliably before they can make it consistently"*

**64. Ms. Reyes teaches an adult intermediate class whose immediate goal is handling workplace problems in English. Carlos wants language for supervising colleagues, Wei completes grammar exercises accurately but contributes little in open discussion, and Fatima speaks readily but says, 'You must to submit the form' and 'She musts call the manager.' The coursebook offers only a page of modal-verb drills. Design a communicative lesson response that (1) states a suitable performance objective, (2) diagnoses and corrects Fatima's two modal errors, and (3) uses the pre-task, task-cycle, and language-focus phases to turn the coursebook material into a purposeful task. Explain why your choices fit these learners and the context.**

   *Answer: A suitable objective is: 'By the end of the lesson, students will give and justify two recommendations for a workplace problem using must, have to, and should accurately enough to be understood.' This targets the learners' stated workplace purpose and names observable performance rather than a vague aim such as 'learn modals.' Fatima's first sentence violates the bare-infinitive rule: a core modal takes the base verb without to, so the repair is 'You must submit the form.' Her second adds third-person -s to an invariant modal; the repair is 'She must call the manager.'

In the pre-task phase, Ms. Reyes can present a short workplace case, clarify the required outcome, and let pairs identify what the manager and staff need to decide. She can make key situation vocabulary available without teaching every modal answer in advance. During the task cycle, groups receive different constraints, agree on a response plan, prepare a concise recommendation, and report it to another group or to the class. This gives Carlos useful supervisory language and gives Wei planning time and a defined contribution before public reporting. Ms. Reyes monitors for meaning and records modal patterns without turning the task into a running correction drill. In language focus, she posts anonymous examples such as 'must to submit' and 'she musts call,' elicits the repairs, compares must, have to, and should in the meanings that emerged, and follows with brief focused practice. The drills can now serve as post-task consolidation rather than the lesson's purpose.

The design is communicative because learners use grammar to reach a workplace decision for an audience, and it remains explicit because form is analyzed after the meaning-focused cycle. A full-credit response should include a measurable context-appropriate objective, both modal diagnoses and corrections, all three TBLT phases with the task/planning/report logic visible, and at least one justified adaptation to the named learners.*
   *Anchor: "A widely used TBLT framework moves through three phases: pre-task, task cycle, and language focus"*

**66. Ms. Reyes must teach and assess a 50-minute discussion lesson for 58 mixed-proficiency secondary students in a room with fixed desks.
