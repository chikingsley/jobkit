## Section C — Scenario (5 points)

**15. Mr. Osei introduces 20 new vocabulary items to his class on Monday. He spends 35 minutes drilling the words with the whole group, displaying each word alongside its translation on the board while learners read through the list repeatedly. He plans to move on to a new topic on Tuesday and not return to these words until the end-of-term test. Carlos and Wei both feel confident after the session because the words feel familiar. Yuki, however, quietly tells Mr. Osei she is worried she will forget everything by next week. Is Yuki's concern justified? What would you advise Mr. Osei to change about his approach, and why?**

   *Answer: Yuki's concern is entirely justified. Mr. Osei is using two practices the module identifies as ineffective: massed practice and re-presentation without retrieval. First, displaying each word alongside its translation while learners read through the list is re-presentation, not retrieval. Carlos and Wei feel confident because repeated exposure to the word-translation pair creates a sense of familiarity, but familiarity is not durable storage. The module explains this directly through the Karpicke and Roediger (2008) finding: learners who kept re-studying word pairs recalled only about a third (around 33 percent) on a delayed test one week later, compared to roughly 80 percent for learners who practiced retrieval. Mr. Osei should restructure the activity so that after an initial presentation, learners cover the translation and attempt to produce the meaning from memory before checking — exactly what Carlos does in the module's own example. Second, planning a single 35-minute block with no return until the end of term is massed practice. The module cites both Nakata (2015) and the Kim and Webb (2022) meta-analysis of 48 experiments to confirm that spaced practice produces stronger retention than massed practice. Mr. Osei should redistribute the same vocabulary across several short sessions — for example, 10 minutes on Monday, a brief retrieval activity on Wednesday, and a further review the following week. Each return should arrive just as memory has begun to weaken, so that the effortful reconstruction strengthens the memory trace. If Mr. Osei also wants to refine his spacing design, the module notes that expanding schedules (progressively longer gaps after each successful retrieval) show a limited but significant edge over equal-interval spacing, though the margin is modest. The core priority is simply to stop massing all practice into one session and to replace passive re-reading with active retrieval at every review point.*
   *Anchor: "learners who kept retrieving the words recalled about 80 percent of them, while learners who kept re-studying recalled only about a third"*
   *Rubric (5 points): identifies massed practice as ineffective (1); identifies re-presentation without retrieval as the second flaw (1); recommends spaced retrieval practice as the fix (2); grounds the answer in the module's evidence (1).*
