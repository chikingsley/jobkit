# Coursebooks: Choosing, Using, and Adapting

## What a Coursebook Is and What It Is Not

A coursebook is a published, sequenced teaching resource that provides syllabus structure, language input, and practice activities across a defined level or course. It organizes what is taught, in what order, and through what kinds of tasks, giving both teacher and learner a shared frame of reference from the first lesson to the last. That organizational function is its defining characteristic.

Several related materials are often confused with the coursebook itself. A workbook provides additional written practice, usually for homework, but contains no new teaching sequence of its own. Supplementary materials, such as grammar reference books or skills-focused readers, address particular areas without covering a full course. A teacher's resource pack supplies photocopiable activities, tests, and procedural notes to support the teacher's planning rather than to place content directly in front of learners. Each of these serves a distinct purpose, and treating them as interchangeable with the coursebook leads to planning confusion.

The central tension in using a coursebook is that the book is a tool, not a curriculum authority. Publishers design coursebooks for a broad, imagined audience. No single class matches that audience precisely, and the teacher remains the professional decision-maker, responsible for judging whether a given unit, task, or text serves the actual learners in the room on a particular day. That judgment must be exercised from the outset rather than deferred until problems become visible. The book offers a starting point from which the teacher determines what is appropriate for a specific group.

When professional judgment is suspended, problems follow quickly. Consider a class of ten-year-olds working through a general English coursebook whose third unit focuses on booking a hotel room by telephone. The unit assumes familiarity with travel, adult transactions, and the social context of speaking to a receptionist. A teacher who moves through the pages without adaptation might produce exchanges like this:

T: "So, you want a double room for two nights. What do you say?"
S: "I don't know what a double room is."
T: "It's a room with a big bed. Now read line three."

The language may be grammatically accessible, but the situation is remote from the children's experience, and motivation collapses. The coursebook has not failed; the decision to follow it without thought has. Recognizing the coursebook as one resource among several, rather than as an unquestionable script, is the first practical skill addressed in this unit.

## Evaluating a Coursebook Before You Adopt It

Before committing to a coursebook, a teacher needs a clear set of criteria to apply systematically. Browsing a few pages in a staffroom gives an impression, not an evaluation. A structured checklist turns a subjective feeling about a book into a set of specific, answerable questions.

The first criterion is level and progression. A coursebook pitched at the wrong level wastes class time from the first lesson. The teacher should read through the first three or four units and ask whether the vocabulary load, sentence complexity, and assumed knowledge match the learners who will sit in the room. A book designed for teenagers who have studied English at school for two years will frustrate a group of adult complete beginners, even if both groups are nominally at A1, because the assumed cultural and educational background differs considerably.

Topic and cultural relevance forms the second criterion. The themes a book uses to present language should feel plausible and interesting to the specific learners, not merely inoffensive in a general sense. A unit built around planning a ski holiday may present perfectly graded language, yet leave a class of adult learners in a coastal city with no mountains nearby feeling that the material has nothing to do with their lives. The teacher should scan the topic list and ask honestly whether at least the majority of themes will generate genuine engagement from this particular group.

The third criterion concerns skills balance. A well-designed coursebook gives meaningful attention to reading, writing, listening, and speaking across each unit, rather than treating one or two skills as the main event and the others as brief add-ons. The teacher should count, roughly, how many activities in a sample unit develop productive skills and how many develop receptive ones. A book that offers rich reading texts but only a single speaking prompt per unit will require the teacher to supplement heavily if oral fluency is a course goal.

Grammar and vocabulary coverage is the fourth area to examine. The syllabus, usually printed at the front of the book, should list the grammatical structures and vocabulary sets in the order they appear. The teacher should check that this sequence is logical, moving from higher-frequency and structurally simpler items toward more complex ones, and that the syllabus is explicit enough to allow lesson planning without guesswork. A book that lists only broad topics such as "travel" or "food" without specifying the target language gives the teacher very little to work with.

Layout and usability, the fifth criterion, is easy to underestimate. Instructions should be short enough to read aloud without losing the class, and activities should require minimal preparation to set up. If a teacher has to read an instruction three times to understand what learners are supposed to do, learners will face the same difficulty independently.

No book satisfies all five criteria equally. Take a teacher choosing between two coursebooks for an adult beginner class. Book A has a logical grammar syllabus and clean layout, but its topics center on university life, which few students in the group have experienced. Book B uses workplace and neighborhood themes that suit the group well, but its skills balance is weak, with writing reduced to copying model sentences. Neither book is the right product; both are workable starting points. The evaluation has done its job by identifying, before the course begins, the precise points where each book will need supplementing.

## Using a Coursebook Flexibly: The Teacher as Editor

A teacher who uses a coursebook flexibly makes deliberate, reasoned choices about what activities to do, in what order, how much time to spend on each section, and what deserves the most emphasis in a given lesson. Principled use follows the book's overall structure and syllabus logic; the decisions are grounded in what the learners need and what the lesson is trying to achieve. Skipping pages without that grounding is a different matter entirely.

One of the most practical flexible-use strategies is reordering activities within a single lesson to match the natural energy arc of a class. Most groups arrive with some readiness to speak and interact, then settle into more focused reading or writing work as the lesson progresses. A coursebook may open a unit with a grammar explanation followed by a controlled exercise, then place a speaking task at the end. A teacher can reverse that sequence, using the speaking task as a warmer to surface what students already know before the explanation consolidates it. The grammar work then feels purposeful rather than abstract.

A second strategy applies when a double-page spread contains more material than a single lesson can absorb without rushing. Splitting the spread across two lessons allows each set of activities to receive proper attention. A teacher might cover the reading text and comprehension questions in one lesson, then return to the vocabulary and speaking extension in the next, treating them as a connected pair rather than a single overloaded block.

A third strategy involves combining two short units that share a grammar point. If one unit introduces countable and uncountable nouns and a later unit revisits quantity expressions, teaching them in sequence, or even within the same week, allows students to see the connection directly. Separating them by several weeks, as the book's chapter order might suggest, means the earlier learning has faded before the related material arrives.

All three strategies should be driven by learner needs and lesson aims. A teacher who reorders activities because a particular exercise looks dull, or who splits a lesson because time ran short the previous week, is making decisions based on personal preference or circumstance rather than on what the students require. Unprincipled changes accumulate. Over time, a course shaped by convenience rather than judgment loses its coherence.

Picture Ms. Reyes working through a pre-intermediate coursebook with a class of adult evening students. She notices that the next unit introduces the present perfect, but her students are still making consistent errors with simple past narration. In a recent lesson, a student, Carlos, produced the sentence "Yesterday I have seen my manager" when recounting a work event, and several classmates made similar errors in their written tasks. Ms. Reyes decides to delay the new unit by one week and use that time to consolidate simple past through additional practice. She is not abandoning the coursebook's syllabus; she is applying it with enough judgment to ensure the foundation is secure before the next layer is added.

## Adapting Coursebook Activities

A teacher who decides to adapt a coursebook activity rather than replace the whole lesson is working at a finer level of control. Adaptation means adjusting what the book provides so that it fits the actual students in the room, and four techniques cover most situations a teacher will encounter: adding, removing, modifying, and replacing.

Adding means extending an existing activity with further practice that serves the same aim. A coursebook might offer six gap-fill sentences practicing the simple past, which a stronger class completes in three minutes with little cognitive effort. The teacher adds a follow-up stage in which students write two original sentences about their own past week and read them aloud to a partner. The aim, consolidating simple past form, remains unchanged; the added stage gives students more time with the target language and shifts the practice from controlled to freer production.

Removing means cutting a task because it is too easy, too difficult, or culturally inappropriate for the group. A unit on celebrations might include a reading text followed by eight comprehension questions, the last three of which ask students to compare the described festival with their own national traditions. If the class shares the same cultural background and the comparison yields nothing new, those three questions consume time without generating useful language. The teacher removes them and moves directly to the speaking task that follows.

Modifying means changing the format, grouping, or focus of an activity while keeping its original aim intact. Imagine a written gap-fill in which students choose between the present simple and present continuous. The teacher converts this into a spoken information-gap activity: Student A receives a card describing what a character does every day, and Student B receives a card describing what the same character is doing right now. They ask each other questions to complete a shared grid.

T: "What does Daniel do every morning?"
S: "He catches the seven o'clock train. What is he doing right now?"
T: "He is waiting on the platform."

The grammatical aim, distinguishing habitual from in-progress actions, is identical to the original exercise; only the mode of practice has changed.

Replacing means discarding a coursebook exercise entirely and substituting a teacher-made equivalent that meets the same lesson aim. A reading text about Bonfire Night may be linguistically appropriate but culturally remote for a class in Bogotá. The teacher writes a parallel text of similar length and difficulty about a locally familiar public event, using the same text type and the same comprehension task format the book would have used. The lesson aim, reading for specific information, is preserved; the content becomes one the students can engage with from their own experience.

Across all four techniques, the guiding principle is that the lesson aim must survive the adaptation intact. A teacher who modifies a listening task so heavily that it no longer practices the target structure has not adapted the lesson; she has accidentally replaced it with something different. Before making any change, the teacher should identify the aim precisely, as a statement of what students will be able to do by the end of the activity, and then check that the adapted version still delivers it.

## Supplementing and Skipping: Knowing When the Book Is Not Enough

Supplementing and skipping are distinct operations, and conflating them leads to poor planning decisions. Supplementing means adding material that the coursebook does not provide; skipping means omitting material that is unnecessary or unsuitable for a particular class. Both are legitimate, but each requires a clear reason grounded in the needs of the students rather than the convenience of the teacher.

A teacher may supplement when the book treats a grammar point too briefly for the class to reach real control of it. If a unit introduces reported speech in a single presentation box and two short exercises, a student like Yuki, who consistently confuses tense backshift in her written work, will not have had enough practice to consolidate the form. The teacher can design additional controlled practice, such as a transformation exercise built from sentences relevant to the unit topic, without abandoning the book's sequence. Supplementing is equally appropriate when a skills gap exists at the level. A B1 coursebook that contains only short, scripted listening texts of under two minutes gives students no experience of sustaining attention across a longer spoken text, so the teacher may bring in a supplementary recording to fill that gap. A motivational reason also justifies supplementing: if a unit on urban life coincides with a major local planning story in the news, a short authentic article can connect the abstract theme to something students already care about.

Suppose a unit on giving directions. The coursebook provides a map task and a short scripted dialogue, but the dialogue is slow, heavily enunciated, and contains no natural hesitation or self-correction. The teacher records a brief audio clip of her own devising, in which two speakers navigate a real-sounding exchange:

T (as Speaker A): "So you go straight on past the library, and then, um, take the second left. No, wait, the first left."
T (as Speaker B): "The first left after the library?"
T (as Speaker A): "Yes. The café is right there on the corner."

Students listen for the correction the speaker makes and then practice a similar exchange in pairs, using the map already in the book. The supplement extends the lesson without displacing its aim.

Skipping is appropriate when a class has already demonstrated clear mastery of a topic. If the students completed a unit on comparatives two weeks ago and the new unit opens with a review section covering the same ground, spending a full lesson on it wastes time that could go toward new learning. A culturally sensitive subject is another sound reason to omit material; a reading text that treats a religious practice in a way likely to cause offense serves no pedagogical purpose that cannot be met by a different text. A task type that closely duplicates one completed in the previous lesson can also be skipped, since repetition of format without new challenge adds little.

The one reason that does not justify skipping is that a section looks difficult to teach. Difficulty in preparation is a signal to plan more carefully, to anticipate student confusion, to prepare additional examples, and to think through the staging of the activity. A teacher who skips a conditional structure because she finds conditionals hard to explain has transferred her own discomfort onto the students, who still need the language.

## Building a Healthy Long-Term Relationship with Your Coursebook

A coursebook works best when a teacher treats it as a working partner rather than a fixed authority. Partners are consulted, questioned, and occasionally overruled, but they are not ignored or resented. The relationship improves with time, provided the teacher invests in it deliberately.

Annotation is the most practical way to build that investment. After each lesson, a teacher who writes two or three brief notes in the margin of the relevant pages is creating a personalized record that no published teacher's guide can replicate. A note as simple as "Wei finished in four minutes; needs extension task" or "Priya confused by the diagram on p. 47; draw timeline on board instead" becomes immediately useful when the same material appears in a revision cycle or when a colleague inherits the book. Over a full teaching year, these marginal notes accumulate into a detailed picture of how the book performs with a specific group, and adaptation decisions in subsequent years become faster and more confident because they rest on direct classroom experience rather than fresh guesswork.

Pre-reading the next two or three units before teaching the current one is an equally valuable habit. A teacher who knows that Unit 9 contains a controlled writing task on formal letters will make a different decision about whether to spend extra time on Unit 7's vocabulary of register. Without that forward view, she might cut the vocabulary work to save time, then find in Unit 9 that her students lack the precise words the writing task requires. Reading ahead converts isolated lesson decisions into coherent course planning, and it ensures that choices about reordering or skipping are made with full awareness of the consequences downstream.

When a coursebook is used in this way, it performs a significant structural service. The sequencing of grammar, the selection of topics, the balance of skills across a term: all of this has been planned in advance, and the teacher does not need to reconstruct it from scratch each week. That released time and attention can go toward the classroom itself, toward noticing that Carlos has misunderstood a concept, that a pair activity has generated unexpectedly rich language worth pursuing, or that the class energy calls for a change of pace. The coursebook handles the architecture; the teacher handles the people inside it.
