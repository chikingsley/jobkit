# Integrating the Four Skills

## Why Real Communication Is Never Just One Skill

Consider a student named Fatima, sitting in a traditional language class where Monday is "listening day" and Tuesday is "writing day." The schedule feels orderly, but it bears little resemblance to how Fatima will use English outside the classroom door. In real communicative situations, the skills do not take turns. A conversation requires Fatima to listen and speak almost simultaneously, and a letter she writes will draw on reading she has done beforehand. The separation of skills into discrete, timetabled slots is a classroom convenience, not a reflection of how language works.

The long-term goal for learners like Fatima is communicative competence, defined here as the ability to communicate in a target language for meaningful purposes. That ability depends on using language skills together in social interaction, not on performing each skill in isolation. A student who can read accurately but cannot respond to a spoken question, or who can write sentences but cannot follow a conversation, has not yet reached communicative competence in any practical sense.

Understanding why integration matters requires a clear account of what the skills are. The four primary language skills divide into two pairs. Listening and reading are the receptive skills, meaning the learner takes in language produced by others; speaking and writing are the productive skills, meaning the learner generates language for others. Beneath these primary skills sit the sub-skills: grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation, and spelling, all of which are necessary for proficient use of the primary skills. Effective communication also calls on non-verbal skills such as gestures, facial expressions, and an awareness of cultural space, which carry meaning that words alone do not always convey.

A brief classroom exchange illustrates how quickly the skills converge. A teacher asks a student to describe a photograph.

T: "Tell me what you see in this picture, Carlos."
S: "There is a man. He is, um, carrying something heavy."
T: "Good. What do you think he feels?"
S: "He looks tired. Maybe angry."

In those few seconds, Carlos listened to the teacher's question, read the image for visual information, spoke his response, and drew on vocabulary and pronunciation as sub-skills. No single skill operated alone.

The source's rationale for building lessons around integrated skills rests on two related claims. Using language skills together can lead to better language retention, because learners encounter and process language through multiple channels rather than one. A teacher who integrates skills can also make lessons more interesting and motivating, creating a more active learning environment. These are not incidental benefits; they are the stated reasons why approaches from Total Physical Response to task-based and content-based learning all depend on skill integration to function as intended.

## A Closer Look at the Integrated Lesson: The Free-Write Activity

The free-write activity from Video Segment 1 shows what skill integration looks like in a concrete, manageable classroom task. A student selects a picture from a magazine, cuts it out, and pastes it onto a sheet of paper. The student then writes about the picture on the same sheet, choosing either a factual description or a creative story. That single decision point matters: the student owns the topic because the picture came from personal choice rather than teacher assignment.

The two students in the video made very different choices. One wrote a factual paragraph about bears, drawing on what he already knew about the subject. The other wrote a short piece of fiction about modern-day pirates, a sinking ship, and a crew in which, as the answer key records, "everyone died." Both students met the minimum requirement of half a page, but the teacher did not impose a fixed time limit, giving each student the room to finish at his own pace. Writing, in this activity, is the spine of the lesson; everything else grows from the text each student produces.

Once the writing is complete, the student reads it aloud to the teacher and the other student. The activity shifts at that point from a productive writing task to a speaking task, and the listener's role becomes active rather than passive. The non-reading student completes a worksheet about the content of the reading. The source calls this technique "active listening," a practice in which the listener has a structured purpose: recording what is heard rather than waiting without direction. Other active-listening options the source describes include keeping a list of unfamiliar vocabulary or writing one or two questions to ask the reader after the reading is finished.

T: "Tell me about your picture."
S: "It's a bear. I wrote about what bears eat and where they live."
T: "Good. Read it to us, please."

The teacher's role during the activity extends beyond managing the task. She gives directions, offers praise, and monitors progress while both students work. Her most telling move comes at the end. She recalls that the first student had written about bears in a previous lesson and asks whether he has a particular interest in the subject. That brief exchange, a few seconds of personalised conversation, connects the current writing to the student's broader identity as a learner, and it demonstrates that speaking and listening continue to operate around the edges of a writing-centered task, not only during the formal read-aloud phase.

The sequence the activity follows repays close attention. A student reads a magazine image, makes a choice, writes a text, reads that text aloud, and is heard by a peer who listens with a specific task in hand. Reading, writing, speaking, and listening each appear, and each serves a genuine communicative function rather than existing as an isolated drill. The written page does not disappear once the reading is done; the source notes that students place their writing in a notebook for the teacher to review later, and the text could also become the basis for a longer homework assignment or a class discussion. One piece of student writing, produced in a single session, carries the potential to generate further language work across multiple skills and across multiple lessons.

## Theme-Based Sequencing: How One Activity Feeds the Next

Content-based instruction, as defined in this unit, means structuring curriculum or lessons around central themes or topics. The theme does more than provide subject matter; it supplies the connective tissue that binds a sequence of activities together, with each stage drawing on what came before and preparing the ground for what follows. Without a unifying theme, a series of activities remains a collection of separate tasks. With one, the activities form a progression.

The "Good Deeds" iEARN project illustrates this principle across an extended sequence. Students began by visiting the library, dividing into two groups in jigsaw style, and reading different sources about good deeds. The reading was not an end in itself; it produced information that students then carried back to the group. Because each group had read different material, the subsequent discussion required genuine listening, since no single student held the complete picture. The theme of good deeds gave every student a reason to attend to what the others reported.

That discussion fed directly into the next stage. Students wrote summaries of the information they had gathered, including citations, working individually to consolidate what the group had assembled through talk. The writing task was shaped by the reading and speaking that preceded it; a student like Priya, writing her summary, was drawing on both her own library sources and the points her classmates had raised in discussion. The written summary became a tangible record of the group's collective understanding.

The summaries did not remain private documents. Students combined them, entered the text into a computer, printed it out, and brought it to class to discuss. The writing became the input for a new round of speaking and listening, this time focused on synthesis rather than initial information-gathering. A student named Daniel, reading a classmate's summary aloud to the group, was using a piece of writing as a script for spoken communication, and the group's response to it constituted active listening with a clear purpose.

The sequence extended further outward from the classroom. Students developed a web page representing their class activities and sent emails to partner classes in another country describing and comparing what they had done. The writing produced at earlier stages, the summaries, the reflections, the records of good deeds performed in the school and community, supplied the content for that authentic communication. The source describes this as purposeful use of language, first within the class and then again to create a web page and communicate electronically with others. Each output became the next input, and the theme of good deeds remained the thread connecting a library visit to an international correspondence.

The "Teddy Bears" project follows the same logic at a different scale. Students took a bear named Uncle Sam to locations around Thailand, photographed the visits, recorded events in a journal, and then used those photographs and written accounts to show Thailand to partner students in the United States. The journal writing fed the email correspondence; the photographs fed the written captions and descriptions; the descriptions fed the spoken planning sessions in which students decided where to take the bear next. A single theme, the bear's travels, organized reading, writing, speaking, and listening into a sequence where each activity produced something the next activity needed.

## Keeping One Skill as the Lesson's Spine

A lesson can integrate several skills and still maintain a clear sense of purpose, provided the teacher identifies one skill as the primary object of instruction and assessment at each stage. The source material describes this balance directly: although there is a time to focus on just one skill, especially at beginning levels, the final goal is to use skills together for communication. Supporting skills should serve the central one rather than compete with it for the learner's attention.

The free-write activity from Video Segment 1 illustrates this arrangement. Writing is the spine of the lesson. A student named, for example, Mateus selects a magazine picture of a bear, pastes it onto paper, and composes either a description or a short story, and the teacher assesses that written product. Speaking enters the lesson when Mateus reads his paragraph aloud, and listening enters when his partner completes a worksheet about what she heard. Neither of those activities displaces writing as the assessed skill; they extend it into a communicative context and give it an audience, while the teacher's spoken directions and praise also serve the writing task rather than redirect the lesson toward a different goal.

A simple three-column planning table, adapted from the activity-skills-resources structure in the source material, makes this hierarchy visible before the lesson begins. The columns record the activity or task, the skills used at that stage, and the resources required. A teacher working through the free-write sequence might note that the magazine-browsing stage draws on reading and speaking as students discuss picture choices, while the drafting stage places writing alone in the primary column. The read-aloud stage returns speaking to the primary column briefly, but the listener's worksheet keeps writing present as a sub-task. Mapping the stages this way shows the teacher at a glance whether any stage has drifted away from the lesson's central purpose.

The Teddy Bears project from Video Segment 2 operates on the same principle across a longer timescale. The travel journal is the spine skill. Students used oral planning sessions to decide where to take Uncle Sam next, and they read the written responses arriving from their partner class in the United States, with those spoken and receptive activities feeding material into the journal entries rather than replacing journal writing as the project's organizing output. When a teacher named, for instance, Ajarn Noi asked her students to plan the bear's next visit, the exchange might have sounded like this:

T: Where should Uncle Sam go this week, and what will we write about?
S: The temple near the school. We can describe the decorations, maybe the colors and the roof.
T: Good. What details will your American partners want to know?

The spoken negotiation produced content; the journal entry recorded and shaped it for an international audience. Reading the partner class's responses then gave students new information to incorporate into subsequent entries. At no stage did oral planning or reading become the assessed product; they remained in service of the writing.

Identifying the spine skill before a lesson begins also helps a teacher make decisions when time runs short. If Mateus needs another five minutes to finish his paragraph, the teacher can shorten the read-aloud stage without abandoning the lesson's purpose, because writing was always the primary skill. A teacher who has not mapped the hierarchy in advance may cut the wrong stage entirely, leaving students with spoken practice but no completed written product to show for the lesson.

## Adapting Integrated Activities for Different Classroom Contexts

The free-write activity described in the previous section works with two students, but the same structure scales to a full class. The source suggests placing magazines in several locations around the room so that students can move to them without crowding, choose a picture, and return to their seats to write. Once students have finished writing, they can work in pairs to rehearse reading their piece aloud before presenting it to a small group or to the whole class. A student like Yuki, who is hesitant to read in front of thirty peers, benefits from that paired rehearsal stage; by the time she reads to her group, she has already heard herself say the words once and corrected her own pronunciation.

For classes where individual writing and reading aloud feel too exposed, the chain story offers a different structure. Groups of three or four students co-author a single story by passing it around in a circle, each person writing one part. In a group of four, the sequence might run: introduction, first middle section, second middle section, and ending. Every student writes each type of section at least once as the paper circulates, so no one is responsible only for the opening or only for the conclusion. When the stories are finished, posting them on walls or doors gives the whole class a reason to read work produced by other groups, adding a reading stage that requires no additional preparation from the teacher.

T: "Fatima, your group's story has three different handwriting styles. Can you tell me which part you wrote?"
S: "I wrote the ending, but then I also wrote a beginning for Wei's group's story."
T: "Good. So you wrote two different kinds of section today."

The source also makes clear that class-to-class exchange projects do not depend on computers. Cultural exchange through mail has a long record of success, and a teacher without reliable internet access can contact local language schools or organizations to find a partner class. The letters, drawings, and written reports that students prepare for a mail-based exchange draw on the same combination of reading, writing, speaking, and listening that an email project requires. A student in a school with one shared computer is not excluded from the kind of purposeful, audience-directed writing that makes the iEARN projects described earlier so engaging.

The source offers a set of options rather than a single prescribed format. A teacher with a large class and limited resources can use the chain story and wall display. A teacher with internet access can pursue an international project; one situated between those two circumstances can begin with mail exchange and add digital components later. Across all three arrangements, students use reading, writing, speaking, and listening together in the service of a real communicative purpose. The specific resources available shape the form that purpose takes, rather than determining whether the purpose is attempted at all.

## Planning Your Own Integrated Lesson Segment

Before designing anything new, take stock of what you already do. Look at your current lessons and ask which tasks already require students to use more than one skill at once. A student named Fatima who reads a short text and then discusses it with a partner is already working with integrated skills, even if the lesson was not planned with that label in mind. Consider also where your lessons feel flat or where students seem disengaged, because those are often the places where skills have been separated in ways that remove the communicative purpose from the activity. The source's action plan gives you a concrete sequence for moving from that reflection to a tested classroom activity.

Step 1 asks you to think about your own classes in relation to five specific goals drawn from the module: integrating skills for better language retention, motivating students, allowing for plenty of practice, providing opportunities for authentic communication, and creating an active, interesting classroom. Write a few notes against each of those goals. A teacher named Carlos, working with teenage learners, might note that his students are motivated by topics they choose themselves, that practice time is limited to controlled exercises, and that authentic communication rarely happens inside the classroom. Those notes become the brief for the design work in Step 2.

Step 2 is the design stage. Working alone or with a colleague, plan a 15-minute segment in which one skill carries the main weight of the activity while the others support it. A useful planning tool here is the three-column table modeled in the module: one column for the activity or task, one for the language skills used, and one for the resources needed. Carlos might plan a segment in which students read a short magazine photograph caption, write three sentences responding to it, and then read those sentences aloud to a partner who listens and asks one question. Reading is the spine; writing, speaking, and listening grow from it. The table makes visible which skills appear at each step and what materials are required, so nothing is left to chance.

Step 3 moves the plan into conversation with others. Share the segment design with a peer or trainer, and explain what activity would come immediately before your 15-minute segment and what would follow it. A segment does not exist in isolation; the teacher who hears Carlos describe his plan might point out that students need a brief brainstorming phase before they write, or that the partner question at the end could feed into a short whole-class discussion. That feedback is the point of this step, and it often surfaces sequencing problems that are invisible when you are working alone.

Step 4 is revision and trial. Adjust the design in response to the feedback, then teach the segment and observe what happens. If you are not currently in a classroom, ask an experienced teacher to review the revised plan and respond to it as if they had taught it. After the trial, share what you found: what worked, what the students did that surprised you, and what you would change next time.

Throughout all four steps, keep returning to the question of theme. The iEARN classes in the module succeeded because every activity, from library research to e-mail exchange, served a single shared purpose. A 15-minute segment works by the same logic on a smaller scale. When reading, writing, speaking, and listening all point toward one central idea, whether that is a photograph, a good deed, or a traveling bear, the integration is purposeful. When skills are combined without that shared thread, the lesson can feel busy without being coherent. The theme is what holds the skills together and gives students a reason to use all of them.
