# Giving Feedback and Correcting Errors

## What feedback is and why it matters

Feedback, as used in language teaching, is information a learner receives about their performance, whether that information concerns the accuracy of their language, the success of their communication, or their confidence as a speaker or writer. A teacher who tells a student that a sentence is grammatically correct is giving feedback. So is a teacher who nods, repeats a student's utterance with a slight change in intonation, or writes a grade at the top of a composition. The form varies widely. The function is consistent: the learner learns something about how they are doing.

That information can land in very different ways. Fatima, struggling for weeks with the present perfect, hears her teacher confirm that her sentence is correct; she smiles, sits up straighter, and tries the structure again unprompted. Teachers call this the 'Aha' moment, the point at which feedback tips from mere information into genuine understanding and renewed effort. A different student, Daniel, is interrupted mid-sentence in front of the class and told his pronunciation is wrong; he falls silent for the rest of the lesson. Both corrections may have been accurate. The outcomes were opposite.

Teachers who reflect on their own experiences of giving and receiving feedback tend to find that those experiences sort naturally into three categories: ones that had a clearly positive effect, ones that had a clearly negative effect, and ones whose effect was ambiguous or hard to read, and of the three it is the ambiguous category that repays the closest attention. A student who says nothing after being corrected may have understood and accepted the feedback, or may have felt embarrassed and withdrawn. Without attending to that uncertainty, a teacher cannot know which outcome occurred.

This three-way pattern points to something course material makes explicit: incorrectness is not the only standard a teacher can use to assess or respond to a learner's production. Feedback operates across linguistic, communicative, and affective domains simultaneously. A response that addresses a grammar point while ignoring the affective dimension may correct the form and damage the learner's willingness to speak again.

Planning feedback deliberately, rather than correcting on reflex, follows directly from this reality. A teacher who corrects every error as it arises is making an implicit claim that accuracy always takes priority over fluency, confidence, and communicative momentum. That claim is not always warranted. Research supports the value of oral corrective feedback for second language development (Lyster and Saito, 2010; Li, 2010), but the decision about when and how to give that feedback still requires the teacher to read the task, the student, and the moment. Deliberate planning means deciding in advance which errors matter for a given activity, how corrections will be delivered, and what the likely effect on the learner will be, before the lesson begins rather than in the middle of it.

## Types of feedback

Feedback in a language classroom takes several distinct forms, and recognizing each one helps a teacher make deliberate choices rather than defaulting to a single habit. These forms sort along several dimensions: the channel through which feedback travels, its timing, its source, and the aspect of language it addresses (U.S. Department of State, Shaping the Way We Teach English, Module 5). Each dimension presents a genuine choice with real consequences for learning.

Oral feedback and written feedback serve different purposes and suit different moments. When Carlos reads a dialogue aloud and mispronounces a vowel, the teacher can respond immediately in speech, keeping the lesson moving. When Carlos submits a paragraph the following day, the teacher can annotate it in writing, giving him something to study at his own pace. The channel should match the activity: oral production calls for oral response, and written work generally benefits from written commentary that the learner can return to.

The timing of feedback, whether immediate or delayed, is a separate decision from the channel. On-the-spot feedback interrupts the learner at the moment of error; delayed feedback waits until the task is complete. If Priya is retelling a story to the class and stumbles over a past-tense ending, correcting her mid-sentence may break her concentration and discourage further attempts. Delaying the correction until after the activity allows Priya to finish the communicative task and still receive the linguistic information she needs. One workable routine is to take notes during a session and address patterned errors once the activity is done, which preserves both fluency and accuracy as goals.

Feedback also differs by its source. Teacher feedback is the most familiar kind, but peer feedback and learner self-assessment are equally legitimate. In one classroom arrangement described in Module 5, student groups created activities, distributed them to other groups, and then collected and checked the completed work themselves. That checking process is peer feedback: students examine each other's output and identify what is correct. Yuki, reading a classmate's written request, must decide whether the phrasing is appropriate, which requires her to apply her own knowledge actively. Self-assessment asks the learner to perform a similar operation on her own work, using a checklist or a set of guidelines provided by the teacher.

The final distinction concerns the target of the feedback. Feedback on form addresses grammar, vocabulary, or pronunciation; feedback on content addresses the ideas, the meaning, or the success of the communication. These two targets can pull in opposite directions during a single activity. If Wei is giving an oral presentation on a topic he researched, interrupting him to correct a preposition error shifts his attention from meaning to mechanics at precisely the wrong moment. The teacher might instead note the preposition error privately and address it later, while responding to the content of Wei's argument during the presentation itself. Course material is explicit on this point: teachers should decide whether an error prevents understanding or whether fluency is more important for the task at hand, and they should let that judgment determine which dimension of feedback takes priority.

## When to correct, and what

Selective correction begins with a clear distinction between two types of classroom activity. During fluency work, such as a pair discussion or a student narrating a personal story, the activity exists to produce sustained speech; constant interruption defeats that purpose. During accuracy work, such as a drill on the past tense or a controlled substitution exercise, immediate correction is the point of the exercise, and withholding it would leave the student practicing the wrong form. The teacher's first decision, then, is to identify which kind of activity is underway before deciding whether to intervene at all.

Not every error that surfaces during a lesson deserves a response. Three categories are worth treating: errors that block communication, errors in the specific language point the lesson is teaching, and errors that several students are making simultaneously. Consider a speaking activity in which Fatima says, "Yesterday I go to the market and I buyed vegetables." If the lesson target is simple past tense, both errors are directly relevant. If the lesson is a fluency discussion about weekend routines, the teacher might note the forms without comment and return to them after the activity closes.

Over-correction carries an affective cost. When a teacher corrects too frequently, students who were willing to attempt new language become reluctant to speak at all. Carlos, who was volunteering answers confidently in the first week, may fall silent by the third if each attempt is met with immediate correction. The guidance is explicit: teachers should avoid techniques that embarrass learners or make them feel incapable, and building confidence is a legitimate instructional goal alongside accuracy.

The decision about which errors to treat also depends on whether the error is a mistake or a true error. A mistake, in the technical sense used in applied linguistics, results from inattention rather than incomplete learning; the student knows the correct form but did not produce it carefully. A true error reflects a gap in the learner's developing system. Correcting a mistake the student can already self-repair is less productive than addressing an error that reveals a genuine misunderstanding of the target structure.

The effort a teacher puts into selecting and delivering corrective feedback is well spent. Lyster and Saito (2010) found, across fifteen classroom studies, that the effects of oral corrective feedback on second language development are significant and durable over time, meaning that careful correction produces gains that persist rather than fade. That finding gives teachers a principled reason to correct selectively and well, rather than either correcting everything indiscriminately or avoiding correction out of concern for the classroom atmosphere.

## How to correct: what the evidence says

Two broad families of oral corrective feedback appear consistently in the research literature, and understanding the difference between them shapes every practical decision a teacher makes at the moment of error. A recast occurs when the teacher repeats the student's utterance with the error repaired, without explicitly signalling that a correction has taken place. A prompt, by contrast, signals to the student that something is wrong and pushes the student to produce the correct form independently; prompts include clarification requests, repetition of the error with rising intonation, metalinguistic cues such as a quiet 'past tense?', and elicitation moves that supply the beginning of the correct form and pause for the student to complete it.

A recast in practice might look like this: Fatima is describing her weekend and says, 'Yesterday I go to the market,' and the teacher responds, 'Oh, you went to the market, that sounds nice,' and the lesson continues without a pause. Fatima hears the correct form embedded in the teacher's reply, but she is never asked to produce it herself. The flow survives. The error may too.

A prompt in the same situation would unfold differently. The teacher hears Fatima's sentence and responds with a clarification request: 'Sorry, you go to the market?' with rising intonation, or offers a metalinguistic cue: 'Yesterday, so which tense do we need?' Fatima pauses, retrieves the correct form, and says, 'I went to the market.' The teacher confirms: 'Yes, you went.' The exchange takes a few seconds longer, but Fatima has done the grammatical work herself, and that difference matters.

The evidence on which technique produces better learning outcomes is now reasonably clear. Lyster and Saito (2010) conducted a meta-analysis of fifteen classroom studies involving 827 learners and found that prompts produce larger effects on second language development than recasts. That finding sits alongside the result reported in the previous section, that oral corrective feedback works and its effects last; the additional point here is that the type of feedback matters, not only its presence or absence. Li (2010) corroborates the general effectiveness of corrective feedback across a separate meta-analysis of thirty-three studies, reinforcing the conclusion that teachers who correct thoughtfully are supporting acquisition rather than merely tidying up surface performance.

The standard interpretation of why prompts outperform recasts on average is that requiring the student to retrieve and produce the correct form engages deeper cognitive processing than hearing the correct form supplied by the teacher. This interpretation is offered cautiously in the literature because the precise mechanisms of second language acquisition remain a matter of ongoing inquiry, but it gives teachers a practical working principle: when the goal is durable learning rather than momentary fluency, a technique that makes the student do the mental work is generally preferable to one that does the work for the student.

Neither technique is universally superior in every classroom moment, and a teacher's choice should reflect the task at hand. During a fluency activity where communication is the primary goal, a quiet recast allows the lesson to continue without breaking the student's concentration on meaning. During a form-focused exercise, or when a student has produced an error that reflects a pattern the class has been studying, a prompt is the more productive choice. A teacher working with a class on reported speech might use recasts during a free discussion and switch to elicitation prompts the moment a student produces a form that the class practiced that morning, because the context signals that accuracy is now within reach. Context decides.

## Techniques in practice

One practical technique is the delayed correction slot, in which the teacher notes errors silently during a fluency activity and addresses them on the board only after the activity ends. Because the errors are written without attribution, no individual student is identified, and the class can treat the correction as a shared problem to solve. A teacher running a role-play might jot down three recurring verb-tense errors, then write them anonymously on the board and ask the group to identify what went wrong before offering any explanation.

Peer feedback with a simple rubric gives students a structured role in assessing each other's work rather than waiting passively for the teacher's judgment. The rubric should be narrow enough to be manageable; for a short paragraph task, it might ask only whether the main idea is clear and whether the verb forms match the tense the lesson introduced. When Yuki reads her partner's paragraph and marks one box 'needs work,' she is doing the same diagnostic thinking the teacher would do, and the writer receives feedback from a peer whose perspective often feels less threatening than the teacher's.

Self-correction time is a brief pause, built deliberately into the lesson, during which the teacher withholds a correction and signals the student to try again. The signal can be a gesture, a raised eyebrow, or a neutral repetition of the student's sentence with a questioning intonation. If Carlos says 'He go to school yesterday,' the teacher might repeat 'He go to school yesterday?' and wait, giving Carlos the chance to hear the error himself before any correction is supplied; Lyster and Saito (2010) found that these prompts, which push the learner toward self-repair, produce larger effects on second language development than recasts in which the teacher supplies the correct form directly.

The three-pile sort, adapted from the module's workshop sorting activity, trains learners to categorize their own production as 'went well,' 'needs work,' or 'not sure.' At the end of a writing task, a student reviews her sentences and physically or mentally places each one in a category before the teacher collects the work. This habit builds the diagnostic awareness that Module 5 describes as understanding the nature of an error, and it gives the teacher immediate information about where a student's uncertainty lies rather than where the teacher assumes it lies.

Praise is most useful when it names the specific thing the student did correctly rather than offering a general approval word. Telling a student 'Good' after a spoken answer tells her nothing she can repeat deliberately; telling her 'You used the past perfect there and it was correct' gives her a piece of information she can act on. When Priya produces a well-formed conditional sentence during a discussion, the teacher might say 'That conditional clause was accurate and it made your meaning very clear,' connecting the linguistic form directly to its communicative effect and reinforcing both at once.
