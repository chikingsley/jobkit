# The Learning-Styles Myth (and What to Do Instead)

## Why the Idea Feels So True

The appeal of learning-styles theory begins with something real. Every experienced teacher has watched one student light up when a diagram appears on the board while another seems to absorb the same content only after talking it through with a partner. The framework gives those observations a name and a structure, and that alone feels like progress.

The source glossary defines learning styles as "the various ways people seem to learn best," noting that most people use a mixture but may favor one or two. Seven types are listed: visual, aural, kinesthetic, verbal, logical, social, and solitary. The categories are memorable, the labels are intuitive, and together they produce a tidy map of the classroom. A teacher can look at a student like Daniel, who always reaches for colored pens and draws concept maps in the margins of his notes, and feel that the label "visual learner" captures something genuine about him.

Professional materials aimed at practicing teachers have reinforced this sense of legitimacy. The Unit 6 video guide in the underlying training course lists "teaching students with different learning styles" as one of its named focus areas, placing it alongside classroom management and integrated skills as a standard concern. The same materials use "kinesthetic learner" and "visual learner" as ordinary descriptive shorthand in glossary entries, without qualification. When the terminology appears in respected training materials, teachers reasonably conclude that the underlying theory is settled.

The framework also carries a moral weight that makes it hard to set aside. Sorting students into types feels like paying attention to them as individuals, and that instinct reflects something genuinely valuable about good teaching. A teacher who asks whether her lesson plan "accommodates different learning styles" is, at minimum, asking whether she has considered the range of people in the room. The question itself is worth asking; the diagnostic-matching answer it implies is the part that the evidence does not support.

Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, and Bjork (2008) found no adequate evidence that identifying a learner's preferred style and then matching instruction to that style produces better learning outcomes. Willingham, Hughes, and Dobolyi (2015) reached the same conclusion, confirming that learning-styles theories lack scientific support as a prescription for instruction. The persistence of the idea is not a sign of carelessness among teachers. It is a sign that the theory latches onto real observations, real professional values, and real institutional language, and then offers a solution that feels proportionate to the problem it describes.

## What the Research Shows

The specific claim that Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, and Bjork examined is called the meshing hypothesis: the proposition that learning improves when the format of instruction is matched to a learner's diagnosed style. To test this hypothesis properly, a study must first assign learners to style categories, then randomly allocate them to matched and mismatched instructional conditions, and finally compare outcomes across those groups. Pashler et al. (2008) reviewed the available research and found that adequately designed studies of this kind either did not exist or failed to support the hypothesis when they did. The finding is not that individual differences are imaginary; it is that diagnosing a fixed style and then restricting instruction to fit that diagnosis does not produce the predicted benefit.

Willingham, Hughes, and Dobolyi (2015) reinforced this conclusion and went a step further in their implications for educators. They argued that learning-styles theories should be presented to teachers and trainee teachers as a popular but scientifically debunked idea, rather than as a basis for planning decisions. The distinction matters in a professional development context because treating the theory as merely unproven leaves open the possibility that better evidence might eventually vindicate it; treating it as a tested and failed hypothesis closes that door more honestly.

Consider what this means in practical terms for a teacher who has sorted her adult intermediate class into visual, auditory, and kinesthetic groups after administering an online style inventory. She now plans separate task streams for each group, believing she is responding to her learners' needs. The research does not suggest that her students are all the same or that their differences are unimportant. It suggests that the particular mechanism she is using, diagnosing a fixed category and then narrowing each student's instructional diet to match it, is the part that lacks support (Pashler et al., 2008; Willingham et al., 2015).

The precision of the research finding is worth holding onto. A teacher who hears "learning styles are a myth" and concludes "therefore individual differences don't matter" has misread the evidence in a direction that is just as unhelpful as the original error. What the studies establish is a much more targeted point: the sorting-and-matching procedure is the unsupported step. Learner differences are real; the proposed mechanism for addressing them through style diagnosis is not.

## The Difference Between Preference and Learning

The slippage begins in the language used to define the categories themselves. The source glossary for this course describes a visual learner as someone who "benefits from or prefers" learning through visual input, and a kinesthetic learner as someone who "benefits from or prefers" hands-on experiences. Those two verbs are doing very different work, and placing them side by side without comment treats them as equivalent. Preferring a format means a learner finds it more comfortable, more familiar, or more enjoyable. Benefiting from a format means that format produces measurably better retention or performance. A student like Fatima may prefer listening to explanations over reading them, yet retain more from a well-structured reading task than from a spoken one on the same material. Her preference is real; it does not determine her optimal learning channel.

The same glossary describes kinesthetic learning as "hands-on" activity incorporating "gestures, body movement, actions, facial expressions, manipulatives." Any experienced teacher recognizes that list as a description of good pedagogy for a mixed classroom, not a prescription for a particular subset of students. When Mr. Osei has his beginners mime the action of verbs while repeating them aloud, every student in the room is encoding the word through movement and sound at once. The activity does not serve only the students who self-identify as kinesthetic learners; it gives all learners an additional retrieval route. Reserving such techniques for a diagnosed group would mean withholding a useful tool from everyone else.

The conceptual gap, then, is between a learner's reported preference and the conditions that support learning. Students often prefer formats that feel easy or familiar, and ease of processing can be mistaken for depth of learning. Take Fatima, who reports that she learns vocabulary best from flashcards because the task feels manageable. A more demanding activity, such as using new words in a short spoken exchange with a partner, may feel harder even though it engages her more actively with the words. Comfort and effectiveness are related but separate, and the learning-styles framework tends to treat a reported preference as if it were a finding about what works.

Willingham, Hughes, and Dobolyi (2015) draw a blunter conclusion: scientific support for learning-styles theories is lacking, and educators' limited time and energy are better spent on practices the evidence does support. This course's guidance follows from that finding. The evidence-consistent response to genuine learner differences is variety of activity formats across a course or lesson sequence, not the diagnosis and matching of individual styles. That guidance reframes the practical question a teacher needs to ask. Sorting students by style is not the productive move; offering a consistent rotation of formats is. Rather than asking which style each student has, the teacher asks which range of formats the lesson provides and how reliably those formats alternate across the sequence. The next section examines what that rotation looks like in a planned lesson and why it serves the full range of learners more reliably than any sorting procedure could.

## Variety as the Evidence-Based Alternative

Offering a variety of activity formats is the approach this course recommends, and it is consistent with what the research shows. Willingham, Hughes, and Dobolyi (2015) are explicit that learning-styles instruction lacks scientific backing and that teachers' effort belongs elsewhere; planned format variety is a place to put that effort which depends on no unsupported diagnosis. A lesson that moves through multiple formats gives every learner more than one way into the material, and it does so without requiring the teacher to sort students by a "style" that the evidence does not support.

The source text's description of learning stations captures this principle in practical terms. Stations "feature complementary activities so that learners can experience the target content/concepts in a variety of ways" (Shaping the Way We Teach English, americanenglish.state.gov). Station designs vary: in some classrooms every student rotates through the full sequence, meeting the same target concept through different formats in turn, while in others students choose which station to work at, a design that builds investment and autonomy. What no sound design does is route students to stations by diagnosed style. No one is confined to a poster station because they have been labeled a visual learner, and no one is barred from a movement station because they have not been labeled kinesthetic.

The Unit 6 classroom in the same materials shows the same principle operating within a single lesson. The teacher uses movement, singing, color, and repetition alongside visual aids to teach grammar and vocabulary to seven- and eight-year-olds. Picture a child who is quiet during the singing segment but becomes visibly engaged when the teacher introduces a color-coded chart.

T: "Can you point to the verb in this sentence?"
S: "This one, 'jumps'?"
T: "Exactly. Why did the color help you find it?"
S: "Because it was different from the other words."

The child's response to the visual format is useful information. It does not mean she should receive only visual input from that point forward. The movement and song she experienced earlier contributed to her recognition of the word in context, even if she could not articulate that connection.

The Unit 9 activity sequence makes the cumulative effect of variety visible at the lesson level. A single class moves through whole-class brainstorming, individual desk work, pair interviews, small-group role-play preparation, and presentations to the full class. A student named Wei, who speaks rarely during whole-class discussion, produces fluent, confident language during the pair interview stage. A student named Priya, who struggles with the open-ended role-play, demonstrates precise recall during the structured individual task. Neither student is well served by a lesson that stays in one format; both are well served by a lesson that moves through several.

Variety, finally, asks nothing of the debunked theory. Willingham, Hughes, and Dobolyi (2015) conclude that learning-styles theories lack scientific support and that teachers' time is better spent elsewhere; a teacher who plans a lesson with whole-class, pair, small-group, and individual components, and who rotates among visual, physical, and oral formats, is spending that time on reaching every learner without sorting anyone into style categories the evidence does not support.

## Formats Worth Rotating: A Practical Inventory

The formats available to a classroom teacher are more varied than any single lesson can contain. Rotating among them is therefore a practical necessity. Visual aids, defined in the glossary as instructional materials that present information visually, include posters, charts, diagrams, graphic organizers, video, and slide presentations. A graphic organizer, sometimes called a concept map, allows a student like Fatima to record and connect vocabulary in a semantic web before a writing task, giving her a spatial representation of relationships that a spoken explanation alone would not provide.

Kinesthetic activities incorporate movement, gesture, and physical manipulation into language practice. Total Physical Response (TPR), an instructional approach that combines language learning with physical movement, is one structured example; a teacher might say "T: Show me 'exhausted'" and expect students to respond physically before any written form appears. These formats reach the content through the body rather than through the eye or ear alone, and they serve the whole class when placed at a point in the lesson where energy is flagging.

Integrated language skills activities combine two or more skills in a single task. When a student like Wei listens to a short audio clip, takes notes, and then discusses his notes with a partner, he is reading, listening, speaking, and writing within one sequence. The training materials treat this integration as a design principle rather than an occasional bonus, and the unit descriptions show it operating across beginner and intermediate levels alike.

Learning stations divide the classroom into defined spaces, each equipped with complementary tasks so that learners encounter the same target content through different formats. One station might feature a reading task, another a drawing or labeling activity, and a third a short conversation prompt. The complementary design means that no single format carries the full instructional load, and students who struggle with one entry point find another available.

Student-centered pair and group work shifts the locus of practice from the teacher to the learners. A role-play or drama activity asks students to produce language for a communicative purpose rather than to repeat a model; when a student like Priya performs a talk-show role in front of the class, she is synthesizing vocabulary, grammar, and social register simultaneously. Teacher-centered direct instruction still has a place, particularly for introducing new forms or giving clear procedural guidance, but it is one component among several rather than the default mode.

Formative feedback loops, whether oral exchanges during an activity or written comments returned before a unit ends, give learners information about how they are doing while there is still time to adjust. These loops can be brief: a short exchange such as "T: What did you notice about the word order here? S: The verb came before the subject?" functions as both feedback and further practice. The Unit 6 action-plan prompt asks teachers to include activities that accommodate different learning styles; read in light of Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, and Bjork (2008), that prompt is most usefully understood as an invitation to build format variety into the plan, so that the lesson offers multiple routes into the material rather than a single channel matched to a diagnosed type.

## Planning for Variety: Putting It Into Practice

When auditing a lesson plan for format variety, a teacher can work through a small set of planning questions drawn from the training materials, rereading each one with variety as the governing principle. The question "What kinds of activities might make the lesson more personalized?" asks how content can connect to students' actual lives, interests, and prior knowledge. A teacher planning a grammar lesson on reported speech might ask whether the practice sentences reflect situations students genuinely encounter, so that a student named Fatima, who commutes long distances, and a student named Carlos, who works part-time, both find something recognizable in the material.

The question "Can you include activities that accommodate different learning styles?" is most productively read as a prompt to survey the lesson's format range. A teacher who notices that every activity in a forty-minute plan involves silent reading and written gap-fills has identified a real problem; the problem is uniformity of format. Adding a brief paired discussion, a physical sorting task with word cards, or a short listening segment addresses that uniformity without requiring the teacher to assign any student to a fixed category.

The question "How might you encourage critical thinking and creativity through open-ended questions or activities?" points toward tasks that require students to do something generative with language rather than retrieve a memorized form. Imagine a teacher who asks "T: How would you explain this rule to someone who had never studied English?" and receives the response "S: I would say the verb has to agree with the subject, like a contract between them." The student is synthesizing rather than recalling, and that cognitive demand is available to every learner in the room regardless of any supposed style preference.

Two further practices support learner-responsive planning without requiring style diagnosis. Sharing a lesson plan with colleagues before teaching it, then revising based on their feedback, introduces an external check on whether the plan's activity formats are genuinely varied or only appear so from inside the teacher's own perspective. Asking students directly where they think learning stations should go and what kinds of station activities they would enjoy, as the materials recommend, gathers real preference data from the actual learners present; such data are more reliable than any general profile assigned in advance.

The core position of this unit can be stated plainly. Recognizing that learners differ in background, preference, pace, and strength is correct and professionally responsible. Treating those differences as fixed neurological categories that require matched instruction is an error, one for which Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, and Bjork (2008) found no adequate supporting evidence, a finding reinforced by Willingham, Hughes, and Dobolyi (2015). The instrument for honoring real learner differences is deliberate, planned variety of activity formats across a lesson and across a course, so that every student encounters the material through more than one route and no single format becomes the only door into the content.
