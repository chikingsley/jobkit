# Teaching with Limited Resources

## The Well-Stocked Low-Resource Classroom

A classroom with few purchased materials is a classroom that has not yet been fully inventoried. The board, the students, and whatever can be made or improvised by hand are present in almost every teaching context, and together they form a workable foundation for a wide range of language activities. Recognizing these assets clearly is the first step toward planning lessons that function well within real constraints.

The board alone, whether chalk or whiteboard, serves simultaneously as a drawing surface, a vocabulary display, a space for student writing, and a stage for teacher demonstration. Ms. Reyes, working in a school where photocopying is limited, can sketch a simple scene on the board and ask students to describe what they see, predict what will happen next, or write a short story about the image. The board costs nothing beyond what the school already provides.

Students themselves are a resource that is genuinely difficult to exhaust. Pair work, group discussion, peer editing, and student-led presentations all draw on the knowledge, language, and creativity that learners bring into the room. When Carlos explains a new word to his partner in his own phrasing, both students are doing productive language work without any printed material in sight.

Hand-made and improvised materials extend this foundation considerably. The assigned reading *Create to Communicate* notes that drawing can be done with very few materials, listing the minimum requirements plainly: a drawing tool such as a pencil, pen, or chalk, and a drawing surface such as paper or a chalkboard. Two items. A teacher holding those can run a drawing-based vocabulary activity, a story-writing sequence, or a peer assessment task without anything else at all.

Scarcity defines a starting point for creative planning rather than a ceiling on what is possible. The gap between what many teachers have and what they need to teach well is considerably smaller than it first appears. A teacher who begins by asking what is already in the room, rather than what is missing from it, will find more possibilities than expected.

## Drawing Activities: The Board and Beyond

Drawing activities divide into two modes. The first is drawing from observation, in which students look directly at a subject and reproduce what they see. Suitable subjects are everywhere in an ordinary classroom or its surroundings: food brought from home, a plant on a windowsill, a shoe placed on a desk, or the view through a window onto a local street. The second mode is drawing from imagination, in which students work from memory and invention rather than from a physical subject in front of them. Both modes are available to any teacher with a pencil and a surface.

For either mode, the module recommends the same practical starting point: identify the basic shapes that make up the subject before attempting to draw it in full. A teacher might say to Priya, "Before you draw the mango, what shapes can you see in it? An oval? A small curve at the top?" Once Priya has sketched those shapes as a loose outline, she adds the form, detail, and surface features over them. This approach makes drawing accessible to students who believe they cannot draw, because it replaces the intimidating idea of reproducing a whole object with the manageable task of placing a few familiar shapes on the page.

Imaginative drawing carries an additional reassurance that the module states plainly: there is no right or wrong way. When Daniel draws a scene from a story he has just heard, his version cannot be incorrect, because no external object exists against which to measure it. This removes a significant source of anxiety. Students who are reluctant to speak or write for fear of making errors will often engage freely with an imaginative drawing task, and that relaxed engagement can then carry over into the language work that follows.

The language goals the module connects to drawing are specific. Drawing reinforces vocabulary by giving words a visual anchor; a student who draws and labels a household object is more likely to retain the word than one who copies it from a list. Drawing also provides a context for practicing grammatical structures, for instance when students describe what they have drawn using present continuous forms or comparative adjectives. A teacher might prompt this directly:

T: Daniel, tell me about your drawing.
S: The man is running. He is faster than the dog.

Beyond vocabulary and grammar, drawing generates oral conversation, because students naturally want to explain, compare, and question each other's work.

When paper is unavailable, the chalkboard or whiteboard becomes the drawing surface. The module lists a drawing tool and a drawing surface as the only required materials, and it names the chalkboard and whiteboard explicitly among the surfaces that qualify. A teacher can draw on the board to model vocabulary, invite individual students to add to a shared class drawing, or divide the board into sections so that small groups each contribute a scene. Colored chalk, markers, and pencils appear in the module's list of optional materials, useful when available but not necessary. The activity functions with chalk alone on a board that is already in the room.

## Students as the Material: Pair and Group Work

When a classroom contains thirty students and little else, those thirty students are already the most productive resource available. Pair and group work formats turn the people in the room into the lesson itself, requiring no printed textbook, no audio equipment, and no internet connection. The module describes a Mad Lib activity completed in pairs or small groups as a vocabulary and grammar exercise, and the only material it requires is the brief set of instructions a teacher can write on the board or read aloud. Carlos asks his partner Fatima for a noun, Fatima supplies one without seeing the text, and the resulting sentence produces both laughter and a genuine discussion of why the grammar does or does not work.

Peer assessment adds a second layer to this approach by giving students a defined role as evaluators of each other's work. The module describes a Peer Assessment Worksheet used when students assess the performance of peer discussion leaders, and a separate Teacher Rubric for the same activity. Once a teacher has written the criteria on the board a single time, the class can return to the same format across many lessons without any additional preparation. Fatima reads a partner's argument aloud, marks the checklist, and explains which criterion the partner met and which still needs work; the teacher circulates and listens rather than standing at the front delivering content.

The self- and peer editing stations described in the Unit 12 supplementary resources show how a writing lesson can run entirely on student interaction. The module explains that students use a Self-Editing and Peer Editing Checklist to assess their own writing first, then pass the checklist to a classmate for a second round of feedback. No printer is strictly necessary; the teacher writes the checklist items on the board before the lesson begins, and students copy them into their notebooks as a permanent reference. Carlos reads through his paragraph against each criterion, corrects two punctuation errors he finds himself, and then exchanges papers with the student beside him.

The Writing Mechanics Checklist, also described in the Unit 12 resources, extends this routine to the sentence level. Working in pairs, students use the checklist to check their own writing and then provide feedback to each other. The module presents this as a paired activity, which means the teacher needs only one shared set of criteria between two students rather than individual printed copies for every person in the room. Once students have used the checklist two or three times, many of the criteria become habitual questions they ask themselves without being prompted.

These formats share a quality that makes them especially practical in low-budget settings: they are reusable routines. The criteria on the board change to match the current lesson, but the procedure stays the same, so students spend less time receiving instructions and more time producing and responding to language. A teacher who establishes peer editing in the first month of a course can return to it every week at no additional cost, and the students themselves carry the activity forward.

## Signal Words and Classroom Routines as Structure

A signal word is a designated word, sound, or gesture that a teacher uses consistently to mark the start or end of an activity or to move the class from one task to the next. In the primary classroom described in course material, one teacher used the word "magic" for this purpose. The word carries no grammatical meaning; its function is entirely procedural, and that is precisely what makes it effective. Students learn quickly that hearing "magic" means something is about to change, so they orient their attention without needing a written prompt, a projected slide, or a timer.

The advantage of this kind of cue becomes clearest in classrooms where printed instructions are scarce or where students have limited reading ability. Consider a teacher working with young learners who has no whiteboard, no handouts, and no way to display a task sequence. If she has spent two weeks using the same signal word to open pair work, a student like Fatima already knows what to do the moment she hears it. The cognitive work of understanding the transition has been done in advance, through repetition, and the lesson moves forward without interruption.

Routines carry a deeper value in such settings. A routine is a predictable sequence of actions that students internalize through repeated exposure, so that the sequence itself requires no fresh explanation each time it occurs. When materials are limited, a teacher cannot rely on visual aids or technology to orient the class at each stage of a lesson. Routines fill that gap. The teacher who always begins group work with the same signal, always closes it with the same gesture, and always moves to feedback in the same way gives students a reliable map of the lesson, drawn entirely from memory.

Body movement offers a parallel tool. Course material recommends including hand, arm, or body movements that correspond to words in songs, poems, or stories. A teacher might assign a specific arm gesture to each new vocabulary item in a poem, so that when she raises both arms, students know the word "tall" is coming, and when she crouches, the word "small" follows. A student like Wei, who struggles to track spoken language at speed, can use the physical cue as an anchor. The movement costs nothing to prepare and nothing to reproduce.

What these devices share is that they convert the absence of equipment into a structural advantage. A classroom with no projector and no printed worksheets can still have clear and smooth transitions if the teacher has built a repertoire of signals and movement cues over time. When a lesson moves from a listening task to pair discussion to a brief presentation without hesitation or confusion, the momentum itself becomes a teaching resource. Students stay in the language rather than waiting for the next instruction, and the teacher's attention remains on the learners rather than on managing materials that are not there.

## Improvised Materials: Puppets, Pocket Books, and Graphic Organizers

Puppets, pocket books, and graphic organizers represent a category of classroom material that teachers can produce from paper, pencils, and a few minutes of preparation. The supplementary resources accompanying *Shaping the Way We Teach English* describe step-by-step instructions for each of these, and the underlying principle is consistent across all of them: a teacher who knows how to make one set of materials can make another set for a different lesson, a different age group, or a different vocabulary set, using the same basic supplies.

Flip books are among the simplest items on this list. The module describes the materials and step-by-step instructions as brief enough to fit a single reference document, which means Ms. Reyes can read the instructions once and then guide students through making their own. The finished book gives each learner a personal, physical object that holds vocabulary or story sequences, and it costs nothing beyond paper and something to cut with.

The puppet options in the same supplementary set cover three distinct forms. Full-sized paper puppets are large enough for a teacher to hold up at the front of the room during a story or dialogue. Hand puppets fit over the fingers and work well for pair activities, as when two students named Daniel and Fatima take turns giving their puppets lines of scripted conversation. Stick puppets, which the module notes can be made from a provided line-drawing template of a boy and girl that students color and cut out, give even very young learners a character to speak through, which can reduce the self-consciousness that sometimes accompanies early oral production.

Pocket books extend the puppet principle into reading and writing. The two examples described in the module, "What Do I See?" and "The Book About Me," are student-made books in which learners fill in blanks to produce their own readable text. Yuki, who fills in "I see a ___" on each page of her pocket book is simultaneously practicing sentence structure, vocabulary, and the concept that written words carry meaning, all without a printed textbook in sight.

Graphic organizers work on a different principle: rather than being made once and kept, they are drawn on the board at the start of an activity and erased when the lesson moves on. The K-W-L Chart, which the module describes as a warm-up tool with a reflection follow-up, organizes what learners know, what they want to know, and what they have learned about a topic. The module states explicitly that teachers can reproduce it on the board, making paper entirely optional. A teacher draws three columns before class begins, and the chart is ready. The Brainstorming graphic organizer works the same way; the module notes it can be reproduced on the board for classroom use, so a single drawing serves the whole group.

The practical consequence of treating these organizers as board templates rather than printed handouts is that they become reusable across many lessons. A teacher who draws the K-W-L Chart before a unit on animals can draw the same chart before a unit on food, on weather, or on any topic the class encounters next month. The template is the teacher's knowledge of the structure, and that knowledge costs nothing to carry from one classroom to the next.

## Mini-Projects as a Routine End Product

Project-based learning does not require a computer lab, a projector, or a set of printed worksheets. The module describes mini-projects as a natural closing structure for a lesson, and the student outputs it lists are deliberately low-cost: role-plays performed in front of the class, short presentations, written stories, and hand-made posters. Each of these can be produced with paper, a pen, and the space at the front of the room. A teacher working in a school with no photocopier can assign any of them without modification.

The Project Checklist: Learning New Vocabulary, described in the module's supplementary materials, illustrates how structure replaces equipment. Small groups use the checklist to guide themselves through preparing a presentation built around new vocabulary items. The checklist includes a self-assessment rubric for students and a separate rubric for the teacher. Both rubrics can be written on the board once and copied by students into their notebooks, so the entire scaffolding apparatus exists on paper the students already have. Fatima, preparing a presentation on market vocabulary with her group, can work through each checklist item independently before the teacher circulates to check progress.

The module is explicit that a well-designed project should include options for both individual and collaborative work, and it should produce a defined end product or performance outcome. That pairing matters in a low-resource setting because it gives every student a clear role. In a class of thirty, a teacher named Mr. Osei might assign each group of five a poster on a different set of vocabulary words; within the group, one student writes the definitions, two draw and label illustrations, and two prepare the spoken presentation. The individual contributions are visible, the collaborative outcome is a single poster, and the whole activity requires only paper and markers.

When the end product is a student performance or a hand-made poster, the absence of technology becomes structurally irrelevant. A role-play between two students named Carlos and Yuki, performed for the class, generates listening practice, speaking practice, and peer feedback without any device. A poster displayed on the wall serves as a reading resource for the rest of the term. The module's advice to create a defined end product points toward something teachers in any context can use: when students know they are working toward something they will show to others, the work itself carries a purpose that sustains attention across the lesson.

Mini-projects function as a recurring structure precisely because the format stays constant while the content changes. A class that produces vocabulary posters in one unit can produce written stories in the next and role-plays in the unit after that. The teacher establishes the routine once, and students learn what a project week looks like. That familiarity reduces the time spent on instructions and increases the time available for language use, which is the point of the activity in the first place.
