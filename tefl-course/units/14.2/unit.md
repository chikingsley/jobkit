# Business English and One-to-One Teaching

## What Is Business English and Who Are Your Learners?

Business English is a specialization within English language teaching that focuses on the language professionals need in order to operate in workplace contexts. General English courses aim at broad language development across many situations; Business English is organized around specific communicative goals such as writing a clear proposal, chairing a meeting, or closing a deal by telephone. The distinction shapes every decision a teacher makes, from the materials selected to the way progress is measured.

The typical Business English learner is an adult who already holds professional responsibilities and arrives at lessons with limited time and clear reasons for studying. A learner like Marco, a mid-level logistics manager preparing for quarterly calls with English-speaking suppliers, is not studying out of general curiosity. He needs particular language for particular situations, and he needs it to work reliably under the pressure of a real professional encounter. This instrumental motivation, the desire to achieve a defined practical outcome, tends to make Business English learners focused and self-directed, though it also means they may disengage quickly from material that feels irrelevant to their work.

These learners frequently arrive with a general English level well above beginner. A student such as Yuki, a financial analyst who reads English reports daily, may already control complex grammar and a wide vocabulary, yet still struggle to participate confidently in a fast-moving conference call. Her difficulty lies in the specific conventions and formulaic language that professional communication relies on, not in any broader deficiency of grammar or range. Recognizing that distinction prevents a teacher from pitching lessons at the wrong level or spending time on structures the learner already commands.

Professional experience is one of the most valuable assets a Business English learner brings to the classroom, and a skilled teacher treats it as a resource. When a learner like Carlos, a senior engineer, explains how a project approval process works in his company, that knowledge becomes the content around which language practice is built. A short exchange such as T: "Walk me through what happens after you submit the report." S: "First my line manager reviews it, then it goes to the board." gives the teacher authentic material and gives the learner immediate, relevant practice. The teacher's subject knowledge matters far less than the ability to draw out and work with what the learner already knows.

Business English lessons commonly address a recognizable set of professional skills. Writing emails that are appropriately formal, participating constructively in meetings, delivering structured presentations, and managing telephone calls or negotiations each place distinct demands on a speaker. A course for one learner may concentrate heavily on written communication, while another learner's course may revolve almost entirely around spoken interaction in international meetings. The specific mix depends on the individual, which is why careful needs analysis, covered later in this unit, is the essential starting point for any Business English engagement.

## Conducting a Needs Analysis

A needs analysis is a systematic process of gathering information about a learner before any teaching begins, so that the course can be built around that person's actual professional life rather than a generic syllabus. Business English learners are rarely beginners in the broadest sense; a client like Marta, a Polish procurement manager, may write fluent internal emails yet struggle to hold her own in a fast-moving conference call with suppliers in Singapore. Another client, Kenji, a Japanese engineer preparing to relocate to a German subsidiary, may read technical documentation with ease but have almost no experience of small talk or informal workplace conversation in English. The gap between what each learner can already do and what they need to do is the central problem the needs analysis must map.

The analysis therefore works along two dimensions. The first is the present situation: the learner's current level, the English tasks they already perform at work, and how confidently they handle them. The second is the target situation: the specific tasks, contexts, and interlocutors they will face, and the standard of performance those situations demand. For Marta, the target situation might be chairing a thirty-minute negotiation call; for Kenji, it might be joining a team lunch and contributing to an informal project debrief. These two dimensions together give the teacher a clear picture of the distance the course must cover.

A pre-course questionnaire is a practical first tool for collecting this information before the opening lesson. A well-designed questionnaire asks the learner to describe their job role, list the English tasks they perform each week, rate their own confidence in speaking, writing, listening, and reading at work, and identify any situations they currently avoid because of language difficulty. The answers give the teacher a working hypothesis about priorities, which the first lesson can then test and refine.

An initial interview allows the teacher to probe further and to hear the learner's English in natural use. During this conversation, the teacher might ask Marta to describe a recent meeting in English, noting where she hesitates, reaches for vocabulary, or simplifies her meaning. A short sample task adds another layer of evidence: asking the learner to write a brief work email on the spot, for instance, reveals far more about register, grammar, and organisation than any self-assessment score.

The needs analysis must also surface practical constraints, because these shape the course as much as language goals do. The teacher should establish how many hours per week the learner can realistically commit, whether sessions will take place at the client's office or online, and whether any fixed deadline is approaching. If Kenji's relocation is in eight weeks, the teacher cannot spend the first month on grammar consolidation; the course must prioritise the spoken interaction skills he will need from day one in Germany. Identifying these constraints at the outset prevents the course from drifting toward comfortable but low-priority content.

## Planning and Delivering Business English Lessons

The findings of the needs analysis become the skeleton of every lesson plan. A learner like Marta, a project manager who needs to chair weekly status meetings with international colleagues, will spend her lesson time on the language of turn management: phrases for inviting contributions, redirecting speakers who have gone off-topic, and closing a discussion point cleanly. A learner like David, a compliance officer who writes regulatory reports for senior management, will instead work on formal register, logical cohesion between paragraphs, and hedging expressions that soften claims appropriately. The same grammar point, say the passive voice, might appear in both courses, but in Marta's lessons it serves spoken meeting summaries, while in David's it serves impersonal report writing.

Role-play is a reliable vehicle for spoken business skills. In a lesson with Marta, the teacher might set up a short video-call scenario in which she opens a call with a new client she has not met before.

T: "You've just joined the call. There are three people waiting. Go ahead."
S: "Good morning, everyone. Thank you for joining. I'm Marta Kowalska, project lead at Fenix Solutions. Before we start, could I ask everyone to introduce themselves briefly?"
T: "Good. Now one participant keeps talking over the others. How do you manage that?"
S: "Sorry to interrupt, Paulo, I'd like to bring in Selin before we move on."

The teacher can then replay the exchange, draw attention to the softening device "sorry to interrupt," and ask Marta to try a second version with a different phrase. The correction is immediate and tied to a situation she will face in real working life.

Writing tasks work on the same principle of direct relevance. David might be given an email he drafted the previous week and asked to identify any phrasing that would read as too informal for a senior audience. A sentence such as "We need to sort this out ASAP or things will get messy" can be redrafted as "We would recommend resolving this matter before the end of the quarter to avoid further complications." The teacher can then make explicit the grammatical choices involved: the modal "would," the nominalisation "resolution," and the hedged time reference. The grammar lesson grows out of the writing problem rather than preceding it.

Authentic materials from the learner's own workplace carry particular weight in one-to-one teaching. An anonymised email chain that David has received, or a meeting agenda from Marta's company, gives the lesson content that no published coursebook can replicate. The vocabulary belongs to their sector; the register is the one their colleagues use, and the communicative stakes feel real. When a grammar point does need explicit attention, the teacher introduces it directly, explains the rule plainly, and returns immediately to the workplace task it serves.

## Introduction to One-to-One Teaching

One-to-one teaching is any lesson in which a single teacher works with a single student, with no other learners present. The format is extremely common in Business English, where professionals often seek targeted help with specific workplace skills, but it also appears regularly in general English tuition, exam preparation, and lessons with young learners whose parents prefer individual attention. Whatever the context, the structural conditions of the lesson are the same: two people, a shared goal, and an unbroken line of communication between them.

Those structural conditions create a teaching environment that differs from group work in several fundamental ways. In a group lesson, a teacher can pose a question to the class and allow a more confident student to answer while others listen and process. In a one-to-one lesson, there is no such buffer. If the student cannot answer, the silence belongs to both people in the room, and the teacher must respond to it immediately and directly.

The absence of peers also means that every difficulty the learner experiences is immediately visible. In a class of twelve, a student who has not understood an instruction can glance at a neighbour and follow their lead. A student working alone with a teacher has no such option. Consider a lesson with a student named Kenji, who has misunderstood the difference between a formal and an informal register. In a group, Kenji might absorb the correct model from a classmate's answer before his own turn arrives. In a one-to-one lesson, the teacher sees the confusion the moment Kenji speaks, and the lesson must adjust on the spot.

The communicative load of the lesson falls entirely on two people, which creates a particular intensity for both parties. The learner receives constant, personalised attention and cannot retreat into the background when the material becomes difficult. A student named Priya, preparing for a job interview, will be asked to respond to every prompt the teacher produces, with no pause while others take their turn. That sustained focus accelerates learning, but it also demands more from the learner in terms of concentration and willingness to be exposed.

The teacher faces an equally demanding set of conditions. Sustaining engagement across a full lesson without the natural variation that peer interaction provides requires a more active and deliberate approach to activity design. A teacher working with a group can introduce a pair-work task and step back briefly; the same teacher working with a single student must remain fully present and responsive throughout. One-to-one lessons therefore demand a higher degree of flexibility than group lessons, because there is no class dynamic to carry momentum when energy dips or a task runs short. The teacher must read the student continuously and be prepared to change direction without the safety net that a group provides.

## Pacing, Rapport, and Tailoring the One-to-One Lesson

Pacing is the skill of matching the speed and intensity of a lesson to the learner's actual state at any given moment. In a group class, a teacher can rely on the energy of several students to sustain momentum; in a one-to-one lesson, that resource does not exist. The teacher must watch for signs of fatigue or disengagement, such as shorter answers, slower response times, or a loss of eye contact, and act on them immediately. This might mean slowing the pace of new input, inserting a brief break, switching from a writing task to a spoken one, or setting aside a planned activity entirely. A rigid attachment to a lesson plan is a liability in one-to-one teaching.

Rapport in one-to-one teaching is both an advantage and a responsibility. When a learner trusts the teacher, she is more willing to attempt difficult language, make mistakes openly, and ask for clarification without embarrassment. Consider a teacher working with Marta, a Spanish-speaking marketing manager who has grown comfortable enough to laugh at her own errors. That comfort is productive, and the teacher should protect it. The risk, however, is that a warm working relationship can allow lessons to drift into extended personal conversation that produces little language learning. The teacher's role is to value the relationship while keeping the session purposeful, redirecting naturally when discussion moves too far from the learning goals.

T: That's a great point about the conference. Can we use that situation to practise the language you'd need to chair a meeting?
S: Yes, good idea. I always get nervous when I have to interrupt someone politely.
T: Perfect. So let's look at some phrases for that.

Tailoring a one-to-one lesson over time requires the teacher to keep a systematic record of what the learner does and how she responds. A running log of recurring errors gives the teacher material for targeted recycling. Notes on which task types produce the most engagement, whether a learner responds better to role play than to gap-fill exercises, for instance, allow the teacher to design sessions that sustain concentration. Revisiting language from earlier lessons in new contexts is one of the most reliable ways to move vocabulary and grammar from short-term recognition into active use.

Suppose a teacher notices across several sessions that Paulo, a Brazilian finance director, consistently uses the simple past where the present perfect is required in spoken production, saying "I already sent the report" instead of "I've already sent it." Rather than correcting this in isolation each time, the teacher builds a short recycling activity into the next three lessons, each time embedding the target structure in a different professional context: a telephone call, a progress update email, and a meeting summary. By the third lesson, Paulo is self-correcting before the teacher intervenes. This kind of planned, incremental recycling is only possible because the teacher has been observing and recording carefully from the beginning.

## Practical Challenges and Professional Habits for the Specialist Teacher

New teachers moving into Business English often feel that their lack of professional experience in finance, law, or logistics makes them unqualified to teach in those fields. That feeling rests on a misunderstanding of the teacher's role. The teacher is present as a language expert, not a business consultant, and the learner already possesses the professional knowledge. A teacher named Sarah, working with a senior procurement manager for the first time, might feel uncertain when her learner begins describing contract negotiation procedures. The appropriate response is to ask the learner to explain the process in plain terms so that Sarah can contextualise the language work accurately, for example by drafting the kind of conditional clauses that appear in supplier agreements.

Workplace lessons bring a specific logistical difficulty: interruptions. A learner who takes a phone call mid-lesson, steps out to speak to a colleague, or cuts the session short because a meeting has overrun is behaving in accordance with the demands of a working environment, not showing disrespect toward the teacher. The teacher needs a mental habit of knowing, at any point in a lesson, which single language point is most worth returning to if time is suddenly reduced. Keeping the lesson plan flexible enough to contract around a core activity means that even a session cut from sixty minutes to thirty still produces something useful.

Teaching a learner who is significantly more senior or experienced than the teacher can feel uncomfortable, particularly in one-to-one settings where there is no group dynamic to diffuse the pressure. A teacher in their late twenties working with a company director in their fifties is not expected to match that person's professional authority. The teacher holds a different kind of authority, one grounded in knowledge of grammar, register, and spoken interaction, and that authority is entirely legitimate. When the learner, call him Mr. Tanaka, explains how quarterly board reports are structured, the teacher listens and then responds: T: "Thank you, that's useful. So when you present those figures, would you typically say 'the figures indicate' or 'the figures show'? Look at the register difference between those two." The teacher's expertise becomes visible without any need to compete on professional ground.

Several professional habits support consistent quality across both specialisations. Writing brief notes after each session, even four or five lines recording what was covered and what needs revisiting, prevents the drift that occurs when a teacher relies on memory alone. Setting clear short-term goals with the learner at the start of a course, and reviewing those goals every three or four sessions, keeps the work purposeful and gives the learner a sense of measurable progress. Being transparent about the lesson plan, showing the learner what is planned and why, gives the learner genuine ownership of their learning rather than positioning them as a passive recipient of the teacher's decisions.

Both Business English and one-to-one teaching ask a great deal of a teacher's attention and adaptability. There is no group to absorb a weak moment, and the content is tied directly to the learner's real professional life, so the teacher is constantly reading small signals: a hesitation before a particular structure, a shift in confidence when the topic moves from written to spoken tasks, a moment when the learner's vocabulary becomes imprecise under pressure. Noticing matters. Developing the habit of responding to those signals in a one-to-one or specialist context produces a quality of observation that carries directly into every other classroom a teacher will ever work in.
