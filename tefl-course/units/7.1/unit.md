# Teaching Listening

## Why Listening Needs to Be Taught

Listening is a skill that develops through deliberate, structured practice, not through incidental exposure alone. A student who hears English spoken around her every day may still struggle to follow a recorded announcement or understand a colleague speaking quickly on the phone. The classroom is where that gap closes, because the teacher can control the input, sequence the tasks, and give the student repeated opportunities to process spoken language with a clear purpose.

Audio materials serve several distinct functions when they are used well. A student named Carlos, working at an intermediate level, may encounter the word "registration" in a reading passage and learn its spelling without ever connecting it to the sound a native speaker produces. Hearing the word in a recorded dialogue, in a sentence where context makes the meaning clear, builds a different kind of knowledge. The source text identifies four specific gains that audio materials make possible: students develop and improve listening comprehension, hear new vocabulary in context, make connections between sounds and written words, and encounter a range of accents they would not otherwise meet in a single classroom.

One practical way to integrate audio is to present instructions and tasks in spoken form rather than written form, so that students must use listening skills to understand what they are being asked to do. A teacher might record the directions for a grammar exercise and play them at the start of class, requiring students like Amara to listen carefully before they can begin. This shifts the cognitive demand from reading to listening without changing the underlying language content of the lesson.

When audio-playing technology is unavailable, the teacher still has workable options. Dictation tasks, in which the teacher reads aloud and students transcribe, give students practice connecting sounds to written words under controlled conditions. Inviting a guest speaker into the classroom exposes students to a voice, a pace, and an accent that differ from the teacher's own, which is itself a form of listening challenge. Neither option requires equipment, and both keep listening at the centre of the activity.

The sections that follow address how teachers design that deliberate practice from start to finish, from the tasks that prepare students to listen, through the listening activity itself, to the work students do once the audio has ended.

## The Pre-Listening Stage: Building the Bridge

Before students hear a single word of audio, the teacher's job is to reduce the distance between what learners already know and what the recording will demand of them. Pre-listening work serves that purpose: it activates prior knowledge, builds interest in the topic, and introduces vocabulary that would otherwise slow comprehension to a halt once the audio begins.

A lesson plan developed for an intermediate adult class at Eastern College illustrates how this works in practice. The teacher opens by displaying four pictures on the whiteboard, each showing a driver engaged in dangerous behaviour: a woman applying makeup at the wheel, a man on a mobile phone, a woman texting, and a man visibly drowsy. The rationale given in the plan is direct: the images create a positive, welcoming atmosphere and generate interest before a word of instruction has been spoken. A student named Maria, who works in hospitality and commutes daily, already has opinions about distracted driving; the pictures give her somewhere to put that knowledge.

The teacher then moves the class toward the topic through a sequence of questions. Students identify what is happening in each picture, decide which behaviour is most dangerous, and consider whether talking on a phone while driving is legal. This sequence does two things simultaneously. It involves students personally, because the content connects to their own lives, and it activates vocabulary they will need when the listening text arrives. A student named David might volunteer the word "speeding" before the teacher has written it on the board; another student might supply "bad weather." The teacher collects these contributions and builds a visible list, so the class constructs the topic together rather than receiving it passively.

The plan then addresses a smaller but equally important problem: certain words in the listening text are likely to be unfamiliar, and encountering them cold inside a fast-moving audio passage can cause a learner to lose the thread entirely. The teacher pre-teaches four items: "take a statement," "accident report," "license, registration, and insurance," and the idiom "come out of nowhere." Each item is presented orally and written on the board, so the approach supports understanding through both the spoken word and visual context. When a student named Yuki hears a police officer say "I need to take your statement" in the recording, she already has a mental slot for that phrase; she does not have to stop and puzzle over it while the dialogue continues.

The final move in this stage is to set expectations about the situation students are about to hear. The teacher tells the class that a police officer will be speaking with two people involved in a traffic accident, that the dialogue will play more than once, and that the first listening requires no textbook. This framing matters because it tells students what kind of attention to pay. Knowing the situation in advance, a student like Carlos can listen for the relationship between the speakers rather than trying to process every word from scratch. The pre-listening stage, in this sense, is not a warm-up in the casual sense of the word; it is the scaffolding that makes the listening task itself achievable.

## Listening for Gist: The First-Listen Task

Gist, as a concept, means the overall meaning or general sense of a spoken text, as distinct from its specific details. A first-listen task asks students to grasp that overall meaning without yet attending to particular facts, names, or figures. The lesson plan from the source text makes this design principle concrete: the teacher tells students, "The first time you listen you do not need your textbook," and writes two or three broad orientation questions on the board before the audio begins.

That single instruction, putting the textbook away, does more than it might appear to do. When a student like Maria has a printed transcript or a worksheet in front of her, her attention divides between reading and listening, and the two channels compete. Removing the written text means her full cognitive capacity goes toward constructing meaning from sound. The spoken message becomes the only input she has to work with, which is precisely the condition under which gist comprehension develops.

The questions the teacher writes on the board serve a related function. In the source lesson, the teacher asks students to consider who the people in the accident are and what the general situation involves. These are meaning-level questions, not detail-level ones. A student who can answer them after a single listening has demonstrated that he followed the thread of the exchange, even if he missed individual words or phrases along the way.

The source text also describes a broader classroom technique in which a teacher plays a short audio text, asks students to take notes as they listen, and then has them discuss the content in small groups before one member reports back to the class. This sequence shows how a gist task can do double work. The listening itself builds comprehension, and the small-group discussion that follows gives students an immediate communicative reason to consolidate what they heard. A student like Yuki, who caught the general situation but missed a name, can fill that gap through conversation with a partner rather than waiting for the teacher to supply the answer.

First-listen design, then, rests on two coordinated decisions: what students are asked to do with the text, and what physical and cognitive conditions surround that task. Broad questions, no written support, and a clear statement of the situation in advance all work together to make the first encounter with the audio productive rather than overwhelming. The detail work comes later, once students have a stable sense of what the text is about.

## Listening for Detail: Returning to the Audio

Once students have established a general sense of the text, the audio serves a second purpose. The lesson plan from *Shaping the Way We Teach English* separates two distinct objectives for the same recording: one asks students to determine the main ideas, and the other asks them to identify specific details. These are not the same cognitive task, and treating them as separate encounters with the same material is the practical mechanism that makes both achievable. A student named Carlos may have understood on the first listen that a police officer is interviewing two drivers after a collision; on the second listen, he is ready to catch the officer's exact questions and the specific details each driver provides.

The lesson plan notes explicitly that students "will get to hear the dialogue more than once," and the changed task is what gives each repetition its purpose. Replaying audio without a new focus asks students to do the same thing again, which produces diminishing returns. Replaying it with a sharper, more specific question directs attention to parts of the text that the first-listen task did not require students to notice. A student named Amara, who grasped the general situation during the first pass, can now concentrate on a single speaker's account of where her car was positioned before the impact; that detail is precisely what the second task demands of her.

This kind of targeted, purposeful listening appears under the label "focused listening" across a wide range of lesson descriptions in *Create to Communicate*, from beginner-level drawing activities to advanced collage and sculpture units. The consistency of that label across proficiency levels and themes signals that focused listening is not a technique reserved for advanced classes. It is a recurring classroom goal that teachers build toward at every stage. The skill involves directing attention deliberately rather than processing audio as an undifferentiated stream, and repeated listening with a changed task is one of the clearest ways to train that habit.

A concrete post-listening sequence from *Shaping the Way We Teach English* illustrates how detail work can be consolidated and made competitive. After the detail-focused listen, small groups write a summary that captures as many accurate specifics as possible, with the most detailed and accurate group winning, determined by class vote or teacher judgment. A student named Jin, who caught the detail about the other driver running a red light, becomes a valuable contributor to her group's summary in a way she could not have been after the first listen alone. The task rewards precision, gives students a reason to compare what they each noticed, and produces a piece of writing the teacher can use to assess how well the detail-listening objective was met.

## The Post-Listening Stage: Using What Was Heard

Once the audio work is complete, the lesson should move directly into activities that require students to use what they heard. The post-listening stage is the point at which comprehension becomes production: students take the vocabulary, grammar, and situational knowledge built during listening and put them to communicative use. In the lesson plan drawn on throughout this unit, the teacher has been deliberate about this connection from the start, noting that the accident scenarios from the listening text will feed directly into an information-gap speaking activity later in the same lesson.

An information-gap activity is one in which two students each hold different pieces of information and must speak to each other to complete a shared task. In the traffic accident lesson, students use the past continuous tense and car and driving vocabulary they encountered in the audio to interview a partner about a fictional accident. A student named Carlos, playing the role of a police officer, asks his partner Amara what she was doing at the moment of the collision. Amara, working from a different prompt card, answers with details Carlos does not have. Neither student can complete the task by reading silently; they have to listen and speak. The activity works precisely because the language it demands is the same language the students have just heard modelled in context.

The post-listening stage can also close with a reflective question that asks students to step back from the immediate task. Rather than ending the lesson when the information gap is finished, the teacher poses an open-ended question requiring students to think about what they learned and where they might use it outside class. The teacher might ask:

T: "You practised reporting an accident today. Where else in your life might you need to describe something that was happening when something else went wrong?"

S: "At work, maybe, if something breaks and my boss asks what happened."

That kind of question asks students to connect classroom language to real situations, giving the lesson a purposeful close rather than an abrupt stop.

The key principle is continuity. The post-listening stage should feel like the same lesson continuing, not a new activity that happens to follow the audio. When the speaking task uses the same vocabulary the students heard, when the grammar they practised in the listening appears again in their own sentences, and when the final reflection ties the content to their lives, students experience the lesson as a coherent whole. A student like Amara, who struggled with the past continuous in an earlier grammar lesson, may find that hearing it in the audio and then using it immediately in conversation with Carlos is what finally makes the structure feel natural.

## Practical Task Sequences: Putting the Stages Together

A complete listening lesson moves through a predictable arc, and the lesson plan built around a police interview after a traffic accident illustrates each stage clearly. The teacher opens with four photographs displayed on the whiteboard: a driver applying makeup, a man on a cell phone, a woman texting, and a man yawning at the wheel. Students identify what is happening in each image and discuss which behaviour is most dangerous. This warm-up does two things at once: it draws on knowledge students already carry into the room, and it builds the mental context they will need when the audio begins.

Before the recording plays, the teacher addresses vocabulary that would otherwise slow comprehension to a halt. Items such as "take a statement," "accident report," and "come out of nowhere" are presented orally and in writing, with the teacher eliciting the concept first and then confirming it. A student like Fatima, who reads well but struggles with fast spoken English, benefits from hearing these phrases before they appear in the audio, because she can recognise them rather than stop to decode them. Pre-teaching of this kind is not about making the task easy; it is about removing obstacles that have nothing to do with listening ability.

The first listening pass happens without the textbook open. The teacher tells students they will hear a police officer interviewing two people involved in an accident, and writes two orienting questions on the board: who are the people, and what happened. This is gist listening, meaning the goal is to grasp the overall situation rather than capture every detail. Students like Marco, who tend to panic when they miss a word, find that a clear gist task gives them permission to keep listening rather than freeze.

The second pass introduces focused listening, a skill label that appears across lessons from beginner to advanced level in art-activity sequences covering topics as varied as sea creatures, neighbourhood collages, and surrealist dream drawings. Focused listening means attending to specific, pre-announced information rather than trying to process everything simultaneously. For the traffic accident lesson, students now open their books and answer detail questions about what each driver was doing before the collision. The same staged approach works at lower levels: a beginner class doing a treasure map activity listens for specific directional phrases, while an intermediate class working with a neighbourhood collage listens for particular community vocabulary. The proficiency level changes the complexity of the target information, not the underlying sequence.

The post-listening task completes the arc by asking students to produce language. In the traffic accident lesson, students use the past continuous and the unit vocabulary to interview a partner about a fictional accident, then report the details to another pair. The listening has supplied both the model and the raw material for the speaking. Writing tasks serve the same function: a summary competition, in which groups listen, discuss, and then write the most accurate account they can, keeps the audio at the centre while moving students toward independent production.

One practical constraint governs the whole sequence: time. Extending the warm-up discussion because students are engaged, or replaying the audio a third time because one student asks, can consume the minutes allocated to the post-listening task and prevent the class from reaching its stated objectives. Each stage needs a realistic time allocation written into the plan before the lesson begins, and teachers should treat those allocations as genuine limits rather than rough suggestions. A lesson that ends before students have produced anything leaves the listening work unfinished, however well the earlier stages went.
