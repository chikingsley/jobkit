# Teaching Grammar: Explicit, Implicit, and the Evidence

## Noticing and Language Awareness: The Starting Point

Communicative language teaching places function at the center of the classroom. The goal is meaningful use: students learn to request, apologize, narrate, and negotiate in the target language. Teachers working within this tradition have consistently found that attending to grammar, vocabulary, and pronunciation also matters, and that leaving form entirely to chance produces uneven results. The tension running through this unit is practical: how does a teacher focus on language form without abandoning communicative principles and retreating to rules, drills, and rote memorization as the primary tools?

One answer that has emerged from classroom practice is the development of language awareness, defined in this course as a focus on aspects of language within a given context, or more plainly, paying attention to and noticing the language detail present in a situation. The definition ties form to context from the outset. A student noticing that a newspaper article uses the past simple to report completed events is doing something qualitatively different from a student memorizing a conjugation table; the first is building language awareness, the second is not.

Language awareness as a teaching orientation has five recognizable characteristics. The first is the use of meta-language, the shared vocabulary teachers and students use to talk about language itself, terms such as "phrasal verb," "past perfect," or "formal register." Consider a brief classroom exchange: T: "Yuki, what form is the verb in that sentence?" S: "It's past tense." T: "Right, and why do you think the writer chose past tense there?" Meta-language is doing real work in that moment. The second characteristic is training learners to become conscious, reflective participants in their own learning, with analysis and noticing treated as habitual practices rather than occasional interruptions. A third characteristic is the deliberate balance of inductive and deductive techniques: sometimes the teacher states a rule and students apply it; at other times students encounter examples first and work toward the rule themselves. The remaining two characteristics concern the quality of material used. Language awareness activities attend to the appropriateness of language, both situational and cultural, and they draw on comprehensible input from authentic sources, texts and recordings produced for real communicative purposes rather than constructed solely to illustrate a grammar point.

These five characteristics together describe a teaching stance rather than a single method. A teacher who builds language awareness holds communication and accuracy simultaneously rather than choosing between them. The question taken up from here is which instructional techniques, explicit or implicit, best support that dual aim, and what the research evidence shows about their relative effectiveness.

## Explicit and Implicit Teaching: Deductive and Inductive Techniques

Deductive techniques, as defined in the training module this unit draws on (Shaping the Way We Teach English, Module 2), are those in which learners are taught the rules of language and then expected to apply them. Inductive techniques work in the opposite direction: learners discover the rules of language themselves through their experience with the language. Both terms describe how a teacher chooses to sequence the relationship between a grammatical pattern and a learner's conscious awareness of it. The distinction matters because it shapes every decision about when to explain, when to withhold explanation, and when to let the language speak for itself.

The Language Safari activity illustrates inductive technique in a classroom-ready form. A teacher, call her Ms. Reyes, gives her adult learners three words from a news article, including "run," before they see the text. Each student writes down what the word means. Then they read the article and discover that "run" appears four times, functioning as a verb in a police chase, as part of the phrasal verb "ran out of," and as a component of "ran red lights." A student named Carlos notes that his original definition covered only one of those uses. The reflection questions then ask learners to consider how the surrounding grammatical structures gave them clues about meaning, turning the reading itself into a site of grammar noticing rather than grammar instruction. No rule was stated in advance; the pattern emerged from contact with authentic language.

Explicit instruction looks different in practice, and the primary classroom vignette from the module provides a clear contrast. During the skit, the teacher pairs the familiar word "cold" with the less frequent word "shivering," repeating both several times while making physical gestures that demonstrate the meaning. The target form is supplied directly, not left for discovery. A child in that classroom does not need to infer what "shivering" means from context alone; the teacher has made the connection visible and immediate. This is deductive in spirit even without a formal rule statement, because the teacher controls the moment of form-meaning mapping and delivers it to the learner rather than waiting for the learner to arrive at it independently.

The module presents these two modes as complementary rather than competing. The action-plan section asks teachers to decide where in a lesson they would teach something deductively and where they would let students figure it out inductively, treating the choice as a design question rather than an ideological one. A single lesson might open with inductive noticing through an authentic text, then shift to explicit confirmation of the pattern once learners have had the chance to encounter it in use. The balance depends on the complexity of the target form, the proficiency of the learners, and the purpose of the activity.

What the module does not settle is which mode produces stronger learning outcomes over time. That question requires evidence from controlled research, and the following sections examine four meta-analyses that have measured the relative effects of explicit and implicit instruction across a wide range of learner populations, proficiency levels, and grammatical targets.

## What the Meta-Analyses Show: Explicit Instruction Has the Edge

Norris and Ortega (2000) conducted the first large-scale meta-analysis in this area, synthesizing results from 49 studies of focused second language instruction. Their central finding was that focused instruction of any kind produces large learning gains compared to no instruction at all. Within that broad result, explicit instruction, defined as teaching in which rules are stated openly and learners are expected to understand and apply them consciously, outperformed implicit instruction, in which learners are exposed to patterned input without direct explanation. A second finding from the same analysis is relevant to classroom planning: Focus on Form, where grammar attention is woven into communicative activity, and Focus on Forms, where discrete structures are taught in sequence, produced effects of equivalent size (Norris & Ortega, 2000). A teacher choosing between a task-based lesson that pauses to address a recurring error and a more traditional grammar syllabus need not assume one approach is inherently superior to the other.

Spada and Tomita (2010) extended this line of inquiry by asking whether the advantage of explicit instruction holds across grammatical forms of different complexity. Across 41 studies, explicit instruction showed larger effects than implicit instruction for both simple and complex forms (Spada & Tomita, 2010). This finding matters because teachers sometimes reserve explicit explanation for straightforward rules, such as third-person singular agreement, while assuming that complex structures like the English passive or conditional forms are better absorbed through exposure. The data do not support that assumption.

Goo, Granena, Yilmaz, and Novella (2015) then updated and extended the Norris and Ortega synthesis with a pool of 34 studies, 11 of which overlapped the original, and arrived at the same conclusion: explicit instruction produces stronger outcomes than implicit instruction (Goo et al., 2015). An update of this kind is not an independent confirmation, since roughly a third of its evidence base is shared with the analysis it revisits, but a refreshed pool reaching the same verdict as two earlier teams still adds weight to the pattern.

Taken together, these three meta-analyses point in a consistent direction. A teacher preparing a lesson on reported speech for an intermediate adult class, as Ms. Reyes does in the exchange below, has good grounds for stating the rule clearly, showing how it operates in a text, and giving learners structured practice before moving to freer production. Leaving learners to infer the pattern from exposure alone is the less effective option, not as a matter of teaching philosophy, but as a matter of measured outcome across hundreds of classrooms and learner groups.

T: "When we change direct speech to reported speech, the verb tense shifts back. 'I am tired' becomes 'She said she was tired.' Can you see the shift?"
S: "So 'am' changes to 'was'?"
T: "Good. Look at the next example and tell me what you expect to change."

That kind of explicit, guided sequence is precisely what the meta-analytic evidence consistently associates with stronger learning.

## The Measurement Caveat: Why the Gap Is Smaller Than It Looks

The picture becomes more complicated when researchers examine the size of the explicit advantage more carefully. Kang, Sok, and Han (2019) analyzed 35 years of form-focused instruction research, drawing on 54 studies involving 5,051 learners, and found that the overall difference between explicit and implicit instruction was only minor. Several variables moderated the results: the mode of the outcome measure used, the proficiency level of the learners, the instructional setting, and the intensity of the treatment. A teacher reading only the headline finding from earlier meta-analyses would miss this qualification entirely.

The most consequential moderating variable is the type of test used to measure learning. Many of the studies in earlier meta-analyses, including those reviewed by Norris and Ortega (2000), relied on grammar tests and error-correction tasks to assess outcomes. Those instruments are designed to tap explicit knowledge, the kind of conscious, rule-based knowledge that explicit instruction directly targets. A learner who has been taught a rule and practiced applying it in controlled conditions will perform well on a test that asks her to identify errors or complete a paradigm. The test is measuring the same kind of knowledge that the instruction delivered.

This concern, that the early evidence base leaned on instruments favoring explicit knowledge, has been a standing critique of Norris and Ortega's measure mix within the field. Kang, Sok, and Han (2019) put the measurement question to a direct test and found that mode did moderate results, though not in the simple way the critique might predict: studies using oral measures showed larger overall effects for form-focused instruction than studies relying on written measures alone. Their most striking finding concerned durability. On delayed posttests, implicit instruction showed the more durable effect, g = 1.76, against g = 0.77 for explicit instruction. Consider a student named Fatima who has memorized the rule for third-person singular agreement and scores well on a fill-in-the-blank test. In a role-play activity where she must respond quickly and naturally, she may still omit the inflection under communicative pressure. The test and the task are measuring different things, and the choice of measure shapes the conclusion a researcher draws.

Explicit instruction does not lose its advantage under all conditions. Spada and Tomita (2010) found explicit effects for both simple and complex structures across a range of measures, and Goo, Granena, Yilmaz, and Novella (2015) confirmed the explicit advantage in their updated pool of 34 studies. The advantage, however, is not uniform across every context, learner group, and assessment type. A trainee who treats "explicit beats implicit" as an absolute law will be poorly equipped to evaluate research claims or to make sensible decisions about instruction for a specific class.

The practical implication for teachers is to examine what a given study measured before accepting its conclusions, because the outcome measure matters enormously. If a study reports that explicit instruction produced better results, the next question is whether the outcome measure tested controlled knowledge, spontaneous production, or something in between, and whether learning was checked immediately or after a delay. Learner proficiency and instructional intensity matter too, since Kang, Sok, and Han (2019) identified both as moderators. A beginner working through a short treatment in a large class is not the same case as an intermediate learner receiving sustained, focused instruction over several weeks, and the evidence should be read accordingly.

## From Evidence to Classroom: Teaching Forms Openly, Then Communicatively

The accumulated evidence points toward a clear starting position for lesson design: teach the form openly, name it, and give learners the metalanguage to talk about it. Metalanguage, as the module defines it, is the language teachers and students use to discuss language itself, including terms such as verb, noun phrase, or present tense (Shaping the Way We Teach English, Module 2). Norris and Ortega (2000) found that focused instruction produces large gains, and Spada and Tomita (2010) confirmed that explicit treatment outperforms implicit treatment for both simple and complex structures. Beginning a grammar sequence with a direct explanation is a response to what the evidence consistently shows.

The primary classroom vignette in the module illustrates how this opening move works in practice. The teacher and her aide introduce target vocabulary by repeating two words together, "cold" and "shivering," while using physical gesture to anchor meaning. The exchange is brief and controlled.

T: "They were cold, shivering. Can you show me shivering, like this?"
S: [students imitate the gesture]
T: "Yes, shivering. Cold, shivering."

This is explicit instruction in the Focus on Forms sense the unit defined earlier, a discrete item presented and rehearsed in its own right rather than attention drawn to form in the middle of communication: the teacher supplies the form, repeats it, and ensures students register it consciously before any extended use is required.

The sequence cannot stop there. Kang, Sok, and Han (2019) found only a minor overall difference between explicit and implicit instruction once 35 years of studies were weighed together, and on delayed posttests the implicit conditions in their pool produced the more durable gains. A learner who can state a rule may still fail to deploy the target form when attention is occupied by meaning. The module addresses this directly, defining language awareness as encompassing both a focus on form and the use of that form in context, a dimension it calls the pragmatic dimension of language use.

The poster task in the same classroom vignette enacts that pragmatic dimension. After the explicit vocabulary work, student groups plan an imaginary trip using authentic travel brochures, then record their decisions on a structured sheet covering destination, packing list, activities, and transport. The brochures are genuine communicative artifacts, not pedagogic substitutes, and the poster requires students to produce language for a real audience within the group.

T: "Look at your brochure. Where does your group want to go? Write the place on your green sheet."
S: "We want to go to the mountains. We need to pack boots."

The form introduced explicitly earlier now appears inside a communicative act, embedded in choices that matter to the learners making them.

The lesson design implication follows directly from this sequence. Plan a focused, explicit moment first: name the target form, use metalanguage to describe it, and give learners enough controlled exposure to register it consciously. Then construct a communicative task that makes the same form genuinely useful, one where students must draw on it to accomplish something, whether planning, persuading, or reporting. Fatima, having learned the structure of noun phrases through direct instruction, will consolidate that knowledge far more durably if she then has to select nouns and modifiers to describe what her group is packing for a trip to the coast. The explicit stage builds awareness; the communicative stage builds the capacity to use what has been noticed.

## Putting It Together: A Lesson Design Framework

Designing a lesson around this sequence requires four decisions made in order. First, identify the target form with precision: the simple past with irregular verbs in narrative contexts, for instance, rather than the broader category of past tense. Second, select authentic material in which that form appears naturally, material students are likely to encounter outside the classroom. A news article, a travel brochure, or a short recorded announcement all qualify; the module's Language Safari procedure offers a ready model, asking students to locate target items before discussing how context shaped their meaning. Third, decide which parts of the lesson call for deductive teaching and which call for inductive discovery. Carlos may benefit from seeing the rule for irregular past forms stated plainly before he reads, while Priya, a more advanced learner, may arrive at the same generalization by sorting examples she has found in the text herself. Both paths can coexist in a single lesson.

The meta-analytic record gives explicit instruction a reliable advantage as a starting point. Norris and Ortega (2000) found that focused L2 instruction produced large gains and that explicit approaches outperformed implicit ones across forty-nine studies. Spada and Tomita (2010) confirmed this pattern for both simple and complex structures across forty-one studies. That evidence justifies opening with a clear, direct explanation or a guided discovery sequence that ends in an explicit statement of the rule. The lesson cannot stop there, however. Kang, Sok, and Han (2019) found that once 35 years of research is weighed together the overall difference between explicit and implicit instruction is minor, and that the durable gains on delayed posttests belonged to implicit instruction. A lesson that ends at the rule-and-exercise stage leaves students like Carlos able to complete a gap-fill but unable to use the form when they are focused on meaning.

The communicative task is therefore not optional. It is the stage where the form earns its place in the student's productive repertoire. The task should require the target form to accomplish something real: Carlos and Priya might record a short group report on a historical event, or plan a journey using travel brochures as the primary school class in the module did, producing a poster that forces them to narrate in the past tense. The teacher's role at this stage shifts from instructor to monitor, noting errors for brief, targeted feedback rather than interrupting the communicative flow.

Reflection closes the sequence. The module's summary discussion poses the question of whether students learn more through contextualized practice and a focus on language within context; that question belongs in the trainee's own planning process as well. After teaching a lesson in this shape for the first time, a teacher might ask whether the authentic material was genuinely comprehensible, whether the explicit stage was long enough to build awareness without crowding out task time, and whether the communicative activity demanded the target form or left it optional. If Daniel never needed the past tense to complete the task, that is a signal that the design needs revision, not that explicit instruction failed. Systematic reflection of this kind turns a single lesson into a source of evidence about what works in a specific classroom with specific learners.
