# The Phonemic Chart

## What the Phonemic Chart Is

The phonemic chart is an organized display of the sounds of English, with each sound represented by a distinct symbol drawn from the International Phonetic Alphabet, a standardised notation system developed by the International Phonetic Association in the late nineteenth century. The chart arranges these symbols so that teachers and learners can see the full sound inventory of the language at a glance. A printed or digital copy of the chart used in this course is available in the course resources section, and you should keep it open as you work through the remaining sections of this unit.

The central principle of the chart is a strict one-to-one relationship between symbol and sound. One symbol corresponds to one sound, and one sound corresponds to one symbol, with no exceptions. Ordinary English spelling offers no such consistency. Consider the letter "c" in three common words: in "cat" it represents the sound /k/, in "city" it represents the sound /s/, and in "church" it forms part of the sound /tʃ/. A learner like Yuki, encountering English spelling for the first time, has no reliable way to predict which sound a given letter will produce; the phonemic chart removes that uncertainty entirely.

The classic ELT version of the chart contains 44 phonemes and was built around standard Southern British English, the accent sometimes called Received Pronunciation. American English, the variety this course teaches, differs in predictable ways: American speakers pronounce the "r" after vowels (in "car" and "teacher"), and several British vowel distinctions merge or shift in American speech, so the exact phoneme count varies slightly by accent and by analysis. None of this weakens the chart's value. The principle of one symbol for one sound holds in every variety, and a teacher who learns the symbols for American English can read and produce transcriptions for any accent her materials use. The chart in this course's resources is the standard IPA chart, which covers them all.

The 44 phonemes divide into two broad categories: vowels and consonants. Vowels are sounds produced with an open vocal tract and no significant obstruction of airflow, while consonants involve some degree of constriction or closure at a point in the mouth or throat. Within the vowel category, the chart makes a further distinction between pure vowels, called monophthongs, and gliding vowels, called diphthongs, where the mouth moves from one position to another during a single syllable. A teacher might illustrate the difference to a student by contrasting the steady vowel in "bed" with the gliding vowel in "bay," asking the student to notice how the jaw and lips shift position as the second sound is produced.

## Vowel Sounds: Monophthongs and Diphthongs

English vowels divide into two groups on the phonemic chart: monophthongs, in which the mouth holds a single steady position throughout the sound, and diphthongs, in which the mouth glides from one position to a second position within the same syllable. The chart displays twelve monophthongs and eight diphthongs, giving English a notably large vowel inventory compared with many other languages.

Among the monophthongs, some symbols will already look familiar because they resemble ordinary letters, while others require careful attention. The symbol /ɪ/ represents the short vowel in "sit", produced with the tongue high and forward but without the muscular tension of a long vowel. Its long counterpart is /iː/, heard in "seat", where the colon-like mark (ː) signals that the vowel is held for a noticeably greater duration. A student named Yuki, accustomed to the five-vowel system of Japanese, may initially treat these two sounds as identical; a teacher can draw attention to the length difference by contrasting minimal pairs: T: "Listen: 'sit', 'seat'. Can you hear the difference?" S: "The second one is longer?" T: "Yes, and the mouth is a little more tense for that second one."

The length mark (ː) appears beside several monophthongs on the chart, always indicating a longer, more sustained articulation. The vowel /ɑː/, as in "car", is both long and produced with the mouth wide open and the tongue low and back. By contrast, /ɒ/, as in "hot", is short, with the mouth open but the lips slightly rounded. The vowel /ʌ/, as in "cup", sits in the center of the mouth and is short; learners whose first language uses a similar sound often find it more accessible than the long vowels.

One monophthong deserves particular attention: the schwa, transcribed as /ə/. The schwa is the most frequent vowel sound in English, appearing exclusively in unstressed syllables. It is the first sound in "about" (/əˈbaʊt/) and the final sound in "teacher" (/ˈtiːtʃə/). Because English reduces unstressed vowels toward this central, relaxed sound, a learner who pronounces every vowel with full value will sound unnatural. A student named Carlos might say "teacher" with a clear /ɛ/ at the end; the teacher can model the reduced form and ask him to let the final syllable go slack rather than holding a precise vowel shape.

The diphthongs each begin at one vowel position and move toward another. The symbol /eɪ/, as in "day", starts near the /e/ position and glides toward /ɪ/. The symbol /aɪ/, as in "fly", begins with a low open vowel and moves upward. The symbol /əʊ/, as in "go", opens with a schwa and rounds toward /ʊ/. Because the movement is part of the sound itself, a learner who holds the starting position without completing the glide will produce something that sounds like a different word entirely. Practicing the direction of each glide in isolation, before embedding it in connected speech, gives learners a reliable way to build that movement into muscle memory.

## Consonant Sounds: Voiced and Voiceless Pairs

The consonant section of the chart is arranged so that many symbols appear in pairs, side by side. Each pair shares the same place of articulation, meaning the same parts of the mouth are involved, and the same manner of articulation, meaning the airstream is shaped in the same way. The only difference between the two sounds in a pair is voicing.

Voicing refers to whether the vocal cords vibrate during the production of a sound. When a sound is voiced, the cords are drawn together and vibrate as air passes through them. When a sound is voiceless, the cords remain apart and no vibration occurs. A learner can feel this distinction directly by pressing two fingers lightly against the front of the throat and alternating between a sustained /s/ and a sustained /z/. The buzzing sensation present for /z/ and absent for /s/ is the physical reality that the chart's pairing system represents.

The most familiar pairs are /p/ and /b/, as in "pan" and "ban", and /t/ and /d/, as in "ten" and "den". A student named Carlos, whose first language is Spanish, may produce both members of a pair with voicing, making "pan" sound like "ban" in connected speech. Pointing to the pair on the chart and asking him to produce the voiceless member while holding his throat gives him immediate physical feedback rather than an abstract correction.

The pairing extends beyond those stops. The fricatives /f/ and /v/, as in "fine" and "vine", form one pair, and /s/ and /z/, as in "sip" and "zip", form another. The postalveolar fricatives /ʃ/, as in "ship", and /ʒ/, as in "measure", are paired in the same way, though /ʒ/ appears far less frequently at the start of English words, a fact that can make it feel unfamiliar even to advanced learners. The affricates /tʃ/, as in "church", and /dʒ/, as in "judge", complete the set of paired consonants on the chart.

A classroom exchange can make the pairing concrete. T: "Put your hand on your throat and say 'ship', then 'measure'. Can you feel the difference?" S: "The second one buzzes." T: "Good. That buzz is voicing, and those two sounds form a pair." Tactile checks of this kind take only a few seconds and anchor the abstract symbol to a physical sensation the learner already possesses.

Several consonants on the chart have no voiced or voiceless counterpart. The consonant /h/, as in "hat", is voiceless and stands alone. The nasals /m/, /n/, and /ŋ/, as in "sing", are all voiced and have no voiceless equivalents in English; the approximants /l/, /r/, /w/, and /j/, as in "yes", are similarly voiced without a counterpart. Unpaired consonants are learned as individual symbols rather than as members of a contrasting set, which requires a slight shift in how the teacher frames them when introducing that portion of the chart.

## Why One Symbol, One Sound Matters for Teaching

English spelling gives learners almost no reliable guide to pronunciation. The letters "ough" alone represent at least five distinct sounds, as any learner who has encountered "though", "through", "tough", "cough", and "bough" in the same week will confirm. A student like Yuki, working through a vocabulary list at home, has no way of knowing from the spelling alone whether a new word rhymes with "go", "blue", "stuff", "off", or "cow". Spelling and sound are disconnected in English in a way that is not true of many other languages.

The phonemic chart resolves this problem by assigning one symbol to one sound, without exception. Once a learner knows that /θ/ represents the voiceless dental fricative, that knowledge transfers immediately and completely to every word containing that sound. A teacher can write /θ/ on the board and a student like Carlos, who has just learned the symbol, will produce the first sound of "think", "three", and "throw" correctly, even if he has never seen those words before. The symbol carries the pronunciation; the spelling is irrelevant.

This consistency also makes learners more independent when they use a dictionary. Most learners' dictionaries, including widely used titles such as the Longman Dictionary of Contemporary English and the Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary, print a full phonemic transcription alongside every headword. A student who can read those transcriptions can look up any unfamiliar word and know immediately how to say it, without waiting to ask a teacher or hear a recording. In a classroom exchange, this might look like the following.

T: "You looked up 'colonel' last night. How did you know it was pronounced /ˈkɜːnəl/?"
S: "I read the symbols in the dictionary. The spelling made no sense to me, but the symbols did."

That exchange illustrates the kind of self-sufficiency the chart is designed to support. The chart is a reference tool, not a subject of study in its own right. A learner who can use phonemic transcription to check pronunciation and to decode dictionary entries has gained something genuinely practical, but the goal was never to memorize forty-four symbols for their own sake. The goal was accurate, confident speech, and the chart is one reliable means of reaching it.

## Using the Chart in the Classroom

A displayed phonemic chart gives the teacher a silent pointing tool that can break a cycle of repeated mishearing. When a learner such as Carlos keeps producing the wrong vowel in a word despite hearing the teacher say it several times, pointing directly to the relevant symbol on the chart shifts his attention from the stream of sound to a single, stable visual target. The teacher says nothing; the finger does the work. That silence is deliberate, because speaking the word again repeats the input that has already failed to land.

Writing a word's phonemic transcription on the board alongside its conventional spelling makes silent and misleading letters visible in a way that explanation alone rarely achieves. A teacher might write *knight* on one side of the board and /naɪt/ beside it, drawing a line through the *k*, the *gh*, and the final *e* to show that none of them contribute to the spoken word. A student like Yuki, whose first language maps spelling to sound with high consistency, often finds this pairing genuinely clarifying rather than merely interesting. The transcription does not replace the spelling; it accounts for it.

Minimal-pair drilling with the chart is particularly useful when two sounds are distinct in English but not distinguished in a learner's first language. The contrast between /ɪ/ and /iː/ causes persistent difficulty for many speakers of Spanish or Japanese, because neither language treats that length and quality difference as meaningful. A teacher can point to /ɪ/ on the chart, say *ship*, then move a finger to /iː/ and say *sheep*, before asking the class to produce each word in turn. When a student like Fatima says both words with the same vowel, the teacher points again to the two separate symbols without comment, making the distinction a matter of location on the chart rather than a matter of repeated correction.

The chart can be displayed in several formats depending on the classroom context. A large laminated poster fixed to the wall becomes a permanent reference point that learners can consult during any activity, not only pronunciation exercises. A projected image on a screen suits a lesson where the teacher wants to zoom in on a particular section, such as the diphthongs, without the rest of the chart creating visual noise. A printed handout allows each learner to annotate their own copy, circling sounds they find difficult or writing example words beside symbols.

Familiarity with the symbols builds gradually and should not be rushed. A teacher who introduces the full chart in a single lesson and then expects learners to use it fluently is setting most of them up for confusion. A more productive approach introduces a small cluster of symbols in connection with vocabulary the class is already studying, so that each symbol arrives with a concrete word attached to it. Over several weeks, the chart fills in naturally, and learners like Carlos or Yuki begin to reach for it as a first resort rather than waiting to be directed toward it.

## Getting Started: Building Your Own Familiarity with the Chart

The most practical starting point is the group of symbols that correspond closely to familiar letter-sound relationships in English spelling. The symbol /p/, for instance, behaves as a native speaker of almost any European language would expect, and the same is true of /b/, /t/, /d/, /m/, and /n/. Spending time with these first gives a trainee teacher a foothold before moving into less predictable territory. Confidence built on the familiar sounds makes the unfamiliar ones less daunting when they arrive.

From that foundation, a trainee can move steadily toward the symbols that have no obvious spelling equivalent. The symbol /ŋ/ represents the sound at the end of "singing" and "long," a sound that never appears at the start of a word in English and that many learners initially struggle to isolate. The symbol /ʒ/ captures the middle consonant in "measure" and "television," a sound that exists in English but rarely appears in its spelling in any predictable way. The pair /θ/ and /ð/ require particular attention: /θ/ is the voiceless sound in "think" and "bath," while /ð/ is its voiced counterpart, produced in the same position in the mouth but with the vocal cords active, as heard in "this," "that," and "those." Many trainees find it useful to say both sounds aloud in sequence, feeling the difference in vibration at the throat, before attempting to teach the distinction.

Transcribing common words by hand is one of the most effective ways to consolidate knowledge of the symbols. A trainee might begin with short, phonetically transparent words: "fish" becomes /fɪʃ/, "dog" becomes /dɒɡ/, and "train" becomes /treɪn/. After writing out a transcription, checking it against a reliable learners' dictionary such as the Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary or the Cambridge Learner's Dictionary confirms accuracy and corrects any symbol a trainee has misremembered. This habit of self-checking builds the kind of careful precision that a teacher needs when a student like Fatima points to a word in the dictionary and asks, "Is this the right sound?"

A teacher does not need the expertise of a trained phonetician to use the chart well in the classroom. A working knowledge of the 44 symbols, the ability to point to a symbol with confidence, and the capacity to model the associated sound clearly enough for learners to imitate it are sufficient. A teacher who can say to a student, "Look at /ð/; now watch my tongue and copy the sound in 'that,'" is doing what the chart is designed to support. That level of practical fluency is entirely achievable through steady, self-directed practice over the weeks of this course.
