# Grammatical Terms and the Parts of Speech

## Why Teachers Need Grammatical Metalanguage

Metalanguage is language used to talk about language itself. When a teacher says "verb," "clause," or "subject," those words are metalanguage: they name features of language rather than carrying ordinary meaning. A working knowledge of these terms is one of the practical tools a teacher brings to the classroom, even when students never hear the terms themselves.

Precise identification of an error depends on knowing what kind of error it is. Suppose a student named Priya writes "She sings beautiful" instead of "She sings beautifully." A teacher who can identify "beautiful" as an adjective used where an adverb is required has a clear picture of what has gone wrong. Without that identification, the teacher can only sense that something sounds wrong, and the feedback given to Priya will be vague at best.

Accurate feedback depends on the teacher having already diagnosed the problem. Consider a classroom exchange where the teacher has spotted the same pattern across several students' writing:

T: Priya, you've used the adjective form here, but this word is describing the verb, so we need the adverb form.
S: So I should say "beautifully"?
T: Exactly.

That exchange is possible because the teacher arrived at it with a diagnosis already formed. The metalanguage may or may not appear in what the student hears, but it shaped the teacher's thinking before the conversation began.

Lesson planning also relies on metalanguage, because syllabuses and grammar references are written in it. A teacher preparing a lesson on reported speech needs to read about backshift in tense, pronoun changes, and the behavior of reporting verbs. Those concepts are named in every grammar reference a teacher is likely to consult, from a classroom grammar to a detailed pedagogical reference. A teacher unfamiliar with the terms will find those resources difficult to use.

The same applies when a teacher reads a course syllabus. Entries such as "present perfect for recent events" or "comparative adjectives" assume the reader can map those labels onto actual language. Ms. Reyes, preparing her first intermediate class, needs to look at the entry "modal verbs of deduction" and know immediately which forms she is planning to teach and what their grammatical behavior is. Metalanguage is the shared vocabulary that connects the syllabus, the grammar reference, and the classroom; a teacher without it is working with an incomplete set of professional tools.

## Nouns, Pronouns, and Determiners

A noun is a word that names a person, place, thing, or idea. The category is broad: *teacher*, *Nairobi*, *kettle*, and *freedom* are all nouns, even though they refer to very different kinds of things. Within this category, a distinction that matters greatly in the classroom is the one between count nouns and non-count nouns. Count nouns refer to items that can be individually enumerated: a student can say *a chair* or *three chairs*, *one mistake* or *several mistakes*. Non-count nouns do not take a plural form and cannot be preceded by *a* or *an*; words such as *advice*, *furniture*, and *patience* belong here. A student named Carlos who writes *I need some furnitures for my new flat* is treating a non-count noun as a count noun, and a teacher who cannot name that error will struggle to explain the correction.

A pronoun is a word that stands in place of a noun phrase. Rather than repeating *the manager* throughout a paragraph, a writer substitutes *she* or *her*, and the meaning is preserved because the original noun phrase has already been established. Personal pronouns indicate the person and grammatical role of the noun phrase they replace: *she* replaces a singular feminine subject, while *them* replaces a plural object, as in *The students arrived late, so the director met them in the corridor*. Possessive pronouns show ownership without accompanying a noun: *hers* in *That coat is hers* stands alone, unlike a possessive determiner, which must precede a noun. Reflexive pronouns, formed with *-self* or *-selves*, refer back to the subject of the clause, as in *The director introduced herself to the new cohort*. Demonstrative pronouns point to something in context; *this* in *Hand me that form, no, not that one, this one* indicates an object close to the speaker, while *those* in *Those are the wrong files* points to items at a distance.

A determiner is a word that introduces a noun phrase and signals information about definiteness, quantity, or possession. Articles are the most familiar determiners: *a* and *an* introduce a noun phrase for the first time or refer to a non-specific item, as in *A student left a bag in the classroom*, while *the* signals that the referent is already known or uniquely identifiable, as in *The bag is still on the desk*. Quantifiers are determiners that express amount: *some*, *every*, and *few* all introduce noun phrases, as in *Every teacher received a timetable* or *Few students had completed the reading*. Possessive determiners such as *my*, *your*, and *their* introduce a noun phrase and indicate who the referent belongs to, as in *Their classroom is on the second floor*.

A point of confusion that catches many new teachers is the difference between possessive determiners and possessive pronouns. The word *my* is a determiner because it always introduces a noun phrase, as in *my dictionary*; it cannot stand alone as the subject or object of a clause. *Mine* is a pronoun. It stands alone, as in *That dictionary is mine*. A teacher who labels *my* as a pronoun in a grammar explanation, or who marks a student's grammar exercise using that label, introduces an inaccuracy that can mislead learners when they encounter possessive pronouns later. Keeping the two categories distinct from the outset prevents that confusion from taking root.

## Verbs: Main Verbs, Auxiliaries, and Basic Categories

A verb is a word that expresses an action, a state, or an occurrence. In the sentence *The student opened her notebook*, the word *opened* expresses an action. In *She knows the answer*, the word *knows* expresses a mental state. In *An argument broke out*, the phrase *broke out* expresses an occurrence, something that happened without a deliberate agent driving it. These three broad meanings cover the range of what verbs do, and a teacher who can identify all three types will be better placed to explain verb choice to learners.

Within the category of verbs, grammarians draw a primary distinction between main verbs and auxiliary verbs. A main verb carries the central meaning of the predicate; *run*, *believe*, and *contain* are main verbs because each one can serve as the sole verb in a simple clause. An auxiliary verb is a verb that supports the main verb by contributing grammatical information about tense, aspect, voice, or mood. The auxiliaries of English include *be*, *have*, *do*, *will*, *can*, *should*, and a small number of related forms.

Each auxiliary serves a specific grammatical function. The auxiliary *be* combines with a present participle to form the progressive aspect, as in *She is writing her essay*. The auxiliary *have* combines with a past participle to form the perfect aspect, as in *He has finished the task*. The auxiliary *be* also combines with a past participle to form the passive voice, as in *The report was submitted on time*. The auxiliary *do* supports negation and question formation in simple tenses, as in *Do you understand the rule?* These functions are distinct, and a learner who conflates them will struggle to produce accurate sentences.

A second distinction, one with direct consequences for classroom teaching, separates stative verbs from dynamic verbs. A stative verb describes a state that exists rather than an action that unfolds, and common examples include *know*, *prefer*, *belong*, *contain*, and *believe*. A dynamic verb describes an action or process that can be observed as it happens, and examples include *kick*, *build*, *laugh*, *write*, and *run*. The distinction is a property of meaning, not of form.

This distinction matters most when teachers introduce the present continuous form. The present continuous is used with dynamic verbs to describe actions in progress, so a sentence like *She is laughing at the joke* is grammatically natural. Stative verbs, because they describe conditions rather than unfolding events, do not normally appear in the continuous form. A teacher working with a student named Fatima might encounter the error *I am knowing the answer* and need to explain that *know* is stative and therefore takes the simple present: *I know the answer*. Without the stative/dynamic distinction in their grammatical vocabulary, teachers have no principled way to account for that correction.

T: Fatima, you wrote "I am knowing the answer." Can you tell me what *know* describes?
S: A feeling? Something in my head?
T: Exactly. It describes a state, not an action happening right now. That type of verb takes the simple present.

Identifying a verb as main or auxiliary, and as stative or dynamic, gives teachers the labels they need to explain errors clearly and consistently.

## Adjectives and Adverbs

An adjective is a word that modifies a noun or pronoun, indicating a quality, quantity, or state. Adjectives appear in two positions in English sentences. In attributive position, the adjective sits directly before the noun it modifies, as in *a dusty road* or *three nervous students*. In predicative position, the adjective follows a linking verb and describes the subject, as in *the students seemed nervous* or *the road is dusty*. Most common adjectives move freely between the two positions, though a small set, such as *asleep* or *afraid*, is restricted to predicative use only.

Adverbs form a broader and more varied class. An adverb is a word that modifies a verb, an adjective, another adverb, or an entire clause. The main semantic categories are manner, degree, frequency, and time. Manner adverbs describe how an action is performed: *the technician worked carefully* or *the child laughed loudly*. Degree adverbs indicate the intensity of an adjective or another adverb: *the exam was extremely difficult* or *she spoke barely audibly*. Frequency adverbs express how often something occurs: *Daniel always checks his work* or *the class rarely finishes early*. Time adverbs locate an event in time: *they submitted the report yesterday* or *the results will arrive soon*.

The boundary between adjectives and adverbs causes persistent difficulty for learners, particularly those whose first language does not mark the distinction morphologically. A common error is using an adjective where an adverb is required, as when a learner writes *she sings beautiful* instead of *she sings beautifully*. In that sentence, *sings* is a dynamic verb, not a linking verb, so the modifier describes the manner of the action rather than the subject herself. The modifier therefore needs to be an adverb. The adjective *beautiful* would be grammatical only in a construction such as *her voice sounds beautiful*, where *sounds* functions as a linking verb connecting the adjective back to the subject.

A teacher who understands this distinction can diagnose the error with precision, identifying its grammatical source rather than marking it wrong without explanation. The following exchange comes from a writing feedback session.

T: Carlos, you wrote "he drives careful." What is *careful* describing here?
S: The way he drives?
T: Right. It describes the action, not the person. When a word describes an action, what class does it belong to?
S: An adverb?
T: Yes. So what form do we need?
S: *Carefully.*

Without the adjective and adverb categories available as working concepts, a teacher can only tell the student that the sentence sounds wrong. With those categories in place, the teacher can explain that *careful* modifies a noun or pronoun while *carefully* modifies a verb, and that the verb *drives* requires the adverb form. That explanation gives the learner a transferable rule rather than a single corrected sentence, and it gives the teacher a consistent framework for handling the same error pattern whenever it reappears.

## Prepositions and Conjunctions

A preposition is a word that links a noun phrase to the rest of the sentence, expressing a relationship such as place, time, direction, or another logical connection. The noun phrase that follows the preposition is called its object, and together they form a prepositional phrase. In the sentence *The report is on the desk*, the preposition *on* links the noun phrase *the desk* to the rest of the clause, indicating location. In *The meeting starts at noon*, *at* expresses a point in time, and in *She walked towards the exit*, *towards* expresses direction.

Grammarians distinguish simple prepositions from complex ones. Simple prepositions are single words: *in*, *on*, *at*, *by*, *from*, *with*, and *through* are among the most common. Complex prepositions consist of two or three words functioning as a single unit: *in front of*, *next to*, *because of*, *in spite of*, and *on behalf of* all behave as single prepositions even though they contain more than one word. A learner such as Fatima might write *The car parked in front of the gate*, using the complex preposition *in front of* to locate the car precisely. Treating *in front of* as three separate words with independent grammatical roles misrepresents the way the phrase functions within the sentence.

A conjunction is a word that connects words, phrases, or clauses. The two main classes are coordinating conjunctions and subordinating conjunctions, and the distinction between them matters for both grammar teaching and error correction. Coordinating conjunctions, including *and*, *but*, *or*, and *so*, join elements of equal grammatical rank. In *Wei finished the exercise, but he checked his answers twice*, the conjunction *but* links two independent clauses, each capable of standing alone. In *She ordered coffee and a sandwich*, *and* joins two noun phrases of the same rank.

Subordinating conjunctions introduce dependent clauses, which cannot stand alone as complete sentences. Words such as *because*, *although*, *unless*, and *whenever* belong to this class. In *Although the instructions were long, the task itself was simple*, the subordinating conjunction *although* introduces a dependent clause that must attach to a main clause to form a grammatical sentence. In *Unless you confirm by Friday, your place will be given to someone else*, the dependent clause introduced by *unless* sets a condition for the main clause. The dependent clause changes position freely: it can open the sentence or follow the main clause without altering the basic meaning.

A practical difficulty arises when teachers conflate subordinating conjunctions with prepositions, particularly with words like *despite* and *although*. Both words can introduce a concessive idea, but they belong to different grammatical categories and take different structures. *Despite* is a preposition and must be followed by a noun phrase: *Despite the delay, the flight landed safely*. *Although* is a subordinating conjunction and must be followed by a full clause containing a subject and a verb: *Although the flight was delayed, it landed safely*. A teacher who treats these two words as interchangeable in structure will be unable to explain why a student's sentence *Despite the flight was delayed* is ungrammatical. Recognizing that *despite* requires a noun phrase object while *although* requires a clause gives the teacher a precise and transferable explanation, one that applies consistently every time a student reaches for the wrong word.

## Putting the Parts of Speech to Work in the Classroom

A teacher who can label every word in a model sentence is equipped to plan grammar lessons with precision. Consider the sentence *The elderly woman placed her keys on the table.* A full analysis runs as follows: *The* is a definite article functioning as a determiner, *elderly* is an adjective modifying *woman*, *woman* is a common noun serving as the subject, *placed* is a past-tense transitive verb, *her* is a possessive determiner, *keys* is a common noun serving as the direct object, *on* is a preposition, *the* is again a definite article, and *table* is a common noun serving as the object of the prepositional phrase. A teacher who has worked through that analysis before the lesson knows which element to foreground and which to leave aside.

Writing a grammar-focused learning objective also depends on this kind of fluency. An objective such as "Students will be able to use adverbs of manner to modify action verbs in written sentences" is precise because the teacher knows what an adverb of manner is, what a verb is, and how the two relate syntactically. A vague objective like "Students will practice describing actions" gives neither the teacher nor the student a clear grammatical target.

Corrective feedback becomes more useful when it is specific. Suppose a student named Fatima writes: *She go to the market every Saturday.* The teacher can identify *go* as a present-tense verb that fails to agree with its third-person singular subject *she*, and can respond accordingly.

T: Fatima, what part of speech is *go* here?
S: A verb?
T: Exactly. And what is the subject of that verb?
S: *She.*
T: *She* is third-person singular, so the verb needs an *s* ending in the present simple. What would the correct form be?
S: *Goes.*

That exchange is possible because the teacher arrived with the metalanguage already in hand.

Grammatical terminology is a professional tool for the teacher rather than a required teaching tool for the student. A student may never need to say the word "adverb" to use adverbs correctly. The teacher who cannot identify one, however, cannot plan for it, write an objective around it, or give feedback on it with any consistency. Fluency with the parts of speech underpins every grammar-focused decision a teacher makes, from selecting a coursebook exercise to responding to an error in the moment.
