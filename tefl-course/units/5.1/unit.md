# How Vocabulary Is Learned: Retrieval Beats Re-Reading

## The Problem with Re-Reading: Why Familiar Feels Like Learned

When teachers ask learners how they prefer to study new words, the answers cluster around a familiar set of activities: drawing pictures of the words, looking up definitions in a dictionary, listening to a recording of the pronunciation, writing the words repeatedly in a notebook. These activities feel productive because the learner is engaged and the material seems to flow easily. That feeling of ease is precisely the problem.

Psychologists call this sensation fluency, meaning the subjective sense that processing is going smoothly. When a learner sees the word "precipice" paired with its translation for the third time, recognition is fast and effortless, and the brain interprets that speed as mastery. The word has not been stored more durably; it has become a familiar visual pattern and nothing more. That distinction carries significant consequences for classroom design.

Karpicke and Roediger (2008) tested this directly with Swahili-English vocabulary pairs. Once a learner had recalled a word correctly once, continued studying of that same pair produced no measurable improvement in delayed recall a week later. The group that kept re-studying reached roughly the same retention level as chance, while the group that kept retrieving the words scored far higher (Karpicke & Roediger, 2008). Repeated studying, in their design, meant re-exposing learners to the word alongside its translation, which is what happens when a student reads through a vocabulary list or a teacher displays a word wall.

Consider a student named Yuna, who spends twenty minutes before a test reading her vocabulary notebook from top to bottom. Each time she reaches a word, she sees the translation printed beside it. The pair confirms itself instantly, she moves on, and the whole list feels secure. Her classmate Omar closes his notebook after the first read-through and instead covers the translation column, forces himself to recall each meaning, and checks only when he cannot retrieve it. Their study time is identical; their outcomes, according to Karpicke and Roediger (2008), will not be.

The source materials used in this course list a wide range of vocabulary activities that learners and teachers find appealing: making poems, labelling diagrams, listening to a computer say the words, writing words in the air or in sand. Engagement matters. Many of these activities carry genuine motivational value, and that value should not be dismissed. The organising question, however, is whether an activity requires the learner to produce the word from memory or whether it re-presents the word to the senses without demanding any retrieval at all. The distinction between retrieval and re-presentation will structure every practical recommendation that follows.

## What the Research Shows: Retrieval and the Testing Effect

The most direct evidence for the value of retrieval in vocabulary learning comes from a controlled experiment by Karpicke and Roediger (2008), published in the journal Science. They gave participants Swahili-English word pairs to learn, then assigned them to different study conditions across several sessions. One condition involved repeated retrieval practice, requiring participants to produce the English translation from memory without seeing it. Another condition involved repeated studying of the pairs without any retrieval demand.

Retrieval, as used in that experiment, has a precise operational meaning. The learner sees the Swahili cue and must generate the target word independently before any feedback appears. No part of the answer is visible during the attempt. Reading a word list, hearing a word pronounced, or matching a word to a picture each place the correct form in the learner's environment, so memory is not required to produce it; retrieval practice removes that support entirely, forcing the learner's memory to do the generative work alone.

The results were measured one week after the final learning session, a delay that reflects the kind of retention gap a classroom teacher cares about, given that students encounter vocabulary again days or weeks after a lesson rather than moments after it. At that one-week point, participants in the repeated-retrieval condition recalled approximately 80% of the word pairs correctly, while participants in the repeated-studying condition recalled approximately 33% (Karpicke & Roediger, 2008). A gap of that magnitude would be visible in any classroom assessment.

A further finding sharpens the practical implication. Once a learner had recalled a word correctly for the first time, additional studying of that same pair produced no measurable benefit to delayed recall (Karpicke & Roediger, 2008). Consider a student named Amara who correctly retrieves the word "harvest" during a classroom activity on Monday. Spending further lesson time showing Amara the word on a card, or having her copy it into a notebook, adds nothing to what she will remember on Friday. The cognitive work that matters has already been done, and the teacher's time is better directed toward words Amara has not yet successfully retrieved.

## Retrieval in the Classroom: What Counts as Testing?

Retrieval practice, defined plainly, is any activity that requires a learner to produce or identify a target word from memory rather than encounter it again on the page. A student who reads a glossary entry is receiving the word. A student who must supply the word without seeing it is retrieving it, and the cognitive difference between those two acts is substantial once a teacher knows what to look for.

The project checklist criteria from a standard vocabulary presentation task illustrate this boundary well. One criterion asks whether students can pronounce the new vocabulary words clearly when presenting; another asks whether they used five or more target words in their project (shaping_frm_observ_508). Both demands require a learner to reach into memory and produce language, not to scan a list and recognise familiar items. Consider a student named Tomás who must stand before the class and say the word "irrigation" while explaining a poster he has made. Tomás cannot borrow the pronunciation from a printed page in that moment; he must have stored it well enough to produce it independently.

Compare that with activities that are primarily re-presentation. A teacher who asks students to scan the next chapter for key words, or who displays a word list on the board while students copy definitions, is placing the target vocabulary in front of learners rather than drawing it out of them. These activities have a role in initial exposure, but they do not constitute retrieval practice and should not be mistaken for it.

The practical question a teacher can ask about any vocabulary activity is direct: does this task require the learner to pull the word from memory, or does it put the word in front of them? A chain story in which each group member must continue a narrative using a target word from a closed list demands genuine retrieval; re-reading that same list before starting the story does not. A student named Priya who must produce the word "erosion" at the right moment in a spoken skit is doing something cognitively different from Priya reading "erosion" in a caption beneath a photograph. The distinction matters because only one of those two acts generates the memory benefit.

Recognising this boundary allows a teacher to audit lesson activities with precision. Many tasks that feel vocabulary-rich, because target words appear frequently on worksheets or slides, are re-presentation activities dressed in productive clothing. The retrieval demand is what generates the memory benefit documented by Karpicke and Roediger (2008), and a task earns that label only when the word must come from the learner rather than from the materials in front of them.

## Recycling Vocabulary Through Retrieval: Practical Activity Structures

The chain story format described in *Create to Communicate* illustrates how a familiar collaborative activity can be structured to impose genuine retrieval demand. Students compile a list of vocabulary words on the board, then break into groups and construct a spoken story in which every word on the list must appear. The critical moment arrives once the story is underway: the next speaker must produce the required word from memory, because the narrative momentum of the group does not pause for scanning. A student named Dara, waiting for her turn, is not re-reading "sediment" on the board; she is holding it in mind and searching for a sentence that will carry the story forward. That internal search is retrieval, and it is precisely the kind of repeated, successful retrieval that produced the large delayed-recall advantage documented by Karpicke and Roediger (2008).

The vocabulary game and skit options from the project checklist in *Shaping the Way We Teach English* function similarly, provided the teacher attends to one design detail. When a group of students performs a skit, the target words must be spoken aloud in context, which means each speaker has to produce the word at the moment of performance rather than read it from a script held in hand. A teacher can make this condition explicit in the task instructions: scripts may be used during rehearsal, but the final performance requires the words to come from the students themselves. The same logic applies to a vocabulary game a group has designed; playing the game they created requires them to recall definitions and forms on demand, without the original word list as a crutch.

The vocabulary word poem closing activity from the same source sits in a different category. Students select a word, write it vertically down the page, and then compose a line for each letter. Because the target word remains visible in its vertical form throughout the writing process, the student is working with a permanent prompt rather than retrieving from memory. This is a re-presentation condition. The activity has genuine value for consolidating spelling and for encouraging students to think about meaning, but it does not generate the retrieval demand that Karpicke and Roediger (2008) identified as the source of durable retention. A teacher who wants to shift the poem activity toward retrieval could ask students to choose their word, close their notebooks, and write the vertical letters from memory before composing the lines.

Auditing a lesson in these terms does not require replacing activities wholesale. It requires asking, at each stage, whether the target word is visible to the learner or must come from the learner. Consider a short exchange during a chain story session:

T: Yusuf, it's your turn. The next word is "accumulate."
S: Um... the river began to accumulate sediment near the bend, and that's when things got strange.
T: Good. Fatou, carry it on.

Yusuf produced "accumulate" under mild time pressure, embedded it in a meaningful sentence, and passed the story on. That sequence, repeated across a lesson for several target words, approximates the repeated retrieval condition that separated the high-retention group from the low-retention group in Karpicke and Roediger's (2008) study. The activity format is not unusual or technically demanding to organise; what makes it effective is the structural decision to remove the word list once the story begins.

## Word Bricks and Ongoing Retrieval: Building a Recycling System

The Word Bricks collection described in the *Activate: Games for Learning American English* teacher's manual offers a concrete model of how a classroom can build retrieval practice into its ordinary routines rather than treating it as a separate event. The collection begins with 280 words printed on double-sided cards, but it is designed to grow continuously. When a new textbook chapter begins, the teacher adds the chapter's key vocabulary to the collection. Students contribute words relevant to their own lives, scanning upcoming chapters or nominating terms from outside the classroom. Function words, including prepositions, modal verbs, and clause markers, are added in sufficient quantity to allow students to construct complete sentences from the bricks rather than work with isolated nouns and verbs.

That cumulative structure has a direct consequence for retrieval. A word added to the collection in the third week of term does not disappear once the class moves on; it remains in the physical set and reappears in every subsequent game. When the teacher calls out a word category and starts a two-minute timer, students must search through the collection and identify matching bricks before time runs out. The search is not passive. A student such as Amara, working through a pile of bricks looking for words that fit the category "things that change over time," must hold the category in mind, evaluate each word against it, and make a rapid decision. That process is retrieval in the technical sense: the active reconstruction of a stored association between a word and its meaning, prompted by a semantic cue rather than the word's printed form.

Karpicke and Roediger (2008) demonstrated that repeated retrieval produced far stronger delayed recall of foreign-language vocabulary than repeated study of the same material, with participants who practised retrieval recalling approximately 80 percent of target words after one week compared with approximately 33 percent in the repeated-study condition. The category-search game replicates the functional logic of that retrieval condition. Students are not re-reading a vocabulary list; they are generating a response to a prompt, which is precisely the operation that the research identifies as the driver of long-term retention.

The ongoing, cumulative nature of the collection extends this benefit across time in a way that a single-lesson activity cannot. Consider a word like "valley," added to the collection early in the course. Each time a category game is played, that word is physically present in the set. If the category called is "geographical features," a student must retrieve the word's meaning to decide whether it qualifies. If the category is "nouns," the decision is grammatical rather than semantic, but the word is still handled and evaluated. Neither encounter is a re-reading of a definition; both require the student to do something with the word. Karpicke and Roediger (2008) found that additional study after a first correct recall added nothing to retention, while additional retrieval continued to strengthen it. A collection that keeps old words in circulation alongside new ones creates the conditions for that additional retrieval to occur naturally, distributed across weeks rather than concentrated in a single revision session.

## Planning for Retrieval: A Framework for the Vocabulary Lesson

A teacher planning a vocabulary lesson can organise the retrieval logic around three questions, each answered before the lesson begins. The first is straightforward: at what point will learners encounter the target words for the first time? That initial encounter, whether through a teacher presentation, a reading text, or a listening task, is the study phase. Its purpose is to give learners enough information about a word, its form, meaning, and pronunciation, to attempt retrieval later. The encounter should be clear and deliberate, but it need not be long.

The second question is more consequential: how soon after that first encounter will learners be required to produce the words from memory, without seeing them again? Karpicke and Roediger (2008) demonstrated that once a learner has recalled a word correctly once, further study of that word produces no measurable gain in retention at one week, while further retrieval continues to strengthen it. A teacher who spends twenty minutes presenting vocabulary and five minutes on practice has the ratio backwards. The planning decision is to compress initial presentation and schedule multiple short retrieval events across the remaining lesson time.

The third question concerns format. A retrieval event, defined here as any activity that requires a learner to produce a target word from memory rather than recognise it on the page, can take many shapes. A timed Word Brick category search, in which a student like Amara must locate and name bricks fitting a category before the timer stops, forces rapid lexical access under mild pressure. A chain story, where Tomás must continue a narrative using a word the previous speaker has not yet used, requires him to hold the full target set in mind and select from it in real time. A skit requires learners to embed words in spontaneous dialogue, as when a pair playing customer and shopkeeper must work five vocabulary items into a scene they have rehearsed only briefly.

T: You have two minutes. Your category is "things you find in a kitchen." Go.
S: Stove. Refrigerator. Oh, and "utensil"!

Each of these formats places a genuine retrieval demand on the learner. None of them involves re-reading a definition or scanning a word list. The source material's project options, including poems, vocabulary games, and group presentations with a pronunciation requirement, serve the same function when they are structured so that learners must recall words rather than copy them from a visible list.

A completed lesson plan built on this framework looks different from a conventional one. The word list appears early and briefly. The bulk of the time is divided among two or three short activities, each separated by other lesson content so that some forgetting, and therefore some effortful retrieval, can occur between them. A student like Priya who plays a word game at the fifteen-minute mark, contributes to a chain story at the thirty-minute mark, and answers audience questions about her group's presentation at the fifty-minute mark has retrieved the same words three times under different conditions. That distribution is what the research supports (Karpicke & Roediger, 2008).

The teacher's central task is to construct conditions in which learners must reach into memory and produce target words themselves, repeatedly, across a single lesson and across the weeks that follow. Exposure matters far less than that effortful production. Frequency of encounter, without retrieval demand attached, leaves retention largely unchanged.
