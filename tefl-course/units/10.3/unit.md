# Planning a Lesson, Start to Finish

## Choosing a Teaching Point and Writing the Aim

A lesson plan without a clear teaching point is a schedule of activities rather than a coherent plan. The teaching point is the single piece of language, skill, or content that students will work with during the lesson. Selecting one concrete teaching point before writing anything else gives every subsequent decision, from the choice of materials to the design of tasks, a fixed reference to return to. This unit walks through that sequence of decisions in order, from the first choice to the finished document, and ends with a complete worked plan for a single hour of teaching.

The module's Lesson Plan Template asks teachers to specify lesson objectives and skill or content focus areas. These two fields are related but distinct. The skill or content focus area names the broad territory, such as reading or vocabulary, while the lesson objective states precisely what students will do with that content by the end of the class. A teacher who writes only "vocabulary" in the objective field has identified a territory without drawing a map. Suppose Ms. Reyes, planning a lesson for an intermediate secondary class, notes "do vocabulary" as her aim. That phrase tells her nothing about which words, what level of knowledge is expected, or how students will demonstrate understanding, and it cannot guide her when she sits down to sequence activities or decide how long each stage should run.

Bloom's Taxonomy offers a practical tool for sharpening that aim. The source describes the taxonomy as providing verbs that correspond to different categories of cognitive demand, and states that teachers can use those verbs when writing lesson objectives. The categories move from lower-order operations such as remembering and understanding toward higher-order ones such as analyzing and creating. Ms. Reyes can ask herself what she wants her students to do with the new words. Recognition calls for the verb "identify." Production calls for "use" or "produce." Her revised objective might then read: "Students will be able to use six adjectives describing personality in original sentences about a character from the story." That sentence names the words, the cognitive demand, and the context in a single clause.

A focused, verb-driven objective does more than satisfy a template. Because Ms. Reyes's objective specifies production, she knows she needs a stage where students generate language, not only a stage where they encounter it. A matching exercise alone will not be sufficient. When she selects materials, she will look for a text that contains the target adjectives in context; when she plans her timing, she will protect enough minutes for the production stage, since that is where the objective will be met or missed. A brief exchange at the end of the lesson, such as T: "Can you describe the main character using one of today's words?" S: "He is ambitious because he never gives up," gives Ms. Reyes observable evidence that the objective was reached.

The teaching point also sets a natural boundary. A lesson that aims to cover personality adjectives, comparative structures, and paragraph organization is three lessons compressed into one. Selecting a single teaching point is an act of discipline that makes the plan teachable and the objective assessable.

## Mapping the Skill Areas and Integrating Language Skills

The lesson plan template asks teachers to name the skill or content focus areas for a session, and that field does more than label the lesson. It prompts a decision about which language skills the lesson will serve and how those skills will relate to one another. Integrated language skills recur across the course materials, named as a central focus in the Unit 10 video and raised again in the Unit 9 action plan, which asks teachers to consider how they might integrate reading, writing, speaking, listening, grammar, and vocabulary into a single coherent lesson. Filling in the skill focus field is the moment when that consideration becomes concrete.

Integration does not mean treating all skills equally within one lesson. A teacher working with a class of intermediate teenagers on the song "Pretty Boy," as in the Unit 10 context, might decide that listening is the primary skill because students encounter the vocabulary and grammar through the audio first. Speaking then becomes a secondary skill when students discuss the lyrics in pairs, and writing enters as a tertiary skill when small groups produce a short project at the end. The hierarchy matters. When a teacher like Mr. Osei sits down to plan, naming listening as primary tells him that the bulk of his task design should support comprehension, and that the speaking and writing activities should grow out of what students heard rather than run parallel to it.

Without that early decision, the lesson tends to drift. A teacher might design a reading task, then add a speaking activity because the topic seems suitable, then attach a grammar exercise because the text contains a useful structure. Each piece may be sound on its own, yet students experience the lesson as a series of unrelated stops. The skill focus field in the template prevents that outcome by asking the teacher to commit to a logic before any individual activity is chosen.

The secondary skills still require deliberate planning. If Ms. Reyes plans a lesson with reading as the primary skill and speaking as secondary, she needs to design the speaking task so that it genuinely requires students to use what they read. A question such as T: "Based on the article, what would you recommend to someone in that situation?" S: "I think they should ask for help because the article says asking is a sign of strength" shows that the speaking activity is drawing on the reading rather than replacing it. That connection is visible in the plan only if the skill relationship was mapped before the activities were written. Deciding which skills are primary and which are secondary is therefore not a formality; it is the structural decision that keeps every subsequent activity pointing in the same direction.

## Sequencing Activities: From Warm-Up to Main Task

Lesson sequencing, as named in the Lesson Plan Template, refers to the deliberate ordering of activities within a class session so that each stage prepares students for the one that follows. Each activity creates the conditions that make the next activity possible. The decisions made at each transition point shape whether students arrive at the main task with enough knowledge, vocabulary, and confidence to succeed.

The warm-up is the first of those transition points, and its purpose is to activate what students already know about the topic or language focus. A K-W-L Chart, described in the source materials as a warm-up activity with a reflection follow-up component, gives this activation a visible structure. Students record what they know, what they want to know, and, later, what they have learned. A teacher working with a class on environmental vocabulary might distribute the chart and ask students to fill in the first two columns before any new material appears. When a student named Fatima writes "I know that deforestation is bad" in the first column and "I want to know why companies still do it" in the second, she has already connected her existing knowledge to the lesson's direction. The teacher can glance at those responses and calibrate how much background explanation the main task will require.

Not every warm-up needs to be reflective in that structured way. The module describes a Mad Lib activity as a fun vocabulary or grammar activity completed in pairs or small groups, and this kind of task serves a different but equally purposeful function. A student named Wei, working with a partner to fill in missing words in a short comic passage, is practicing word categories and collocations without the pressure of producing original sentences. The activity is engaging, but it is also doing preparatory work: students are handling the target language in a low-stakes context before they encounter it in the main task. The teacher's choice between a reflective warm-up like the K-W-L Chart and an interactive one like a Mad Lib depends on what the main task demands. If the main task requires students to read and analyze a text, activating prior knowledge through the chart makes sense. If the main task requires students to write or speak using specific vocabulary, a production-oriented warm-up like the Mad Lib builds the necessary fluency first.

The transition from warm-up to main task is the moment where sequencing decisions become most consequential. A teacher who ends the warm-up abruptly and moves immediately to a complex writing task leaves a gap that students must cross on their own. Closing that gap deliberately is the work of sequencing. If the class used the K-W-L Chart, the teacher might ask two or three students to share what they wrote in the "want to know" column before introducing the main reading. When a student named Priya responds to T: "What do you want to find out?" with S: "I want to know if there are any solutions that work," the teacher can respond by pointing to the section of the main text where that question is addressed. The warm-up has now created a reason to read.

After the main task, the sequencing question shifts again. The K-W-L Chart's third column, where students record what they have learned, functions as a built-in reflection stage that closes the lesson's arc. Students return to their own earlier questions and measure what the lesson provided. That return is not decorative; it gives students a concrete record of their own progress through the session and signals to the teacher which questions the lesson answered and which it left open. Planning that reflection stage before the lesson begins, rather than adding it if time permits, is what keeps the sequence intact from the first minute to the last.

## Timing, Flexibility, and Staying on Task

A lesson plan that assigns rigid minute-by-minute slots to every activity treats time as though students will behave like a clock. In practice, a productive discussion can run longer than anticipated, and cutting it short because the plan says so wastes the learning already happening. The module describes this situation directly: a teacher chooses to let a discussion continue during the oral retelling of a story, and the follow-up question posed to teachers asks how that choice affects the rest of the lesson plan. The question matters because extending one activity does not make the extra time disappear; it borrows from somewhere else in the sequence.

The practical answer is to build the plan around flexible time blocks rather than fixed minutes. A teacher might note that the vocabulary pre-teaching stage needs roughly ten minutes, while the main task needs between fifteen and twenty-five, depending on how students engage. When Fatima's group in a reading lesson generates an unexpectedly rich debate about a character's motives, the teacher can let it run to twenty minutes without panic, because the plan already identified which later activity could be shortened or moved to the following session. That kind of planning requires the teacher to rank activities by their importance to the lesson's objectives before the class begins, not during it.

Timing decisions and classroom management decisions are connected more tightly than they first appear. Off-task behavior, students standing when they should be sitting, not following directions, or talking out of turn, creates moments where the teacher must act without disrupting the lesson's flow. A teacher already behind schedule because an earlier activity ran long will feel pressure to stop and address the behavior at length, costing still more time. A teacher with a time buffer built into the plan can redirect a student like Carlos, who has started a side conversation, with a quiet word or a directed question rather than a full pause.

T: "Carlos, what did your group decide about the ending?"
S: "Oh, um, we thought the character made the wrong choice."
T: "Good. Tell us why."

That exchange pulls Carlos back into the task, gives him a genuine role in the discussion, and costs the class fewer than thirty seconds. The technique works because the teacher is not simultaneously managing a time crisis.

Flexibility and looseness are not the same thing. A flexible plan has clear objectives, a ranked sequence of activities, and identified points where time can be compressed or expanded. A loose plan has no structure to return to when something goes wrong. When a teacher decides to extend a discussion, that decision should be conscious, with a clear sense of what it costs and what it gains, rather than a drift that leaves the lesson without a closing stage. The reflection activity, the wrap-up, the final check for understanding: these are the first casualties of poor time management, and they are also the stages that tell the teacher whether the lesson's objectives were met.

## Wrapping Up and Checking Objectives

The closing stage of a lesson deserves the same deliberate planning as the opening. A wrap-up is a designed activity that serves a specific purpose, and the choice of that activity should follow directly from the lesson's stated objectives. When a teacher writes a lesson plan, the closing stage should already be named, timed, and connected to what students were meant to learn.

Movement is one tool worth considering at this stage, particularly with younger learners. The module asks explicitly why a teacher might include movement in the final minutes of a lesson, and the question itself signals that the choice is pedagogical rather than incidental. A teacher who has spent forty minutes on a reading task might close with a brief stand-and-share activity, asking students to move to a partner they have not worked with and retell one thing they understood. The physical transition signals a shift in cognitive mode and gives the teacher a final sweep of the room.

That sweep is the informal assessment. Informal assessment means the teacher gathers evidence of learning without a test or a grade, through observation, targeted questions, or a short production task. Suppose the lesson objective was for students to use reported speech to summarize a short text. In the closing stage, the teacher, call her Ms. Reyes, circulates while students share their summaries and listens for whether they are producing the target structure. She hears one student, Daniel, say "He said that he was tired," and another, Yuki, say "He said he is tired," and she notes the difference without interrupting either student. That observation tells her directly whether the objective was met and what needs revisiting in the next lesson.

The connection between the wrap-up activity and the lesson's objectives should be visible in the written plan. A teacher who writes "free conversation" as the closing activity has planned a gap-filler, not a closing stage. Instead, Ms. Reyes writes: "Students summarize the text to a new partner using reported speech; teacher circulates and notes accuracy of tense backshift." The activity produces evidence. That evidence answers the question the lesson was built around, so the lesson ends with information rather than with time running out.

## A Worked Example: One Lesson from Start to Finish

The decisions this unit has walked through are easier to hold together when they appear in a single document, so here is the compact plan Ms. Reyes prepares for a sixty-minute lesson with twelve intermediate adult learners, a group that includes Carlos, Daniel, Wei, Fatima, and Priya. The teaching point is reported speech. The class read a short celebrity interview in the previous session, which means the text is familiar and the grammar is the new element.

**Aims.** By the end of the lesson, learners will be able to convert direct speech to reported speech with accurate tense backshift, and to use reported speech to summarize the interview aloud to a partner. Both statements name observable performances, so the closing stage can check them directly.

**Skill and content focus.** Grammar is primary: reported speech with tense backshift. Speaking is secondary and carries the production stages. Reading appears only briefly, as the vehicle for the interview text.

**Materials.** Copies of the six-line interview from the previous lesson, twelve direct-speech sentence cards, and the board. Nothing needs photocopying on the day if the interview copies survived the last session; Ms. Reyes notes "bring six spares" anyway.

| Time | Stage | Procedure | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| :00 | Warm-up (5 min) | Students tell a partner one thing the interviewee said, quoting it any way they can. | Reactivates the text and shows how students handle quoted speech before any instruction. |
| :05 | Presentation (10 min) | Ms. Reyes writes two of the students' quoted lines on the board and converts them to reported speech, eliciting each tense change. | Builds the backshift rule out of language the students themselves produced. |
| :15 | Controlled practice (15 min) | Pairs convert the twelve sentence cards; whole-class check of six selected answers. | Accuracy work while the rule is fresh; errors surface where they are cheap to fix. |
| :30 | Freer practice (20 min) | Each student summarizes the interview to a new partner using reported speech; the listener notes one reported statement. | Moves the structure into communicative use; deliberately the longest block. |
| :50 | Wrap-up and check (10 min) | Students report to the class one thing their partner told them; Ms. Reyes circulates and notes backshift accuracy. | Informal assessment against both aims; the lesson ends with evidence. |

The timings add up to the sixty minutes available, and the largest block sits under the freer practice, which is also the stage Ms. Reyes has marked as compressible if an earlier stage overruns.

**Anticipated problems.** Some students will shift pronouns but not tenses, producing "He said he is tired"; the presentation stage therefore keeps both converted examples on the board for the rest of the lesson as a silent reference. Fast pairs will finish the sentence cards early, so three extra cards wait in reserve. If the freer practice generates the kind of rich exchange worth extending, the wrap-up compresses to five minutes of reports rather than disappearing, because it is the stage that tells Ms. Reyes whether the aims were met.

Every line of this plan traces back to a section of this unit. The aims use the observable formulation and were written before any activity was chosen. The skill hierarchy was settled early, so the speaking stages grow out of the grammar rather than running beside it. Each stage prepares the next, the time column marks elapsed minutes rather than clock times, and the closing stage doubles as the assessment. The whole document fits on one page, which is also part of the design: a plan the teacher can read at a glance mid-lesson is a plan that will be used.

## Reviewing, Sharing, and Revising the Plan

A completed lesson plan is a working draft, not a finished document. The action-plan cycle described in *Shaping the Way We Teach English* makes this explicit: after writing the plan, teachers are directed to share it with colleagues, collect feedback, and revise before entering the classroom. That sequence treats the written plan the way a careful writer treats a first draft, as something that improves through outside eyes.

Sharing the plan with a colleague serves a specific purpose. A teacher who has spent time writing objectives, sequencing activities, and designing a closing stage is too close to the document to see its gaps. A colleague reading the plan for the first time will notice what is missing or unclear: the instruction that assumes prior knowledge the students do not have, the timing that leaves no room for a difficult transition, the objective that the closing activity fails to address. Before teaching a reading lesson on reported speech, Mr. Osei shows his plan to a colleague; she points out that his checking-for-understanding questions all require yes or no answers, which will not reveal whether students have grasped tense backshift. He revises the questions before the lesson begins.

The reflective loop does not end when the lesson ends. The same action-plan cycle asks teachers to try the plan in class, then reflect on what went well and what they might do differently. Reflection here is substantive work. A teacher who notices mid-lesson that students are confused by an instruction, or that an activity runs ten minutes short, has gathered information that belongs in a revised version of the plan. Writing those observations down immediately after class, while the details are still precise, makes the next version of the plan more accurate than the first.

The student-facing materials in the module model the same habit. The Discussion Leader Self-Assessment Worksheet asks student discussion leaders to evaluate their own performance; the Peer Assessment Worksheet asks classmates to evaluate the same performance from the outside. The self-editing and peer-editing station instructions organize writing feedback so that students first assess their own drafts and then receive structured responses from peers. In each case, the underlying structure is identical: produce something, examine it yourself, receive an outside perspective, and revise. A teacher who builds that cycle into student activities is already familiar with its logic and can apply it to the lesson plan without difficulty.

The parallel deserves careful attention. When Ms. Reyes designs a peer-editing station, she is telling her students that a first draft is the beginning of a writing process, not its conclusion. The same principle applies to the document she wrote the night before. A plan that has been shared, questioned, taught, and reflected upon is a more reliable guide to the classroom than one that moved directly from the teacher's notebook to the lesson. The habit of review, built carefully into the student's experience of learning, belongs just as deliberately in the teacher's experience of planning.
