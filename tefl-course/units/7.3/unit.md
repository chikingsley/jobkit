# Extensive Reading: The Evidence

## What Is Extensive Reading?

Extensive reading means reading large quantities of texts that are easy enough to be understood without a dictionary, chosen by the student, and read primarily for enjoyment and overall meaning. The term distinguishes this activity from intensive reading, in which a teacher selects a short, often difficult passage and guides students through it line by line, focusing on grammar, vocabulary, and comprehension questions. A learner doing extensive reading moves through whole books or articles at a comfortable pace; a learner doing intensive reading works slowly through a paragraph under close direction.

The Reading Station described in classroom observation materials offers a useful picture of what extensive reading looks like in a physical space. Students read independently or with a partner, seated at ease, with a variety of books available and a calm atmosphere maintained around them (Shaping the Way We Teach English, americanenglish.state.gov). A teacher walking past might see a student named Mia absorbed in a graded reader she picked up herself, while her classmate Omar pages through a simpler picture-based text at his own speed. Neither student is being tested or corrected; both are reading. The activity is defined by that absence of external pressure.

Student choice is not incidental to extensive reading; it is one of its defining conditions. When students select their own texts, they engage with material that interests them, which sustains the reading habit over time. The principle is stated plainly in classroom guidance on learning stations: students are allowed to choose so that they can enjoy their learning experience and take responsibility for their own learning (Shaping the Way We Teach English, americanenglish.state.gov). A teacher who hands every student the same graded reader, however appropriate the level, has already moved away from the model.

The research record on whether extensive reading improves language proficiency draws primarily on two large meta-analyses. Nakanishi (2015), published in TESOL Quarterly, synthesised 34 studies and found effect sizes of d = 0.46 for group comparisons and d = 0.71 for pre-post designs. Jeon and Day (2016), published in Reading in a Foreign Language, synthesised 49 studies and reported d = 0.57 for experimental-versus-control comparisons and d = 0.79 for pre-post designs. The section that follows works through those findings carefully before turning to practical guidance on setting up an extensive reading programme in your own classroom.

## The Research Base: Nakanishi (2015)

A meta-analysis is a study that pools the results of many individual experiments and calculates a single summary statistic, allowing researchers to draw conclusions that no single classroom study could support alone. Nakanishi (2015) gathered 34 studies on extensive reading in foreign language contexts and subjected them to this procedure, publishing the results in TESOL Quarterly. The combined sample gave the field its first large-scale quantitative picture of what extensive reading does to reading proficiency.

To read the findings, you need to understand what an effect size is. An effect size expresses how large a difference is in standardised terms, so results from different studies can be compared on one scale: a larger number means a bigger gap between learners who took part in a programme and those who did not. The figures reported for extensive reading below are all positive, and in education research they sit in the range usually described as a meaningful benefit rather than a marginal one.

Nakanishi (2015) reports two distinct figures, and they must be read separately. When studies compared an extensive reading group against a control group that did not receive the same treatment, the effect size was d = 0.46, a moderate effect. This between-group comparison is the more conservative and informative of the two estimates, because the control group absorbs the background gains that any student accumulates through continued instruction and exposure to English over time, gains that have nothing to do with extensive reading in particular.

The second figure, d = 0.71, comes from pre-post comparisons within a single group of extensive reading students, measuring how much those students improved from the start to the end of a programme. This number approaches what researchers classify as a large effect, and it is genuinely encouraging. It is also higher partly because no control group is present to absorb those background gains, so the figure cannot isolate the contribution of extensive reading alone.

Consider a trainee teacher named Marcus, preparing to justify an extensive reading component to a sceptical department head. The pre-post figure of d = 0.71 (Nakanishi, 2015) tells him that students in these programmes improved substantially over time. The between-group figure of d = 0.46 (Nakanishi, 2015) tells him that a meaningful portion of that improvement can be attributed to extensive reading specifically, over and above what comparison students achieved. Both numbers belong in his argument, but the between-group figure carries the stronger evidential weight.

## The Research Base: Jeon and Day (2016)

Jeon and Day (2016) conducted a second independent meta-analysis, drawing on 49 studies published in Reading in a Foreign Language, and their findings reinforce the picture that Nakanishi's work established. The larger pool of studies matters: where Nakanishi (2015) synthesised 34 investigations, Jeon and Day (2016) incorporated 49, and the broader sample reduces the chance that the overall effect reflects an unusual cluster of results from a narrow range of contexts. When a pattern holds across a greater number of independent studies, conducted by different researchers with different learner populations, confidence in that pattern increases.

The pre-post effect size in Jeon and Day (2016) was d = 0.79, meaning that students in extensive reading programmes showed substantial gains from the beginning to the end of the treatment period. That figure is notably larger than the pre-post d = 0.71 reported by Nakanishi (2015), though both sit in a range that most applied linguists would describe as a meaningful instructional effect. Marcus, the trainee teacher from the previous section, would find this convergence useful: two separate research teams, working from different sets of studies, arrived at pre-post figures that point in the same direction.

The between-group comparison in Jeon and Day (2016) produced an effect size of d = 0.57. This figure represents the advantage that extensive reading students held over comparison students who received other forms of instruction, and it is the more conservative and evidentially demanding of the two statistics. It exceeds Nakanishi's comparable between-group figure of d = 0.46 (Nakanishi, 2015), and the consistency across the two meta-analyses is significant. A trainee observing only one study might reasonably wonder whether its findings were an artefact of the particular studies selected; the fact that a second, larger synthesis produces a between-group effect in the same moderate-to-strong range makes that explanation less plausible.

Both meta-analyses measure reading proficiency broadly, aggregating across tests of reading speed, comprehension, and vocabulary-based reading ability. Neither Nakanishi (2015) nor Jeon and Day (2016) isolates a single sub-skill, so a teacher hoping to demonstrate gains on one specific measure, such as a timed reading fluency test, cannot read these figures as a direct prediction of that outcome. Consider a student named Priya, who reads graded readers for twelve weeks and then sits a standardised reading comprehension exam. The meta-analytic evidence suggests she is more likely to improve than a peer who did not participate in extensive reading, but the effect sizes describe averages across many programmes, not a guarantee for any individual classroom. Trainees should carry both the confidence and the caution that this distinction requires.

## An Honest Look at the Numbers: Hamada's Caveat

The two meta-analyses reviewed in the previous section present a persuasive case, and it is tempting to walk into a staffroom meeting and announce that extensive reading reliably produces large gains. Hamada (2020) provides a necessary check on that enthusiasm. The issue he addresses is that classrooms which adopt extensive reading programmes are rarely chosen at random. A teacher named Ms Okonkwo who introduces a graded-reader library is probably already enthusiastic about reading instruction, and her students may already be more motivated readers than the average class down the corridor. When those students improve, the gain is real, but it cannot be attributed to extensive reading alone. The programme and the pre-existing conditions arrived together, and separating their contributions is difficult after the fact.

Using methods designed to adjust for these differences between groups, Hamada (2020) arrives at a more conservative estimate, with the adjusted effect size falling to approximately d = 0.37. That figure is still positive and still meaningful; a student like Priya, from the scenario above, remains more likely to improve under an extensive reading programme than without one. The adjusted estimate is, however, noticeably smaller than the unadjusted group-comparison figures reported by Nakanishi (2015) at d = 0.46 and by Jeon and Day (2016) at d = 0.57. Treating the higher numbers as definitive while ignoring Hamada's more cautious estimate would misrepresent what the research supports.

Reflective practitioners present all three sets of figures together. Imagine a trainee named Daniel preparing to argue for a graded-reader programme at his secondary school. His head of department asks how strong the evidence is, and Daniel replies:

T: Nakanishi's analysis found d = 0.46 in group comparisons and d = 0.71 in pre-post designs; Jeon and Day found d = 0.57 and d = 0.79. Hamada's 2020 analysis, adjusting for publication bias, puts the corrected figure closer to d = 0.37.
S: So it works, but it's not a miracle cure?
T: Exactly. The evidence is consistent and positive, but we should plan the programme carefully and track our own results rather than assuming the published averages will transfer automatically.

That exchange captures the stance this course asks trainees to adopt. Extensive reading is a well-supported intervention, not a guaranteed one, and the difference between those two descriptions matters when setting expectations with colleagues, parents, and school administrators.

## Setting Up an Extensive Reading Programme

The physical space in which students read shapes their relationship with reading itself. A classroom environment that feels organised and comfortable encourages students to approach books with curiosity rather than obligation (Shaping the Way We Teach English, americanenglish.state.gov). When trainees begin planning an extensive reading programme, the reading station deserves the same deliberate attention they would give to lesson objectives or materials selection.

Separation from high-activity areas is the first practical consideration. A reading corner placed next to a noisy content-based station will undermine the calm atmosphere that independent reading requires. Bookshelves, easels, or other vertically oriented objects can mark the boundary between zones, giving students a physical signal that they are entering a different kind of space. Comfortable seating, even a few cushions or low chairs, reinforces that signal without requiring a large budget.

Wall space along the perimeter of the room can carry display shelves for books, freeing up floor area for seating and movement. A varied collection matters here: if Amara always reaches for the same two titles because nothing else looks accessible, the station has not yet done its job. Stocking a range of genres, topics, and difficulty levels gives students genuine choice, the condition under which they begin to take responsibility for their own reading lives.

Student involvement in setting up the station is part of the pedagogical logic of extensive reading, not a courtesy added afterwards. Asking students where the reading corner should go, which books they would like to see there, and what behaviour feels right for that space gives them a stake in the environment they will use. A brief conversation might sound like this:

T: Where do you think the reading corner should be in our classroom?
S: Maybe near the window, away from the door?
T: Good thinking. Why there?
S: It's quieter, and there's more light.

That exchange takes two minutes and produces a student who already feels some ownership of the space.

Once the location is agreed, posting activity guidelines and behaviour rules at the station gives students a reference point they can consult without interrupting the teacher or each other. Rules written with student input, and displayed with simple illustrations where literacy levels vary, are more likely to be followed than rules handed down from above. Role-playing expected behaviour before the station opens for the first time removes ambiguity: students who have rehearsed the routine of settling in, selecting a book, and reading on their own are far less likely to treat the station as a social area.

Starting with a single reading station before expanding to additional ones is sound advice for any teacher new to this approach. One well-resourced, well-organised corner that students understand and value is more effective than three stations that feel provisional. As both teacher and students grow comfortable with the routines, the station can be extended, the book collection deepened, and the guidelines refined. The goal at every stage is an environment in which picking up a book feels like a natural and pleasant thing to do.

## Putting It Together: What the Evidence Means for Your Classroom

The three sets of figures examined in this unit tell a consistent story, and reading them together gives a clearer picture than any single study can. Nakanishi's (2015) meta-analysis of 34 studies produced effect sizes of d = 0.46 in group comparisons and d = 0.71 in pre-post designs. Jeon and Day (2016), drawing on 49 studies, found d = 0.57 and d = 0.79 respectively. Hamada (2020) cautions that selection bias inflates both sets of figures, and offers an adjusted estimate of approximately d = 0.37. Even that more conservative number sits in the range that researchers in education describe as a meaningful, consistent benefit, not a marginal or unreliable one.

Understanding what these figures mean in practical terms helps trainees set honest expectations. A student like Fatima, who reads graded readers for twenty minutes three times a week over a semester, is unlikely to transform her reading speed overnight. What the evidence predicts is a steady, cumulative gain in reading proficiency, one that compounds across months and years of sustained practice. The effect is real and replicable; it is not dramatic in any single lesson, which is precisely why the programme needs to be maintained rather than abandoned after a few weeks when results are not yet visible.

The conditions under which these gains were observed map directly onto the practical guidance covered earlier in this unit. Student choice, a comfortable and quiet reading space, a wide variety of texts pitched at accessible levels, and a low-pressure atmosphere are not incidental features of a well-run extensive reading programme. They are the conditions present in the studies that produced the figures above. A teacher who stocks only a narrow range of topics, or who seats readers in a noisy corner of the room, is not replicating the environment the research describes.

T: "You can pick anything from the shelf that looks interesting to you. There's no test on it."
S: "Even the football one?"
T: "Especially the football one."

That brief exchange captures something the effect-size tables cannot: the motivational climate that makes sustained reading possible in the first place.

When speaking to learners and parents, trainees should frame extensive reading honestly. It is a proven complement to other classroom instruction, supported by two independent meta-analyses, and it carries the additional advantage of being genuinely enjoyable for many students. Presenting it as a standalone solution to all language learning needs would misrepresent the evidence and set the programme up for unfair criticism. Presenting it as one well-supported strand of a broader course is both accurate and persuasive.

Published averages, however, describe populations rather than individuals. A trainee's student Omar may progress faster than the meta-analytic mean; another student may need more time or a different text selection before gains become visible. Keeping simple records, such as titles read, self-reported enjoyment ratings, and periodic short reading tasks, allows the teacher to track individual trajectories and adjust the programme accordingly. The research provides a sound reason to begin; the teacher's own observation provides the evidence needed to sustain and improve what happens next.
