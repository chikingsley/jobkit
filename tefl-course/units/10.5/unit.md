# Supplementary and Self-Made Materials, Teaching Aids

## Why Go Beyond the Coursebook: The Case for Supplementary Materials

A coursebook provides a shared starting point, but no single published text can anticipate the vocabulary priorities, cultural references, or classroom routines of every teaching context. A teacher working with six-year-olds in a rural primary school faces different demands from one teaching teenagers in an urban secondary classroom, and both face demands that a general coursebook addresses only partially. Supplementary materials, meaning practical, printable or physical resources that sit alongside the main text, exist precisely to close that gap.

The principle behind using such materials is adaptation to local needs. A worksheet on farm vocabulary may suit one class perfectly and require only minor changes for another; a graphic organizer designed for brainstorming can be reproduced on the board at no cost and used across age groups and topics. The source materials underlying this unit describe supplementary resources as things teachers can "use or adapt to suit their teaching context and topics," and that framing is significant. Adaptation is presented as a routine professional act, not a sign that the coursebook has failed.

Self-made materials are also a normal feature of experienced teaching, not a fallback when published resources are unavailable. Consider a teacher named Fatima who notices that her teenage students respond well to a popular local song but that the coursebook offers only formal reading texts. Fatima writes a gap-fill activity around the song's lyrics, adds two discussion questions, and produces a resource that fits her students precisely. That process, repeated across a career, builds a personal bank of materials that no catalogue can replicate.

The practical dimension matters too. Many of the materials surveyed in this unit require only paper, scissors, and basic adhesives. Flip books, stick puppets, pocket books, and mixed-media collages can all be produced with recycled cardboard, old magazines, and classroom stationery. A teacher named Carlos, working without a photocopier, can still draw a graphic organizer on the board and ask students to copy it into their notebooks before a brainstorming task.

T: "Copy this diagram into your book. You have two minutes."
S: "Do we write the words or just draw the boxes?"
T: "Draw the boxes first, then we'll fill them in together."

That exchange illustrates how a self-made aid becomes part of the lesson's structure rather than an addition to it. The sections that follow survey the main types of low-cost supplementary material a TEFL teacher can design, produce, and adapt for a wide range of learners and contexts.

## Flashcards, Flip Books, and Pocket Books: Simple Visual Aids You Can Make

A flip book is a small, hand-held booklet in which each page displays a word, image, or sentence that can be flipped independently to reveal new combinations. According to the *Shaping the Way We Teach English* supplementary materials, the accompanying guide for flip books lists the required materials and provides step-by-step construction instructions, so a teacher can prepare a set before class or walk students through the making process during a lesson. The physical act of assembling the book gives learners like Fatima, a six-year-old working on animal vocabulary, repeated contact with the target words before any formal practice begins. Cutting, labeling, and ordering the pages requires students to read and process each item, which means the construction stage is itself a form of language work.

Pocket books are a related format: small, foldable booklets that students can carry and return to outside the classroom. The *Shaping* materials describe two specific pocket book titles designed for very young learners. The first, called "What Do I See?", is built around a fill-in-the-blank structure; students complete the sentences in their own copies, so each finished book reflects the individual child's responses. A teacher might hand blank templates to a class of five-year-olds and say:

T: "Write the word here. What do you see in the picture?"
S: "A cat."
T: "Good. Write 'cat' on the line."

That brief exchange shows how the completion task generates genuine communicative output rather than passive copying.

The second pocket book, "The Book About Me", follows the same construction principle but centers on personal information, giving students like Daniel a reason to write sentences that are directly meaningful to them. Because each child produces a unique version, the finished books can serve as reading material for classmates, extending the activity into a peer-reading task. The *Shaping* materials note that teachers can have students make their own copies by filling in the blanks, which means the teacher's preparation is limited to producing or reproducing the template.

All three formats share a practical advantage: they require only basic materials such as paper, scissors, and something to write with. A teacher working without a budget for commercial resources can produce usable templates by hand or on a simple photocopier, then distribute them for students to complete and keep. The finished objects also have a durability that a worksheet does not; a pocket book fits into a school bag and can be reviewed at home, while a flip book can be stored in a classroom box and returned to across several lessons. Producing the aid and using it are, in these cases, two stages of the same learning sequence rather than separate activities.

## Puppets as Teaching Aids: Full-Sized, Hand, and Stick Puppets

Puppets are physical figures that a teacher or student manipulates to represent a character during a lesson. The three types described in the source materials are full-sized paper puppets, hand puppets, and stick puppets, each with its own set of construction instructions and a distinct physical form suited to different classroom moments.

Full-sized paper puppets are large enough to be seen across a classroom, which makes them well suited to whole-group storytelling. A teacher playing the role of a market vendor, for instance, can hold the puppet at chest height and speak through it while students respond as customers. The scale of the figure draws attention and signals to students that a narrative frame has opened, separating the activity from ordinary question-and-answer work.

Hand puppets fit over the fingers or the whole hand and allow more fluid movement than a flat paper figure. Because the puppet sits on the teacher's hand, it can turn toward individual students, lean in as if listening, or react with a visible gesture when a student speaks. A teacher named Priya might use a hand puppet to address a quieter student directly: T: "Oh, Wei, my puppet wants to know what you had for breakfast." S: "I had rice." T: "Rice! My puppet loves rice." The puppet mediates the exchange and reduces the pressure a student might feel when speaking to the teacher alone.

Stick puppets are simpler to construct and, for that reason, practical when students make their own. The source materials include a template showing a line drawing of a boy and a girl that students can color and cut out before attaching the figure to a stick. Preparing the template in advance removes the drawing step, so class time goes toward coloring, cutting, and the language work that follows rather than toward producing a recognizable figure from scratch.

All three puppet types serve the same underlying function: they give a voice to a character who is not physically present in the room. This matters because the source materials describe effective storytelling as a conversational process, one in which the teacher interacts with students through questions and responses so that students feel actively involved rather than passive listeners. A puppet makes that interaction concrete. When a stick puppet of a girl named Yuki asks the class where she should go on her holiday, students are answering a character, and the exchange carries a small but genuine communicative purpose. The puppet is not decoration; it is the mechanism through which a story becomes a dialogue.

## Worksheets, Graphic Organizers, and Assessment Tools

Worksheets, graphic organizers, and assessment checklists each serve a distinct purpose in the classroom, and understanding that distinction helps teachers decide when to reach for an existing tool and when to make one from scratch. A worksheet built around a rubric and visual cues, such as the vocabulary assessment described in the source materials for very young learners, is primarily a practice and measurement instrument. The teacher uses the visual cues to prompt a response from a child who may not yet read, and the rubric converts that response into usable information about what the student knows. A teacher working with five-year-olds on farm vocabulary could draw simple pictures of a cow, a fence, and a barn in separate boxes, then sit with each child in turn and record which items the child can name correctly.

A graphic organizer serves a different function: it supports the planning stage of a task rather than measuring its outcome. The brainstorming organizer described in the source materials is blank by design, which means the teacher can reproduce it on the board and complete it together with the class before students attempt their own versions. When a teacher draws the organizer on the board and says, "T: We're going to write about our town. What do we know about it? S: There's a market. T: Good, where shall I write that?" the organizer becomes a shared thinking space rather than a private worksheet. Its value lies in making the process of organizing ideas visible to every student at once.

The self- and peer-assessment checklist represents a third function, one concerned with reflection and accountability. In the writing activity described in the source materials, a student named, for example, Priya uses the checklist first to review her own story, then passes it to a classmate who applies the same criteria to Priya's work. The checklist does not tell Priya what to write; it tells her what to look for once the writing is done. That shift in perspective, from writer to evaluator, asks the student to apply the lesson's criteria consciously rather than passively receive a grade.

Teachers who want to create their own versions of these tools can follow the same underlying principles visible in each example. The layout should be clear enough that a student can work through it without stopping to ask what is required. The content should be specific to the topic at hand but easy to swap out when the topic changes, so that the same basic format serves multiple units across the year. Each tool should give the student a defined task: name the picture, fill the organizer, tick the criterion. When those three conditions are met, a self-made worksheet or checklist functions as reliably as any commercially produced material, and it has the additional advantage of fitting the exact vocabulary, text type, or age group in front of the teacher.

## Realia and Mixed Media: Using Everyday Objects and Recycled Materials

Realia, the term for real-world objects brought into the classroom, need not come from a catalogue or a specialist supplier. Everyday and recycled materials serve the same purpose at a fraction of the cost, and the *Create to Communicate* resource makes this explicit by listing cardboard, shoe boxes, cereal boxes, brown paper bags, recycled papers and magazines, and glass or plastic jars as usable components in art-based language activities. A teacher preparing a unit on household objects, for instance, can ask students to bring in an empty cereal box from home, turning the object itself into both the subject of discussion and the raw material for a lesson task.

The same resource notes that teachers may collect recycled materials from their own personal belongings or invite students to contribute, which distributes the preparation load and gives students a sense of ownership before the lesson begins. When a student like Priya arrives carrying a brown paper bag she has decorated at home, she already has something to talk about.

T: Priya, what did you bring today?
S: I bring a bag. It is brown. I put flowers on it.
T: You decorated it. What did you use?

That brief exchange produces authentic language from a genuine object, with no printed prompt required.

Mixed media, the technique of combining two or more art materials in a single piece, extends this principle by giving students multiple ways to work with the same language content. The *Create to Communicate* source describes combinations such as collage with pencil or marker details added on top, paint with collage elements glued to the surface, and bookmaking with drawings placed inside the finished book. A student who struggles to write a full sentence about his neighborhood may still be able to cut a photograph of a building from a recycled magazine, paste it onto card, and then label it with a single word or phrase. The physical act of assembling the piece gives him time to think, and the finished object gives him something concrete to describe to a partner.

Materials from nature, listed in the same source as optional components, include grass, straw, twigs, sand, small rocks, and leaves. These cost nothing and are available in most teaching contexts. A class working on descriptive adjectives can press a leaf onto a painted background, then write texture words around it: rough, smooth, dry, green. The object anchors the vocabulary to a sensory experience rather than to an abstract definition.

The practical value of this approach is that the range of available combinations is, as the source puts it, limitless. A teacher does not need to settle on one format. Collage, paint, printmaking with cut vegetables or sponges, and sculpture from recycled containers can all be adapted to the language point at hand, whether that is daily routines, personal symbols, or chain stories. Because the materials change with each project, students encounter the same underlying language structures in varied physical contexts, which keeps the work from feeling repetitive. The teacher's role is to select the combination that suits the vocabulary or grammar focus of the week and to ensure that the instructions for assembling the piece are clear enough that students spend their energy on the language, not on working out what to do with the glue.

## Putting It All Together: Choosing and Using Aids in Your Lessons

Selecting an aid is only the first decision; the more demanding question is how to build the rest of the lesson around it so that the aid does genuine instructional work. Before a teacher introduces a flip book, a set of stick puppets, or a mixed-media poster, it is worth asking what vocabulary or interaction the aid is meant to support, what students will do with it once it is made, and what follow-up activities will consolidate the language afterward. A pocket book titled "What Do I See?" is not complete as a teaching event the moment students fill in the blanks; the teacher still needs to plan how pairs will share their books, what questions will prompt comparison, and how the session will close.

Classroom management becomes especially pressing when physical materials are involved. A teacher distributing hand-puppet templates to a class of thirty six-year-olds will find the activity collapse into noise unless the transition into and out of the task is clearly marked. The source materials point to signal words as one practical solution: a teacher might use the word "magic" or a single clap to indicate that cutting stops and listening begins. This kind of non-material aid costs nothing to prepare, yet it gives the physical activity a structure that keeps students oriented. Without it, even a well-designed self-made resource can generate more confusion than language practice.

Planning the deployment of an aid also means anticipating the language demands at each stage. When a teacher named Fatima introduces a graphic organizer for brainstorming, she might first reproduce it on the board and model one entry aloud before students work on their own copies. The dialogue at that moment might run: T: "I'm going to write one idea here. What do you think this word means?" S: "Something you do every day?" T: "Good. Now you add one word to your own sheet." That brief exchange checks comprehension, models the task, and gives students a concrete starting point, all before the organizer does its main job of organizing thought.

The source's "observe, evaluate, adapt, act" cycle applies as directly to material-making as it does to any other aspect of teaching. A teacher who makes a set of story-writing picture cards for the first time will notice, after the lesson, that some pictures generated rich language while others produced only one-word responses. That observation is the basis for the next version of the cards. A teacher named Carlos might record in a brief note that the urban street scene worked well for narrative but that the abstract pattern card confused students; the following week he replaces the abstract card with a market scene and tries again. Treating self-made materials as drafts rather than finished products is what turns material-making from a preparation chore into a sustained form of professional development. Each revised version of a resource carries the teacher's accumulated understanding of what that particular class needs.
