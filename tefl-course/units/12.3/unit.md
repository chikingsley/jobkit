# Learning Strategies

## What Are Learning Strategies? Core Categories Explained

A strategy is a tool, plan, or method used for accomplishing a task. In a language classroom, this definition covers an enormous range of behaviors, from the way a student silently rehearses a new verb form to the way she chooses a partner for a speaking activity. Because strategies serve so many different purposes, the source material groups them into three broad categories, each addressing a distinct dimension of the learning process.

Cognitive strategies are the most immediately recognizable of the three. They involve memorizing and manipulating target language structures, and they include specific techniques such as visualization, making associations, chunking information into manageable pieces, and using mnemonics. A student like Wei, who writes new vocabulary on index cards and then groups the cards by grammatical category, is applying cognitive strategies to organize and retain new material. These are the strategies most people think of first when they recall how they once studied a foreign language.

Metacognition, defined in the source as a global understanding of one's own mental processes, gives rise to a second and distinct category. Metacognitive strategies involve managing and supervising strategy use itself; a learner who monitors her own comprehension during a listening task and decides to slow down and re-read her notes is operating at this level. A student like Yuki, who pauses mid-task and says to herself, "I am not understanding this; I need to try a different approach," is demonstrating metacognitive awareness in action. This self-regulatory capacity is what allows learners to become, over time, effective self-teachers.

Social and affective strategies form the third category. Social strategies include cooperating with other learners and seeking opportunities to interact with native speakers; affective strategies address the emotional side of learning, such as managing anxiety and maintaining motivation. When a student named Fatima deliberately chooses to sit with a more proficient classmate during a group reading task, she is using a social strategy to compensate for a gap in her own knowledge. The affective dimension matters because a learner's feelings, attitudes, and motivation shape how readily any other strategy can take hold.

One practical difficulty unites all three categories: strategies are mostly invisible mental processes. A teacher watching a classroom activity sees students reading, writing, or speaking, but the cognitive work underneath those behaviors remains hidden.

T: "How did you figure out what that word meant?"
S: "I looked at the words around it and kind of guessed from the sentence."

That exchange reveals inferencing at work, a strategy the teacher could not have seen without asking. For this reason, teachers must analyze what they observe and, in many cases, infer which strategies are operating rather than expect to identify them through direct observation alone.

## Cognitive Strategies: Tools for Processing and Remembering Language

A cognitive strategy, as Beckman describes it, is a strategy or group of strategies that a learner uses to perform academic tasks or to improve social skills. The term covers a wide range of mental tools, from simple memory aids to complex procedures for organizing and processing new information. What distinguishes cognitive strategies from other categories is their direct focus on the mental work of learning itself: taking in language, storing it, retrieving it, and applying it to new tasks.

Successful learners do not rely on a single cognitive strategy. Beckman notes that they use numerous strategies, often drawing on several at once depending on the task and their own established habits of learning. A student preparing for a vocabulary quiz might use visualization to form a mental image of a word's meaning, verbalization to say the word aloud in a sentence, and making associations to connect the new word to something already familiar. Each strategy reinforces the others, and the combination tends to produce more durable learning than any one approach used alone.

The range of cognitive strategies described by Beckman includes visualization, verbalization, making associations, chunking, questioning, scanning, underlining, accessing cues, using mnemonics, sounding out words, and self-checking and monitoring. Chunking, for instance, involves grouping items of language into manageable units rather than treating each element separately. A student learning a set of irregular past-tense verbs might group them by sound pattern, "sang, rang, sprang," rather than memorizing each verb in isolation. Scanning allows a reader to move quickly through a text to locate specific information without processing every word, a skill that transfers directly to real-world reading tasks such as finding a bus time or skimming a news article.

Mnemonics offer a particularly clear example of how a cognitive strategy can be made concrete and teachable. Beckman provides the mnemonic PLAN, developed by De La Paz, Owen, Harris, and Graham (2000) for use in writing tasks: Pay attention to the prompt, List main ideas, Add supporting ideas, Number your ideas. A teacher introducing this device gives students a visible, repeatable procedure they can apply independently whenever they face a writing assignment. Consider a classroom exchange where a student, Yuki, has been staring at a blank page for several minutes.

T: "Yuki, do you remember the PLAN steps we practiced?"
S: "Oh, right. So first I need to read the question carefully, then write down my main ideas."

That brief prompt is enough to activate the strategy. Yuki now has a sequence to follow rather than an open-ended task with no clear entry point.

Self-checking and monitoring deserve particular attention because they allow learners to catch and correct their own errors without waiting for teacher feedback. A student like Carlos, reading a passage aloud, pauses when a sentence does not sound right and rereads it before continuing. He has not been told to do this; the strategy has become part of how he processes text. When teachers make self-monitoring explicit, by asking students to review their written work against a checklist before submitting it, they give all learners access to a habit that stronger learners often develop on their own.

The consistent point across Beckman's account is that cognitive strategies work best in combination. Memory tasks benefit from pairing visualization with verbalization and mnemonics together, rather than relying on any single technique. A teacher who introduces these strategies one at a time, then shows students how to combine them for specific tasks, gives learners a flexible toolkit rather than a fixed set of isolated procedures.

## Metacognitive Strategies: Helping Learners Think About Their Own Learning

Metacognition, as defined in this module, is the global understanding of thinking or mental processes that enables learners to recognize, monitor, and organize those processes in themselves. Where cognitive strategies address specific tasks such as memorizing vocabulary or comprehending a text, metacognitive strategies operate at a higher level, allowing learners to step back and observe how they are learning, not just what they are learning. A student who notices mid-task that her reading strategy is not helping her understand a dense paragraph, and who then switches to rereading with self-questioning, is using metacognition in a practical, immediate way.

Cohen's account in Reading B describes several behaviors that characterize this kind of strategic self-awareness. Learners who use metacognitive strategies can self-diagnose their strengths and weaknesses in language learning, monitor and self-evaluate their performance during tasks, and transfer successful strategies to new learning contexts. Take a student like Carlos who realizes, after a listening exercise, that he understood the general topic but missed most of the specific details. He identifies the gap, adjusts his approach for the next listening task, and applies the same monitoring habit when he moves to reading comprehension work in another class. That transfer of a self-monitoring habit across contexts is precisely what Cohen describes as one of the goals of strategy training.

Chamot and O'Malley's four-stage framework, also presented in Reading B, gives teachers a concrete structure for building metacognitive strategy use into classroom tasks. In the first stage, Planning, students decide how they will approach a learning task before beginning it. Monitoring follows: students pay attention to their own strategy use and check their comprehension as they work. In the third stage, Problem Solving, they identify and address difficulties as they arise, and in the fourth stage, Evaluation, they assess how well a given strategy worked after the task is complete. A teacher might introduce this sequence explicitly before a group reading activity:

T: Before you start, take one minute. What is your plan for reading this text?
S: I'm going to read the first sentence of each paragraph first to get the main idea.
T: Good. While you read, notice whether that approach is working for you. We'll talk about it after.

After the activity, the teacher returns to the evaluation stage, asking students whether their chosen approach helped and what they might do differently next time. Over repeated cycles, this sequence becomes a habit of mind rather than a teacher-directed procedure.

Beckman's description of the independent, strategic learner in Reading A captures the endpoint that metacognitive strategy instruction aims toward. That learner asks clarifying questions, checks and monitors work and behavior, and sets personal goals. Crucially, Beckman notes that such a learner knows the value of using particular strategies through experience and is eager to learn others that might prove beneficial. This eagerness does not appear automatically; it develops when teachers make the process of evaluating strategy use a regular part of classroom life, rather than an occasional activity.

The teacher's role in this process begins with observation and diagnosis. According to the module's introduction, teachers need to learn about their students' backgrounds, identify student problems, and find out what strategies learners are currently using before they can help students determine which strategies might work best for them. A teacher who builds brief planning and evaluation moments into ordinary lessons, without treating them as separate from the content, gives students repeated low-stakes practice in thinking about their own thinking. As students become more adept at this, they become, in the module's terms, better self-teachers.

## Social Strategies: Learning With and From Others

Social strategies, as defined in Reading B (Cohen), are strategies for enhancing learning through cooperating with other learners and seeking to interact with native speakers or more proficient speakers of the target language. They differ from cognitive and metacognitive strategies in that the learning itself happens through interaction rather than through individual mental processing. A student who asks a classmate to explain an unfamiliar word, or who arranges to speak with a native speaker outside class, is using a social strategy deliberately.

The classroom described in the module's answer key offers a clear illustration of how a teacher can build social strategies into ordinary lesson design. The teacher formed student pairs and groups with care, placing students together to provide mutual support and to draw on shared knowledge. Each group received a different topic, so students had to communicate their findings to the rest of the class rather than complete a task and stop there. That arrangement made genuine communication necessary; students were not practicing a simulated exchange but producing language that their classmates had a real need to receive. The affective result was visible in students' willingness to contribute to their own group's report and, when they felt moved to do so, to other groups' reports as well. They also connected the content to their own personal experiences, which deepened engagement with the material.

The peer tutoring model described in Reading B (Cohen) extends this principle beyond the single classroom. In tandem programs, students of different native language backgrounds are paired for mutual tutoring sessions. A student whose first language is Korean and who is learning English might be paired with an English-speaking student learning Korean; each alternates between the role of learner and the role of teacher, with equal time devoted to each language. One practical consequence is that students exchange suggestions about the strategies they use, so strategy training occurs informally as a by-product of the sessions. A student like Priya, for example, might tell her partner how she uses contextual clues to infer the meaning of unfamiliar words, while her partner explains a different approach: grouping vocabulary by topic to aid memory.

T: Priya, when you don't know a word in English, what do you do first?
S: I look at the words around it. If the sentence gives me enough context, I can usually work it out.
T: That's a useful strategy. Have you told your partner about it?

Such exchanges are not incidental. When students articulate the strategies they use, they consolidate their own understanding of those strategies and make them available to others. A teacher who designs group tasks with this in mind, whether through structured peer tutoring, carefully formed classroom groups, or whole-class reporting activities, gives students repeated practice in the social dimension of language learning. The goal is for students to see other learners not only as conversation partners but as sources of strategic knowledge they can add to their own developing repertoire.

## Building Strategy Training Into Normal Lessons

Strategy instruction does not require a separate lesson slot, a special unit, or time taken away from language content. Beckman describes effective strategy instruction as "an integral part of classroom instruction, regardless of the content being taught; it is not an additional subject." A teacher who waits for the right moment to introduce strategies, or who treats them as an occasional add-on, misses the point entirely. The strategies belong inside the regular lesson, woven into the activities students are already doing.

The sequence Beckman outlines gives teachers a practical order to follow. The first step is to describe the strategy and explain its purpose: when it can be used, why it helps, and how to apply it. A teacher might say, T: "Before we read this article, I want to show you a way to predict what the text is about by looking at the headings and the first sentence of each paragraph. This saves time and helps your brain prepare for new information." The second step is to model the strategy openly, thinking aloud so students can observe the mental process. The third step is assisted practice, during which the teacher monitors the class, provides cues, and gives feedback while students try the strategy themselves. S: "I looked at the heading and I think the paragraph will be about crocodile habitats." T: "Good. Now check whether the first sentence confirms that." Repeated practice of this kind moves the strategy toward automaticity, so students apply it without having to stop and deliberate. The fourth step asks students to monitor and evaluate their own use of the strategy, noticing whether it worked and why. The fifth step encourages them to transfer the strategy to a different task or subject, such as using the same prediction technique when approaching a biology text or an exam passage.

The Strategies-Based Instruction approach described by Cohen takes this integration further by making strategy work a continuous feature of the classroom rather than a sequence applied to a single lesson. In a typical SBI classroom, teachers describe and model potentially useful strategies, then elicit additional examples from students based on their own learning experiences. A teacher might pause mid-lesson and ask, T: "Has anyone used a different way to remember these verb forms?" S: "I make up a sentence where the first letters spell something." T: "That's a mnemonic. Let's hear it." The teacher then leads small-group or whole-class discussions about which strategies seem to work and in what situations, and encourages students to experiment with approaches they have not tried before. Strategies enter everyday class materials in two ways: through direct explanation and through task design, where the activity itself requires students to behave strategically while pursuing a language goal. A reading activity, a group report, a note-taking exercise: each can carry strategy practice inside it without displacing the language goal.

Physical reminders in the classroom support this ongoing integration. Written reminders for learning strategies posted on walls and bulletin boards help students keep those strategies present in their minds between explicit teaching moments. A poster listing the steps for self-monitoring during reading, or a display showing common memory techniques, gives students a reference point they can consult independently. Some teachers produce these materials themselves, and some involve students in making them, which itself becomes a form of strategy consolidation. The classroom environment, in this way, becomes part of the instruction.

## Helping Learners Build Strategies Between Classes

The work of strategy development does not stop when students leave the room. A teacher who builds strategy awareness into lessons can also prepare learners to apply those strategies in the hours and days between classes. The action-plan steps in this module point toward several concrete ways to do this, beginning with the classroom conditions that make independent practice possible in the first place.

A trusting atmosphere is the foundation. When students feel safe enough to attempt tasks they might fail, they are more willing to try unfamiliar strategies on their own. Picture a student like Fatima, who has always memorized vocabulary by writing words repeatedly. If her teacher has made it clear that experimenting with a new approach carries no penalty, Fatima may try grouping words by meaning or associating them with images she already knows. That willingness to experiment is itself a strategic behavior, and it grows from the confidence built inside the classroom.

Self-diagnosis is a skill teachers can teach directly. One goal of strategy training, as Cohen describes it in Reading B, is for learners to identify their own strengths and weaknesses in language learning. A teacher might ask students to keep a brief weekly log, noting which tasks felt manageable and which felt difficult, and recording what they did in response. A student like Yuki, for example, might notice that she understands spoken English in films far better than in her content-area science class; that observation becomes the starting point for a conversation with her teacher about why the gap exists and what she might do about it.

Activating schema before a task helps learners connect new material to knowledge they already hold, and this habit can be practiced outside class as well. When a teacher regularly models schema activation in lessons, asking students what they already know about crocodiles before reading a text about them, students begin to apply the same mental move on their own. A learner like Priya, preparing to watch an English-language documentary at home, might pause before pressing play and ask herself what she already knows about the topic. That pause is a strategy, and it transfers.

Transfer to contexts beyond the classroom is one of the clearest measures of whether strategy instruction has taken hold. Reading B lists the ability to transfer successful strategies to new learning contexts as a central goal of strategy training. The action-plan section of this module names several of those contexts explicitly: content classes taught in English, music, films, and conversations with speakers of other languages. A teacher who points to these possibilities during lessons gives students a map for where their strategies can go. When a student like Yuki realizes that the note-taking method she practiced in English class also helps her follow her biology lessons, the strategy has become genuinely her own.

When learners reach this point, something shifts in how they relate to their own learning. The source material describes a set of outcomes that follow from becoming strategic: students trust their minds, recognize that more than one approach can work, acknowledge their mistakes and try to correct them, and develop a personal study process they can rely on. They become, in the language of this module, self-teachers. A learner who has internalized even a small set of strategies carries those tools into every new context, including contexts the teacher will never see. That capacity for continued, self-directed learning after leaving the language classroom is the deepest purpose of strategy instruction.
