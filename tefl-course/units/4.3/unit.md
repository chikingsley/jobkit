# PPP and Test-Teach-Test

## What Is PPP and Why Does It Matter?

Presentation-Practice-Production, usually shortened to PPP, is a three-stage framework for organising a language lesson so that new material moves from teacher control toward student independence. Each stage has a distinct purpose, and the three together form a coherent sequence rather than a collection of separate activities.

In the Presentation stage, the teacher introduces new language items directly and ensures students understand their meaning and form before any practice begins. A teacher might hold up two small puppets, name each one, and use them to demonstrate target vocabulary in context, as the source describes in one observed lesson. The teacher carries most of the communicative load at this point; students are primarily receiving and processing new information rather than producing it independently.

The Practice stage follows, and its defining feature is control. Activities are structured so that students work with the new language in a supported, predictable way, reducing the chance of error while building familiarity. Listen-and-repeat sequences, chants performed at varying speeds, and Total Physical Response tasks where students act out commands all serve this function. When a teacher leads the class through a chant, then slows it down, then speeds it up again, she is giving students repeated, low-risk contact with the target forms before asking them to do anything more demanding.

Production is the third stage, and here students use the language with greater freedom. The teacher steps back and creates conditions in which students must draw on what they have practised to communicate something of their own. A game such as Hot Potato, an activity where students construct sentences using vocabulary items, or a puppet-box task where children respond spontaneously all represent this kind of freer output. The teacher's role shifts from instructor to facilitator, monitoring rather than directing.

The value of the PPP sequence lies in its logic. A student named Maria, for instance, is unlikely to produce a sentence like "She is going to eat" correctly if she has never heard the structure modelled, never repeated it in a controlled setting, and never had a structured opportunity to try it on her own terms. PPP addresses each of those needs in order, giving the teacher a clear principle for deciding what comes first, what comes next, and when to release responsibility to the learner.

## The Three Stages of PPP in Detail

In the Presentation stage, the teacher's primary task is to make the meaning of new language clear before asking students to use it. One way to do this is through realia and puppets. In the source lessons, a teacher introduces vocabulary by bringing two small puppets into the classroom and using them to demonstrate the meaning of target words physically, so that a student named Kofi can see what "sit" and "stand" mean rather than receiving a translation. The teacher speaks only in the target language throughout, relying on gesture, facial expression, and the puppets themselves to bridge the gap between word and meaning. Concept-checking questions follow the initial presentation: rather than asking "Do you understand?", the teacher asks questions that require students to demonstrate comprehension, such as pointing to the correct puppet or selecting the right word from the board.

The teacher's role at this stage is to control the input tightly. Students are largely receptive, watching and listening rather than producing. A student named Yuki may not yet say anything independently, but she is building a mental representation of the new item by observing the teacher model it repeatedly in context. The teacher uses individual student names to check whether specific learners have grasped a distinction, for instance asking whether a particular child is "he" or "she" to confirm that personal pronouns have landed. This targeted checking allows the teacher to gauge readiness before moving the class forward.

The Practice stage introduces controlled production, where students begin to use the new language within a structure that limits the chance of error. Listen-and-repeat sequences are a common starting point: the teacher says a word or phrase, and the whole class repeats it in chorus. Chanting extends this naturally, and the source describes teachers varying the pace of chants deliberately, moving from slow to fast, so that a student named Dmitri must process the language at increasing speed rather than reciting it as a rote performance. TPR sequences ask students to perform physical actions on command, connecting the spoken form to a bodily response; students stand, turn around, drink from glasses, and sit again in a cycle that keeps the language active and the body engaged.

The student's role in Practice is to respond accurately within the given frame. The teacher still controls what language appears, but students are now producing it rather than only receiving it. Pointing exercises, where a student selects the correct image or word on the board in response to a prompt, sit at the more receptive end of this stage. Asking a student to supply the correct verb to accompany a subject on the board sits at the more demanding end, requiring a small but genuine act of retrieval and production. The teacher monitors closely, providing immediate corrective feedback when a student like Ana gives an incorrect answer, and using elicitation rather than direct correction to guide her toward the right form.

The Production stage asks students to use the language more freely, drawing on what they have practised to communicate in a less controlled setting. The source describes a Hot Potato game in which students pass an object around the group and must produce language when the music stops, creating a context where the pressure of the game motivates genuine output. Hands-on puppet activities extend this further: students handle the puppets themselves and use the target vocabulary to make simple sentences, so that a student named Tariq is no longer responding to a teacher prompt but initiating language in his own right. The teacher's role at this stage is to observe and support rather than to direct, stepping in only when a student is genuinely stuck or when an error pattern suggests that the earlier stages need revisiting.

Each stage carries a distinct relationship between teacher control and student autonomy. Presentation is teacher-led and student-receptive. Practice is teacher-framed but student-active, with production happening inside a structure the teacher has set. Production is student-driven, with the teacher present but no longer at the centre of every exchange. The movement across these three stages mirrors the movement from hearing a new word for the first time to being able to use it without prompting.

## When PPP Fits Best

PPP suits lessons where the teacher has identified a single, clearly bounded target item before the class begins. That item might be a vocabulary word, a short grammar structure, or a set phrase such as "See you tomorrow." When the target is discrete in this way, the teacher can plan a precise sequence of events, prepare all necessary materials in advance, and move students through the three stages without ambiguity about what is being learned. The source describes a teacher who arrives with realia set out, desks rearranged, and colour-coded sentence parts ready on a metal board; that level of preparation is both a symptom and a requirement of the PPP approach.

Lessons for very young learners offer some of the clearest illustrations of PPP in action. A teacher introducing vocabulary to beginners typically opens with a movement-based warm-up, perhaps a TPR song in which children act out verbs such as walk, eat, and drink on hearing the words spoken aloud. The physical response gives the teacher immediate, visible evidence that the word has registered, and it gives children a kinesthetic anchor for the new item before any formal practice begins. This warm-up stage corresponds directly to the Presentation phase, where the target language is made comprehensible through context, gesture, and repetition rather than through translation or explanation.

The puppet-based vocabulary sequences described in the source show how the Practice stage can be built from multiple activity types while keeping the same target item at the centre. A teacher introduces two small puppets, uses them to present a handful of vocabulary items, then cycles through listening and repeating, pointing, chanting at varying speeds, and asking individual students to stand when a word applies to them. Each activity reinforces the same words from a slightly different angle. Variety of this kind is not decoration; it gives students repeated encounters with the target in forms that differ enough to hold attention without shifting the learning focus away from the item being taught.

PPP also fits well when the teacher needs to manage a class of very young learners whose attention spans are short. Because each stage has a defined purpose and a defined end point, the teacher can plan transitions between activities with confidence. When a student named Mia loses focus during choral repetition, the teacher can move the group into the standing-and-sitting sequence without losing the thread of the lesson, because the target vocabulary remains constant even as the activity format changes. The structure of PPP gives the teacher a clear sequence to return to after any such interruption.

The approach is less suited to lessons where the target language cannot be specified in advance, or where the teacher wants students to draw on a range of previously learned language simultaneously. For a lesson built around a single new word, a grammar pattern with a regular form, or a fixed phrase that beginners need to produce accurately, PPP provides a logical and manageable framework. The teacher knows what success looks like at each stage, and students know what they are expected to do with the language before the lesson ends.

## What Is Test-Teach-Test?

Test-Teach-Test, often abbreviated as TTT, is a framework in which students attempt to use target language before the teacher provides any formal instruction. The sequence runs: an initial task that exposes what students already know or can do, a teaching stage shaped by what that task reveals, and a second task that checks whether the instruction has addressed the gaps. The order of the first two stages is the defining difference from PPP, where presentation always precedes practice.

The first Test stage functions as a diagnostic. The teacher sets a task that requires students to produce or engage with the target language, then observes where understanding breaks down. Consider the teacher described in the source material who begins a science lesson by asking students what they already know before introducing any new content. She is not warming up in a casual sense; she is gathering information about the class's existing knowledge so that her subsequent teaching can be targeted rather than generic. A student named Marco might demonstrate a partial understanding of plant biology, while a student named Yuki reveals a significant gap. The teacher now knows where to focus.

The Teach stage is shaped directly by what the first task exposed. Because the teacher has already seen where students struggle, the explanation or demonstration can address those specific points rather than covering ground students have already mastered. The source material offers a clear illustration in the teacher who introduces the word "photosynthesis," meaning the process by which plants convert sunlight into food, without telling students the answer outright. She guides students toward that meaning through questions and elicitation, building on what they already partially understand. The teaching is responsive rather than predetermined, and its content is calibrated to the actual gap between what students know and what the lesson requires them to know.

T: So the plant is taking something from the sun. What do you think it does with that?
S: It makes food?
T: Exactly. And we have a word for that whole process.

The second Test stage then checks whether the teaching has landed. The teacher sets a task similar in type to the first, and the students' performance shows whether the gap has closed. If a student who could not explain photosynthesis in the opening task can now use the term accurately in a sentence, the teaching has been effective. If the gap persists, the teacher has clear information about what needs further attention, either in the remainder of the lesson or in a subsequent one.

TTT suits lessons where students are likely to have some prior exposure to the target language, even if that exposure is incomplete or partially incorrect. It is less appropriate when students have no basis at all for the initial task, since a diagnostic stage requires something to diagnose. For a class of intermediate learners revisiting a grammar point, or for a group of young learners who have encountered vocabulary in another subject, the framework allows the teacher to build instruction around real and observed need rather than assumed ignorance.

## The Three Stages of TTT in Detail

The first Test stage asks students to demonstrate what they already know, and the teacher's primary job is to observe rather than instruct. In the source material, teachers achieve this by asking students to share or display knowledge before any formal explanation has been given. One teacher, for example, spends time with individual students during a science activity, asking questions and prompting them to add more detail to their answers, recording what each child can and cannot produce. In a TTT lesson, a teacher might do something similar: sitting beside a student named Marco and asking him to explain what a word means, listening carefully, and noting whether his answer is complete, partial, or absent. The goal is to gather information about the gap between what students already control and what the lesson needs to address.

The Teach stage is shaped entirely by what the first Test revealed. The source describes teachers who, when a student gives an incorrect answer or no answer at all, respond without scolding, using smiles and patient re-elicitation to guide the student toward the correct form. One teacher does not tell students what a difficult vocabulary item means; instead, she asks questions, mimes, and waits, drawing the meaning out through guided steps. In TTT, this same principle applies: the teacher addresses only the specific points where students struggled, rather than presenting everything from the beginning. If the first Test showed that a student named Yuki could use the target vocabulary in isolation but could not form a correct sentence with it, the Teach stage focuses on sentence-level production, not on the words themselves. The teacher models, re-elicits, and corrects with precision, keeping the instruction narrow and responsive.

The second Test asks students to perform again with the same language, and the teacher now watches to see whether the instruction has taken effect. The source shows teachers using wrap-up activities that revisit the lesson's main focus, asking comprehension questions and observing whether students can produce what was just taught. In one classroom, the final activity reviews target vocabulary through direct questioning and miming, giving the teacher a clear view of who has consolidated the new knowledge and who has not. In a TTT lesson, a teacher might ask the whole class to complete a short task, then circulate and listen, noting whether a student like Yuki can now produce the sentence structure that caused difficulty earlier. The second Test is informal in most classroom settings, but it is purposeful: the teacher is checking against the specific gap that the first Test identified.

Across the three stages, the teacher's role shifts in a consistent direction. During the first Test, the teacher is an observer and diagnostician, holding back information and creating space for students to show what they know. During the Teach stage, the teacher becomes a focused instructor, responding to observed need with targeted explanation, modelling, and re-elicitation. During the second Test, the teacher steps back again, watching student performance and comparing it against the baseline established at the start. TTT differs from a lesson that presents new material and then practises it because the instruction is sandwiched between two moments of genuine student performance, making observed student need the engine that drives what gets taught.

## A Worked Mini-Example and Choosing Between the Two Frameworks

Consider a teacher preparing a forty-minute lesson for a class of seven-year-olds. The vocabulary target is two action verbs, "jump" and "sleep." The teacher has no reason to believe the children have met these words before, so PPP is the natural choice.

The lesson opens with a movement warm-up. The teacher leads the children through a short sequence of physical actions, clapping and chanting to settle them and focus their attention. She then produces two small hand puppets and holds them up one at a time, naming each action clearly while the puppet performs it. The children watch, listen, and begin to repeat. In the practice stage, the teacher moves through controlled chanting and a TPR sequence: she calls out "jump" and every child jumps; she calls out "sleep" and every child folds their hands under a tilted cheek. A student named Mia hesitates on the first round, watches her neighbour, and joins in on the second. By the third repetition she responds without looking around. The lesson closes with a Hot Potato game, in which children pass a soft ball around a circle and the child holding it when the music stops must produce a sentence using one of the target words. This is freer production: the teacher has stepped back, and the children are generating language rather than repeating it. The sequence is linear and teacher-led throughout, which suits the situation precisely because the language is entirely new.

Now consider a different lesson, this time with eight-year-olds who have been studying plants in their science class. The teacher suspects some children already know something about how plants make food, even if their English vocabulary for the concept is incomplete. She begins by asking the class what they already know.

T: "What do plants need to grow?"
S: "Sun. Water."
T: "Good. And what do you think the plant does with the sun?"

Several children offer fragments: "eat it," "drink it," "make food." A student named Omar says confidently, "the leaf does something with the light," but cannot supply the word. The first Test has done its work: the teacher can see that the children hold partial conceptual knowledge and are reaching for language they do not yet have. She introduces the word "photosynthesis," writes it on the board, and guides the class toward its meaning through a series of elicitation questions rather than a direct definition. She points to a chart hanging from the ceiling that shows a diagram of a leaf, sunlight, and arrows, and asks children to connect what they see to what they said moments earlier. Omar is asked to come up and point to the part of the diagram that matches his earlier answer. The second Test follows: the teacher moves among the children, asking individuals to explain in their own words what the diagram shows, and recording which students can use the new word accurately and which still need support.

The choice between these two frameworks comes down to one question: what do the students already know? A lesson built around puppets and action verbs for complete beginners calls for PPP because there is no existing knowledge to surface; the teacher must build the language from the ground up through clear presentation and graduated practice. A lesson on a topic the students have encountered in another subject calls for TTT because asking students to display what they know first makes the instruction more precise and gives students a genuine role in shaping the lesson. The source material notes that the puppet lesson was teacher-centred and that students had limited opportunities to produce language freely; PPP lessons carry that tendency by design. TTT, by contrast, opens with student output and returns to it at the end, making the learner's existing knowledge the starting point rather than an afterthought. Neither framework is superior in the abstract; the decision belongs to the teacher who knows the class, the item, and the moment.
