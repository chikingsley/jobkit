# Writing Good Tests (and Why Testing Teaches)

## What Makes a Test Item Fair and Clear

A well-written test item has three core qualities: it is clear, it is simple, and it is aligned with a stated learning goal (American English, n.d.). Each quality does real work. Clarity means a student can read the item and understand what is being asked before attempting an answer. Simplicity means the item does not introduce vocabulary, grammatical structures, or procedural complexity that would interfere with measuring the intended skill. Alignment means the item targets one specific objective and nothing beyond it.

Alignment deserves particular attention because it is the quality most often violated in practice. Consider a teacher who has spent three lessons helping students give and support opinions in spoken discussion. If the end-of-unit test asks students to write definitions of discourse markers, the item measures something the teacher never formally taught and the students never formally practiced. Yuki might know every discourse marker by name yet struggle to deploy them in real conversation; Carlos might use them fluently in speech yet blank on a written definition. Neither result tells the teacher what she needs to know about communicative readiness.

Course material draws a direct contrast between testing a student's ability to use English for real-world communication and testing only a student's knowledge of English (American English, n.d.). A multiple-choice item asking which linking phrase is grammatically correct tests knowledge. A rubric-scored discussion task, in which Priya leads her group through a structured exchange on a real-world topic, tests performance. Both item types have legitimate uses, but a teacher who relies exclusively on knowledge items will consistently underestimate students whose communicative ability outpaces their metalinguistic awareness, and overestimate students whose test-taking skill masks weak spoken production.

Clarity operates at the level of the individual item and at the level of the whole task. Before a graded discussion, the teacher in course material ensures that students have seen both the teacher rubric and the peer assessment worksheet in advance, so they know precisely how they will be evaluated (American English, n.d.). That transparency is a form of clarity in itself: the student is not guessing at the standard. A brief exchange illustrates the point.

T: "Before we begin, look at criterion three on your sheet. What does it mean to include everyone?"
S: "Like, making sure nobody talks too much and nobody stays quiet the whole time?"
T: "Exactly. That is what I am listening for."

When students can articulate the criterion in their own words, the item has passed the clarity test.

A practical filter for any item before it is finalized runs as follows. First, check clarity: could a student of the target level read this item and state in plain language what it is asking? Second, check simplicity: does the item introduce any difficulty that is not the difficulty being measured? Third, check alignment: does the item correspond to a specific objective written in the lesson plan, with no surplus content attached? Fourth, check authenticity: does the item ask students to do something with English, or only to report what they know about English? An item that passes all four checks is ready to use; an item that fails any one of them should be revised before it reaches students.

## Writing Multiple-Choice and Gap-Fill Items: Dos and Don'ts

Multiple-choice items fail most often at the stem, the part of the item that poses the question or presents the incomplete sentence. Take this flawed example: "Which of the following is correct and also shows that you understand the present perfect tense and when it is used versus the simple past?" Yuki, working through a placement test, reads this and cannot tell whether she is being asked to identify a grammatically correct sentence, to demonstrate knowledge of when the present perfect is used, or to compare two tenses. The item is double-barreled, meaning it tests two separate things at once, and the phrasing "correct and also shows" makes the selection criterion genuinely ambiguous. A corrected version reads: "Choose the sentence that uses the present perfect correctly." That revision removes the comparison with the simple past, states one criterion, and lets the distractor options carry the contrastive work.

Distractors, the wrong answer choices in a multiple-choice item, require the same discipline. Implausible distractors reduce an item to a guessing exercise with an obvious answer. If the target sentence is "She has lived here since 2019" and the distractors include "She live here since 2019" and "She living here since 2019," Wei can eliminate both wrong answers on the basis of elementary subject-verb agreement rather than any knowledge of the present perfect. Each distractor should represent a plausible error that a student at the target level might produce; "She lived here since 2019," for instance, confuses the present perfect with the simple past and therefore tests the intended distinction.

Gap-fill items carry a different but equally serious risk: the blank that accepts too many answers. An item reading "She ______ in Paris for three years" could be completed with "lived," "has lived," "was living," "studied," "worked," or dozens of other verbs, all grammatically acceptable. Fatima, who writes "studied" has produced a perfectly correct sentence, yet a scoring key that lists only "has lived" marks her wrong. The item has failed because the surrounding context does not constrain the answer to the target form. Adding a time reference resolves the problem: "She ______ in Paris for three years, and she is still there today." The phrase "and she is still there today" signals an ongoing situation that began in the past, constrains the blank to the present perfect, and makes the item defensible.

Course material states plainly that assessment tools should be clear, simple, and well aligned with learning goals (American English, n.d.). Each word in that standard applies directly to item writing. Clarity means the student understands what is being asked without needing to interpret the question itself. Simplicity means the only difficulty in the item is the difficulty being measured, not the difficulty of parsing a convoluted stem. Alignment means the item corresponds to a lesson objective and nothing else.

These are not merely technical concerns. The same source identifies washback, the effect that assessment has on learning and teaching, and notes that assessment produces negative washback when students do not see how it relates to their learning (American English, n.d.). A poorly written multiple-choice item that tests reading comprehension of the question rather than grammatical knowledge teaches students that tests are arbitrary. A gap-fill item that penalizes a correct answer because the key was written carelessly teaches students that accuracy does not matter, only guessing the teacher's preferred word. Karpicke and Roediger (2008) demonstrated that the act of retrieval itself drives long-term retention, and every test item therefore functions as a learning event. An item that retrieves the wrong knowledge, or retrieves nothing coherent at all, wastes that event entirely. Writing each item with the same care given to a lesson objective is an instructional decision, not an administrative one.

## Writing Short-Answer Items and Simple Marking Guides

A short-answer item is a written prompt that asks a student to produce a sentence, a short paragraph, or a brief spoken response, and that can be scored against a predetermined set of criteria. The defining quality of a good short-answer item is that it tells the student precisely what to produce, how much to produce, and which language feature is being assessed. When those three elements are absent, students guess at the task and teachers guess at the score.

Picture two versions of the same prompt. The first reads: "Write about the topic." Fatima reads this and produces three sentences about her weekend because nothing in the prompt told her otherwise. The second version reads: "In two or three sentences, explain one advantage of peer feedback for language learners. Use at least one subordinate clause to connect your ideas." Fatima now knows the expected length, the communicative purpose, and the grammatical feature under assessment. Her response is gradable; the first was not. Specificity is what separates the two prompts, and specificity costs the teacher nothing except a moment of deliberate planning.

Course material on rubric design offers a direct model for building a marking guide around short-answer items. The principle stated there is that all learning goals for an activity should correspond to the categories on the rubric (Shaping the Way We Teach English, americanenglish.state.gov). A teacher who has written the focused prompt above has already identified three gradable dimensions: content accuracy, appropriate length, and correct use of subordination. Each of those dimensions becomes one row in a simple marking guide. A five-point Likert scale, where 1 means "needs improvement" and 5 means "excellent," provides enough granularity for consistent scoring without requiring lengthy rater training, as the module describes in its discussion of the peer assessment worksheet and teacher rubric.

In practice, two or three criteria with brief descriptors at each anchor point are sufficient. A marking guide for the prompt above might score content on whether the advantage described is accurate and relevant, score length on whether the response falls within the specified range, and score grammar on whether a subordinate clause is present and correctly formed. A score of 1 on the grammar criterion might read "no subordinate clause attempted or clause is structurally incomplete," while a score of 5 might read "subordinate clause is correctly formed and meaningfully connects the two ideas." A teacher marking twenty papers with this guide can apply it consistently from the first paper to the last.

The module also warns that assessment tools must be well aligned with learning goals, and that misalignment produces negative washback, the term used there to describe the effect that assessment has on learning and teaching. A marking guide that rewards handwriting neatness or penalizes British spelling when the lesson objective was subordinate clause use sends students the message that the wrong things matter. When Daniel receives a low score because his handwriting was judged untidy rather than because his clause was malformed, he revises his handwriting before the next test. The learning goal goes unpracticed. Misalignment of this kind redirects student effort away from the language feature the teacher intended to teach, and the consequences compound over time. Karpicke and Roediger (2008) showed that retrieval events drive long-term retention, so a short-answer item that retrieves the wrong knowledge, scored by a guide that rewards the wrong features, produces a learning event pointing in the wrong direction entirely. Writing the prompt and the marking guide together, before the lesson ends, ensures that what is tested is what was taught.

## The Testing Effect: Why Retrieval Beats Re-Studying

The testing effect is the finding that retrieving information from memory produces stronger long-term retention than spending equivalent time re-reading or reviewing the same material. The term names a phenomenon with direct consequences for how teachers design classroom activities, not merely how they design end-of-unit exams.

Karpicke and Roediger (2008) demonstrated this with particular clarity. In their study, participants learned Swahili-English vocabulary pairs and then either practiced retrieving the words repeatedly or spent the same additional time re-studying them. After one week, the retrieval group recalled approximately 80% of the items correctly; the re-study group recalled approximately 33%. The gap is large enough to matter in any classroom where vocabulary retention is a stated goal.

One detail from that study deserves particular attention. Once a student had successfully recalled an item on the first test, further passive review of that same item produced no measurable improvement in delayed retention (Karpicke & Roediger, 2008). The memory consolidation came from the act of retrieval itself. Priya, who reads her vocabulary list three more times after getting an item right is spending time that would serve her better elsewhere.

The practical implication is that a short quiz at the start of class is not a distraction from learning; it is one of the most efficient learning activities the teacher can provide. Imagine a teacher who opens a lesson with ten gap-fill sentences using vocabulary from the previous session. Daniel struggles to recall "negotiate" in context, writes a near-miss, and then sees the correct form. That retrieval attempt, even an imperfect one, does more for Daniel's retention than another pass through his notes would have done.

Gap-fills, short-answer questions, and oral prompts all qualify as retrieval events in this sense. The format matters less than the cognitive demand: the student must produce the target language from memory rather than recognize it on a page. A teacher who builds two or three such moments into every lesson is not adding assessment to instruction; the retrieval practice is the instruction.

This reframes the role of frequent, low-stakes testing in a TEFL course. Tests of this kind are not checkpoints inserted between learning episodes. They are learning episodes, and the evidence from Karpicke and Roediger (2008) gives teachers a principled reason to treat them as such when planning lessons and explaining their purpose to students.

## Positive and Negative Washback: How Test Design Shapes Learning

Washback is the effect that assessment has on learning and teaching. A test measures what students know, and it also shapes what they study, how they study, and how they feel about the subject. Understanding washback helps a teacher predict whether a given assessment will reinforce good learning habits or undermine them.

Course material identifies four conditions that tend to produce negative washback. Students who are unfamiliar with the form of an assessment, who doubt its validity, who cannot see how it connects to their learning, or who are unprepared for the kind of feedback they will receive are likely to respond with confusion or disengagement rather than productive effort. Suppose Fatima, who has spent weeks practicing spoken discussion skills and then receives a peer assessment worksheet for the first time on the day of the activity, with no prior explanation of the five-point scale. She may dismiss her classmates' scores as arbitrary, ignore the written comments, and learn nothing from the process. The assessment form is sound, but the conditions for negative washback are all present.

Positive washback depends on reversing those conditions. When students see the value of an assessment method, are well prepared to give and receive feedback through it, and can clearly trace a line between the assessment criteria and their language learning goals, the assessment reinforces rather than disrupts learning. Wei, who has studied the peer assessment rubric in advance, practiced scoring a sample discussion with his class, and heard his teacher explain why each criterion matters is far more likely to read a peer's comments carefully and adjust his own performance accordingly.

The same logic applies to written test items. A multiple-choice quiz that asks students to identify the correct article in a sentence stripped of any communicative context may produce negative washback: students like Fatima or Wei may study by memorizing rules in isolation, unable to connect that activity to the speaking and writing they do in class. A gap-fill or short-answer task tied explicitly to a lesson objective, such as completing a short email using the vocabulary set from the previous unit, signals to students that the test reflects real language use. That visible connection is the mechanism through which positive washback operates.

Three practical design principles follow from the module's advice on rubrics and checklists. First, align every test item directly with a stated learning goal, so that students can see the connection between what they practiced and what they are being asked to demonstrate. Second, share the assessment criteria before the test; a simple checklist of what a correct short-answer response should include serves the same orienting function as a rubric does in a speaking activity. Third, keep the criteria plain and the scale manageable, since a clear assessment tool is easier for students to internalize and easier for a teacher to apply consistently. A test built on these principles does not merely measure learning. It points students toward the kind of study that produces it.

## Putting It Together: A Simple Test-Writing Workflow

The workflow below treats test-writing as a sequence of decisions, each one constrained by the decision before it. Working through the steps in order prevents the most common error in classroom testing: writing items first and inventing a rationale for them afterward.

The first step is to write out the lesson's learning goals in plain language before touching a single test item. Every category on a sound rubric corresponds directly to a stated learning goal (American English, n.d.). A teacher whose goal was "students can use the simple past to narrate a sequence of events" already knows what the test must reach; a teacher who skips this step often ends up measuring something adjacent to the goal rather than the goal itself.

The second step is to match item types to those goals. Recognition tasks, such as multiple-choice, suit goals that ask whether a student can identify a correct form or meaning. Gap-fill items suit form-focused production, where the student must supply a specific word or ending. Short-answer items suit meaning-focused production, where the student constructs a response in their own words. Take a class where Priya's goal is to explain the difference between "borrow" and "lend" in context: a short-answer item asking her to write one sentence using each word tests that goal directly, while a multiple-choice item tests only recognition of the distinction.

The third step is to draft the items and run each one against the checklist developed in earlier sections of this unit. Each item should be clear enough that a student who knows the answer is not confused by the wording, and aligned tightly with the goal identified in step one. A useful self-check is to read the item aloud and ask whether a student like Carlos, who has studied the material thoroughly, could fail it for reasons unrelated to the learning goal.

The fourth step is to write the marking guide before administering the test. For short-answer items, a two-point scale works well in most classroom contexts: one point for a response that is partially correct or incomplete, two points for a response that fully meets the stated criterion. Where more discrimination is needed, a three-point scale modeled on the module's logic of "needs improvement, meets expectations, exceeds expectations" (American English, n.d.) gives teachers a manageable range without introducing the inconsistency that wider scales invite.

The fifth step is to consider washback, the effect the assessment has on how students study and what they believe about their own learning (American English, n.d.). A teacher should ask whether students, on seeing the test returned, will understand the connection between each item and the work they did in class. If the answer is uncertain, sharing the marking guide alongside the test results closes that gap. When students can trace each item back to what they were taught and why, the assessment reinforces rather than undermines their sense of progress.

Running this workflow regularly, rather than reserving tests for end-of-term summative moments, produces a compounding benefit. Karpicke and Roediger (2008) found that repeated retrieval of foreign-language vocabulary produced a delayed-recall advantage of roughly 80 percent compared to 33 percent for repeated study, while additional study after a first correct recall added nothing measurable. A short, well-designed quiz built on the steps above therefore does two jobs at once: it tells the teacher what students have learned, and the act of completing it accelerates the retention of what students are still consolidating.
