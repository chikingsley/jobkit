# Spacing and Review Schedules

## Why Spacing Beats Massing: The Core Principle

Massed practice means a learner studies a set of new words intensively in a single block of time, then moves on. A teacher who introduces twenty vocabulary items on Monday, drills them repeatedly for forty minutes, and does not return to them until the end-of-term test is using massed practice. The words receive concentrated attention once, and the learner's memory is left to manage the rest without further support.

Spaced practice, by contrast, means returning to the same material across multiple sessions separated by gaps of time. Instead of one forty-minute block, a teacher might spend ten minutes on the same twenty words on Monday, revisit them briefly on Wednesday, and return again the following week. Each return to the material arrives just as memory of it has begun to weaken, and that act of retrieval under mild difficulty appears to consolidate the learning in ways that unbroken repetition cannot replicate.

The difference in outcome between these two approaches is not trivial. Nakata (2015) compared spaced and massed conditions directly in second-language vocabulary learning and found that spaced practice produced stronger retention than massed practice. A student like Yuki, who reviews a set of words across three short sessions spread over a week, is likely to remember more of them at a delayed test than a student like Daniel, who spends the same total study time on those words in a single sitting.

This finding does not rest on a single study. Kim and Webb (2022) conducted a meta-analysis drawing on 48 experiments with 3,411 participants and confirmed a positive effect of spaced practice on second-language learning across a wide range of contexts and learner populations. The consistency of that result across so many independent experiments gives teachers a reliable basis for reconsidering how they distribute vocabulary work across a course.

The underlying reason spacing works is often explained through the concept of memory consolidation. When a learner encounters a word again after a gap, the brain must reconstruct the memory rather than hold it in active attention. Reconstruction takes effort. That effortful retrieval, even when it feels slightly uncomfortable to the learner, appears to strengthen the memory trace. A student who hesitates before recalling that "accumulate" means to gather gradually over time is doing more useful cognitive work than one who reads the word and its translation five times in a row without pause.

For the classroom teacher, the practical implication is that the scheduling of vocabulary review matters as much as the quality of the initial presentation. Introducing words well on the first day is necessary but not sufficient; the design of what happens in subsequent lessons determines how much of that vocabulary survives into long-term use.

## Equal Spacing vs. Expanding Schedules

Two broad scheduling designs appear in the research on spaced practice. Equal-interval spacing means a learner reviews a word at fixed, uniform gaps: for example, once on Day 1, again on Day 3, again on Day 5, and again on Day 7. Expanding schedules follow a different logic, stretching the gap progressively longer after each successful retrieval, so a word reviewed on Day 1 might return on Day 2, then Day 4, then Day 8. The underlying assumption is that a word retrieved successfully can afford to wait longer before the next review, while the memory trace is still relatively fresh.

Nakata (2015) compared these two approaches directly in the context of second language vocabulary learning and found that expanding schedules showed a limited but significant edge over equal spacing. The word "limited" carries real weight here. Teachers who switch from equal-interval review to an expanding schedule should not expect a dramatic transformation in their students' retention scores. The difference is statistically real, but the margin is not large enough to treat expanding spacing as a superior method that renders equal spacing obsolete.

What the finding does justify is treating expanding gaps as a worthwhile design principle when building review sequences. Consider a teacher working with a class that includes a student named Fatima, an adult learner who has just met the word "legislation" in a general English course. Under an equal-interval plan, the teacher might recycle that word in a warm-up activity every two days across a two-week period. Under an expanding schedule, the teacher would bring the word back after one day, then after three days, then after a week. If Fatima retrieves the word correctly each time, the growing gap is appropriate; the memory trace has been reinforced and can sustain a longer wait.

The practical implication is that neither schedule is wrong. A teacher who finds equal-interval review easier to manage within a fixed timetable is not making a significant error. The expanding schedule offers a modest advantage, and where lesson planning allows for flexible review timing, it is sensible to build in progressively longer gaps after successful retrieval. Both approaches require genuine spacing between encounters; the benefit disappears when review is compressed into a single session (Nakata, 2015).

## What the Research Base Looks Like

When teachers or curriculum coordinators ask why a course is structured around spaced review rather than concentrated practice, it helps to point to a specific body of evidence rather than a general impression. Kim and Webb (2022) conducted a meta-analysis of 48 experiments involving 3,411 participants, examining the effect of spaced practice on second language learning across a range of contexts and learner populations. A meta-analysis is a study that pools and statistically analyzes the results of many independent experiments, allowing researchers to draw conclusions that no single classroom study could support on its own. The breadth of Kim and Webb's dataset means that the positive effect they found for spaced practice is not an artifact of one unusual group of learners or one particular teaching context. Their findings confirm that spacing produces a positive effect on L2 learning (Kim & Webb, 2022).

Nakata (2015), published in Studies in Second Language Acquisition, complements that broad picture by examining a more specific question: whether the type of spacing schedule matters. Where Kim and Webb establish that spacing works across the field, Nakata's study looks at what happens when the intervals between review sessions are equal versus progressively expanding. A learner like Yuki, who reviews new vocabulary on day one, day three, and day five, is following an equal-interval schedule; a learner like Carlos, who reviews on day one, day three, and day seven, is following an expanding one. Nakata found a modest but statistically reliable advantage for expanding schedules over equal spacing in L2 vocabulary retention (Nakata, 2015).

The two sources approach the question from different angles, yet they arrive at the same practical conclusion. Kim and Webb establish the reliability of the spacing effect across a large and varied sample; Nakata refines the picture by showing that schedule shape also carries weight. A teacher explaining to a colleague why vocabulary from last month's unit is reappearing in this week's lesson can draw on both sources with confidence. The evidence base is not a single promising experiment but a convergence of findings across thousands of learners, and that convergence justifies treating spaced review as a principled feature of course design rather than an optional extra.

## Translating Spacing into a Course Structure

Translating that evidence into a workable course structure requires no software, no elaborate tracking system, and no departure from a normal lesson sequence. The core move is to distribute vocabulary review across several lessons rather than concentrating all the work in the lesson where words first appear. When a teacher introduces ten new words on Monday and then moves on to new content for the rest of the week, those words receive massed practice at best, and the spacing effect that Nakata (2015) and Kim and Webb (2022) document has no opportunity to operate.

A simple weekly planner can approximate the expanding schedule Nakata (2015) identifies as slightly more effective than uniform spacing. Suppose Ms. Reyes is running a four-week intermediate unit on environmental topics. In lesson one she introduces twelve target words, spending perhaps twenty minutes on meaning, pronunciation, and a first controlled practice activity. In lesson two, two or three days later, she opens with a five-minute review: students work in pairs to recall definitions before she confirms them with the class. The gap is short because the words are new and consolidation is still fragile.

By lesson four, roughly a week after the introduction, the interval has widened, and Ms. Reyes spends eight minutes at the start of that lesson asking students to use the target words in sentences about a short reading passage. A student named Carlos produces the sentence "The factory's emissions contaminated the river," and Ms. Reyes asks the class whether the word choice is accurate, prompting a brief discussion that deepens processing without consuming the lesson. Such an embedded moment counts as a full spaced practice event; the review does not need to be a formal test or a dedicated activity block.

At the start of the following week, a final review returns to the same vocabulary one more time. The intervals have now expanded from two days, to roughly five days, to eight or nine days, tracing the shape of an expanding schedule. That expanding pattern outperformed equal-interval spacing for second language vocabulary in the comparison by Nakata (2015), if only by a small margin, and the meta-analytic evidence assembled by Kim and Webb (2022) confirms that spaced practice produces a positive effect on L2 learning across a wide range of conditions. A planner built on this four-point sequence is therefore grounded in converging research rather than intuition.

Brief review moments carry genuine value. A five-minute pair activity at the start of a lesson, a single elicitation question that recycles a word from the previous week, or a short written task requiring students to use previously taught vocabulary all constitute spaced practice, provided they occur after a meaningful gap. To capture them, Ms. Reyes does not need to redesign her unit; she needs to reserve small, deliberate slots across the unit's timeline and protect them from being displaced by new content. The planner becomes the mechanism by which the research finding enters the classroom.

## A Practical Low-Tech Review Routine

The routine described here requires no special materials and fits within the first ten minutes of any lesson. A teacher needs the board, whatever vocabulary the course has already introduced, and a habit of returning to old words before presenting new ones. Each return is a spaced retrieval event, and Kim & Webb (2022) confirm across 48 experiments that such events produce a positive effect on second language learning.

The first step is a brief retrieval prompt delivered the moment students settle. The teacher writes three to five words from a previous lesson on the board, words chosen because at least a week has passed since students last encountered them. No definition is provided. Students write, on a scrap of paper, what each word means and one sentence using it. Carlos, working silently for two minutes, might write "accumulate: to collect over time" and then "The river accumulates sediment after heavy rain." The silence matters; each student must search memory independently before any peer support arrives, because the effort of recall, not mere re-exposure, consolidates the word's representation (Nakata, 2015).

The second step is a pair-check phase. Students compare their sentences with a partner and negotiate any differences in meaning or usage before the teacher intervenes. The exchange might run as follows.

T: Compare your sentences with your partner. Do you agree on the meaning?
S: I wrote "collect slowly," but Daniel wrote "build up over time." Are those the same?
T: Talk it through. Which fits the example sentence better?

This peer discussion serves two purposes. Students who retrieved the word successfully consolidate their knowledge by articulating it; students who could not retrieve it receive a low-stakes correction from a classmate rather than from the teacher, keeping the affective cost of forgetting low. The pair phase also mirrors the pair-discussion-before-whole-class-review structure that runs through the *Shaping the Way We Teach English* lesson plans, where students check answers with a partner before the teacher confirms.

The third step is whole-class confirmation. The teacher elicits one or two sentences aloud, writes a correct example on the board, and asks a brief comprehension check question to ensure the class has converged on an accurate meaning. Eliciting form and meaning from students, rather than announcing them, keeps retrieval active rather than passive. The board example stays visible for the remainder of the lesson, so the word remains present in the room's environment even while new content is being taught.

Once this three-step sequence is established as a routine, it can absorb more creative production activities without requiring extra lesson time. On days when the schedule allows five additional minutes, the pair phase can expand into a short chain story. One student begins a sentence using one of the board words, the next student continues the story using a second word, and the group works through the list until every word has appeared in a connected narrative. The chain story format requires students to use words productively rather than to recognize them passively, a more demanding retrieval event by any measure. Nakata (2015) found that spaced practice produces significant advantages over massed practice for second language vocabulary, and a chain story built from words introduced across several previous lessons is, by definition, a spaced production task.

The routine's value lies in its repeatability. A teacher who runs this sequence at the start of every lesson creates a schedule in which every vocabulary item returns to active use multiple times across the unit, with natural gaps between each encounter. No single session is long or elaborate. The cumulative effect of many small, well-spaced retrieval events is what the research supports.

## Planning and Monitoring Your Spacing Schedule

A spacing schedule is only as reliable as the records behind it. Without some form of written tracking, a teacher cannot know whether a word introduced three weeks ago has been reviewed twice or not at all. A simple paper grid serves this purpose well: column headings carry the dates of each lesson, row labels carry the target words, and the teacher marks each cell to show whether a word was retrieved that day and whether the student response was accurate. A teacher like Ms. Reyes might keep such a grid in the inside cover of her lesson planner, updating it in the two minutes after class while the session is still fresh.

The grid also makes gaps visible. If a word like "accelerate" appears in the introduction column dated the third of the month but shows no further marks until the twenty-eighth, the gap is immediately obvious and can be corrected in the next lesson. Reviewing her grid on a Friday, Ms. Reyes might notice that four words from the previous unit have not been retrieved since their introduction; she can then plan a brief chain-story warm-up for Monday targeting those items. A written record tells the teacher where the plan has drifted, and that information alone is enough to prompt a correction.

Deciding when a word is sufficiently consolidated requires a different kind of judgment. The expanding-schedule logic described by Nakata (2015) offers a practical criterion: when a word has survived several retrieval attempts across progressively longer gaps without error, the teacher can reasonably reduce how often it appears in planned review. A word that a student like Priya retrieves correctly after one day, then after three days, then after a week, then after two weeks, has demonstrated the kind of durable storage that no longer requires weekly attention. The teacher can move it to a monthly check rather than removing it from the grid entirely, since occasional reactivation still supports retention.

No grid will ever be perfect. Lessons run long, planned reviews get displaced by unplanned needs, and some words will slip through the record unnoticed for a week or two. That imperfection is not a reason to abandon the system. Kim and Webb (2022), whose meta-analysis drew on forty-eight experiments and more than three thousand participants, found a positive effect for spaced practice across a wide range of instructional conditions, none of which required flawless execution. A teacher who tracks most words most of the time, and who corrects gaps when the grid reveals them, is already working within the conditions that the evidence supports. Consistency across a course, not precision on any single day, is what separates a spacing plan from a spacing habit.
