# Teaching Speaking

## Fluency and Accuracy: Two Goals, Two Approaches

When teachers plan speaking activities, they face a recurring decision about what to prioritize: the smooth, confident production of language or the grammatically precise production of language. Fluency, in this context, means the ability to communicate ideas without excessive hesitation or self-correction. Accuracy means producing language that conforms to grammatical and phonological norms. These two goals are not always compatible within a single activity, and experienced teachers treat the choice between them as a deliberate planning decision rather than an accident of classroom management.

A teacher who wants students to develop fluency will often allow a conversation or discussion to run without interrupting to correct errors. The reasoning is straightforward: stopping a student mid-sentence to address a grammatical mistake breaks the flow of ideas and can make that student, and others watching, reluctant to speak again. Imagine Fatima midway through explaining her opinion about a controversial topic. If the teacher interrupts to correct her verb tense, Fatima may lose her train of thought, feel embarrassed in front of her classmates, and become more cautious about contributing in future lessons. Allowing the discussion to continue signals to Fatima that her ideas have value and that communication is the primary goal of the activity.

A teacher who focuses on content and communication rather than grammatical form is not failing to notice errors; she is making a considered decision about what the lesson is designed to achieve. The goal of the activity, whether developing fluency or developing accuracy, is one of the central factors a teacher must weigh when planning speaking work. A role-play in which students brainstorm solutions to a problem and then perform their scene for the class calls for a different orientation than a controlled drill in which students practice a specific grammatical structure.

Accuracy-focused activities are designed so that students attend closely to the form of what they produce. A game in which players must unscramble a mixed-up question and reconstruct it correctly, as in the Mix It, Fix It activity, keeps students' attention on grammatical structure throughout. Carlos, working with his group to reassemble the words of a question into the right order, is attending to grammatical form in a way that a free discussion does not demand. The task constrains him productively.

Teachers must also consider their students' personalities and confidence levels when deciding which goal to foreground. A class of shy or anxious speakers may need sustained fluency work before accuracy becomes a productive focus. Choosing the right goal for the right moment is one of the more consequential judgments a speaking teacher makes.

## Lowering the Affective Barrier

A student's willingness to speak is shaped by more than vocabulary or grammar knowledge. Personality, shyness, and confidence all affect how readily a learner will attempt to produce spoken English, and a teacher who ignores these factors will find that even well-designed activities fall flat. When assessing speaking, teachers should take into account whether students are shy and how much confidence they bring to the task, because the same activity can feel manageable to one learner and genuinely threatening to another.

One practical way to reduce anxiety before a speaking task is to give students time to write or think through their answers first. Priya, who hesitates when called on without warning, may speak with far greater ease if she has spent two minutes jotting down her ideas before the discussion begins. Preparing in advance removes the pressure of generating language and content simultaneously, which is often what makes spontaneous speaking feel so daunting.

The social arrangement of an activity also matters considerably. Performing in front of the whole class places a student under a kind of scrutiny that small-group work does not, and many learners who stay silent in plenary settings will speak freely when the audience is just two or three peers. Teachers can structure activities so that the more exposed, whole-class phase comes only after students have already rehearsed their ideas in a smaller, lower-stakes setting.

For lower-level students, the transition to English-only speaking can itself be a source of anxiety. One approach is to allow students to use their first language during small-group preparation, then ask them to switch to English when their group presents to the whole class. Carlos, who is still building basic fluency, benefits from working through the content of what he wants to say before he must also manage the challenge of saying it in English.

Open-ended, student-centered activities carry their own motivational advantage in this context. When students are asked to express what they genuinely think rather than produce a single correct answer, they have a personal stake in the conversation. That investment tends to make participation feel worthwhile rather than merely obligatory.

Opinion-based topics can generate strong engagement, and they require careful handling. Students are likely to hold firm views, and a discussion can become heated if the teacher does not actively maintain an atmosphere of respect for different perspectives. The teacher's role is to ensure that all viewpoints receive a fair hearing and to mediate if the exchange becomes too sharp, so that the classroom remains a place where every student feels safe enough to speak.

## Information-Gap Tasks: Getting Learners Talking Through a Purpose

An information-gap task is an activity in which each student holds information that others do not have, and the exchange of that information gives the conversation a real purpose. Activity C in the module illustrates this design clearly: students move around the room asking each other the questions written on their papers and recording their classmates' answers. No single student begins with the full picture, so the talking is not a performance for the teacher but a practical means of completing the task.

The sequence does not end there. Once students have gathered their data, Activity F brings them together to discuss what they found. Priya, for instance, might have collected five different answers to the same question and now needs to compare them with what her classmate Daniel recorded. The discussion that follows is driven by genuine curiosity about the results rather than by a prompt from the teacher.

This two-stage design matters because each stage creates a different kind of speaking demand. In the first stage, students need to ask questions clearly and listen carefully enough to write accurate answers. In the second stage, they need to interpret and compare, which draws on the kind of critical thinking that open-ended, student-centered work is designed to develop. The shift from gathering to discussing means that students are producing different types of language across the same lesson without the teacher having to introduce an entirely new activity.

The teacher's role across both stages is largely one of facilitation rather than instruction. While students are circulating and asking questions, the teacher can monitor for difficulties and note patterns to address later. During the discussion phase, the teacher ensures that all students have an equal opportunity to contribute, a central responsibility in a student-centered classroom. In such a classroom, most of the class time involves students listening to each other and speaking with each other, with the teacher speaking far less than in a traditional lesson.

T: You have five minutes to ask your questions and write down what people say. After that, we'll talk about what you found.
S: (to a classmate) What did you write for question three?
S: I said twice a week. What about you?

This kind of exchange, repeated across the room with different pairs, produces a high volume of student talk in a short time. Because the students need each other's answers to complete the task, the conversation has a built-in reason to continue, and the teacher is freed from being the sole source of input and response.

## Role-Play Tasks: From Problem to Performance

A role-play task, as used here, is a speaking activity in which students take on assigned or chosen characters and act out a scenario, either one they have been given or one they have constructed themselves. The module describes a two-phase model that separates preparation from performance, and this separation is central to how the activity works.

In the first phase, students read a short problem and work in small groups to create a role-play that offers a solution. Fatima, for example, might read a scenario in which a neighbor's loud music is disturbing a family trying to sleep. Her group decides who will play the neighbor, who will play the family member, and what the conversation between them might look like. This preparation stage asks students to think critically about the problem, weighing what a realistic solution might be, and creatively about how to dramatize it, choosing words, tone, and character behavior. Activities of this kind require both critical and creative thinking, because students must support a position rather than produce a single correct answer.

The second phase is the performance itself, when groups present their role-plays to the rest of the class. Carlos, who has been quiet during pair work, may find that stepping into a character gives him a frame for speaking that reduces self-consciousness. The audience, meanwhile, is engaged because each group has worked from the same problem but may have arrived at a different solution, so the performances invite comparison and discussion. The two phases work together: the preparation gives students enough security to perform, and the performance gives the preparation a genuine purpose.

The TV Talk Show activity extends this model into a more elaborate format. Talk Show Hosts from all groups work together to brainstorm questions based on a picture card, while Celebrity Guests, in a separate group, decide which real or invented personalities they will play and develop details such as name, speaking style, and body language. When the groups reassemble, each presents a complete talk show to the class, with hosts introducing guests, raising a topic, and asking questions while guests respond in character. The preparation stage here is longer and more structured than in the problem-solution role-play, but the underlying logic is the same: students invest time in creative character work before they are asked to perform.

Both activities share three features that distinguish them from simpler speaking tasks. Students have time to prepare, so the performance is not an improvisation under pressure. They make creative decisions about characters or scenarios, which means the language they produce is genuinely their own rather than a repetition of a model. The final stage is a public performance, which raises the stakes just enough to motivate careful preparation without making the activity feel like a test.

T: Your group has decided the neighbor refuses to turn down the music. What does the family member do next?
S: Maybe she calls the landlord? Or she offers to buy him headphones.
T: Either could work. Build that into your scene.

This brief exchange illustrates how the teacher's role during preparation is to prompt thinking rather than supply answers, keeping the creative and critical work with the students.

## Managing Speaking Time: Groupings and Interaction Patterns

When students speak one at a time in front of the whole class, only one voice is active while twenty or more learners wait. The module's activity table maps a range of interaction patterns, from individuals addressing the class to pairs, small groups, and small groups presenting to the whole class. This range exists because different groupings produce very different amounts of student talk. A teacher who relies mainly on whole-class formats is, in effect, rationing speaking time rather than distributing it.

Pair work and small-group work change that ratio at once. When a class of twenty-four students splits into groups of three, eight conversations run simultaneously, and every student is either speaking or listening at close range to a peer. In the activity table, the survey discussion and the role-play preparation both use small groups for this reason: the task requires each student to contribute rather than allowing quieter members to stay silent while a confident classmate holds the floor. A student like Yuki, who rarely volunteers in whole-class settings, will often speak at length when her audience is two classmates rather than the entire room.

Reducing the audience also reduces performance pressure, and the two effects reinforce each other. Moving students into small groups instead of having them speak in front of the whole class is a direct way to increase participation. A student who freezes when asked to address the room may speak fluently in a group of four, and that fluency, practiced repeatedly in low-stakes conditions, is what eventually makes the higher-stakes whole-class presentation manageable. The talk show activity illustrates the progression: students prepare in groups before any public performance takes place.

The teacher's role in this kind of classroom is not passive. It means ensuring that all students have an equal opportunity to interact with classmates and that every student understands the purpose of each activity. In practice, this means circulating during group work and noticing when one student is doing most of the talking, then redirecting without disrupting the group's momentum.

T: Carlos, you've been the note-taker. What's your opinion on this one?
S: I think the second solution is better because it costs less.
T: Tell the group why.

That small intervention shifts the balance without interrupting the group's flow, keeping speaking time distributed rather than concentrated in the hands of the most confident students.

## A Toolkit of Concrete Speaking Activities

A brainstorm-and-survey opener gives students an immediate reason to speak because they are responding to information that concerns them directly. In Activity A, students brainstorm ideas about television and then react to survey results about TV viewing habits. The whole class discusses together, so every student hears a range of opinions before anyone is asked to defend a position. Yuki, for example, might discover that her viewing habits differ sharply from the class average and feel genuinely motivated to explain why. The speaking practice here is open discussion, with students responding to real data in real time rather than producing rehearsed language.

The opinion word-card activity shifts the mode from group discussion to individual public speaking. In Activity D, each student selects a word from a pile, places it on the board, and gives an opinion about that topic to the class. The procedure is simple enough to run without lengthy setup, yet it requires each student to organize a thought and deliver it to an audience. Daniel might draw the word "technology" and need to commit to a position on the spot; that is a different cognitive demand from chatting in a small group. This format gives every student at least one moment of uninterrupted speaking time in front of peers.

Paraphrase It, a game that has students say the same thing in a variety of different ways, targets a specific and transferable skill: expressing a single meaning through different grammatical structures. One student produces a sentence, and others must restate it without repeating the original wording. Fatima might say "I enjoy cooking on weekends," and her partner rephrases it as "Cooking is something Fatima likes to do when she has free time." The activity builds flexibility rather than narrow accuracy, encouraging students to reach for structures they might otherwise avoid.

Word Strings develops sentence construction incrementally, making it accessible to students at lower proficiency levels while still engaging stronger students. Players sit in groups of three or four, take a vocabulary picture card, and build a sentence one word or phrase at a time: the first player names a word, the second adds a word to make a two-word phrase, the third extends it to three words, and so on until the group has a complete sentence. In the module's example, "baseball" becomes "baseball player," then "the baseball player," then "the tall baseball player hits," and the chain continues. Yuki, who hesitates to speak at length, can contribute a single word and still participate meaningfully in the construction of something larger.

Taken together, these four formats cover opinion expression, open discussion, paraphrasing, and sentence-level construction. Variety of activities is a key planning consideration, and these examples illustrate why: different formats place different cognitive and linguistic demands on students, so a lesson built from only one type will leave some skills unpracticed and some students underserved.
