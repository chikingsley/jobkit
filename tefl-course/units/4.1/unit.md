# The Communicative Approach and Its Rivals: A Short, Honest History

## Why Methods History Matters (and Why It's Usually Told Wrong)

The standard account of language teaching history reads like a relay race. Grammar-Translation passes the baton to the Direct Method, which passes it to Audiolingualism, which eventually yields to the Communicative Approach, each handoff presented as a correction of the errors that came before. That narrative is tidy and misleading. Methods rarely disappear when theorists declare them obsolete, classrooms rarely conform to a single approach, and the teachers doing the actual work have generally been more pragmatic than the textbooks written about them.

The triumphalist version of this history also tends to flatten the evidence. When Stephen Krashen's Input Hypothesis gained wide influence in the 1980s, it reshaped curriculum design across many contexts, yet Gregg (1984) demonstrated that the hypothesis, as Krashen had stated it, was not empirically testable in any rigorous sense. That does not make comprehensible input an irrelevant concept. Krashen remains a historically significant figure whose ideas deserve careful handling rather than uncritical adoption or wholesale dismissal, and the field has not always managed that balance well.

Practitioner materials have told a different story. The observation-based teacher development resources produced by the U.S. Office of English Language Programs ask teachers to work through a cycle of observe, evaluate, adapt, and act, a design philosophy that assumes no single method will transfer intact from one classroom to another. A teacher watching a video of a bilingual salmon unit in a Seattle primary school is not being told to replicate that lesson; she is being asked to extract what is transferable to her own context. That assumption of principled eclecticism runs through those materials at every level, from the flexibility built into activity selection to the explicit encouragement to modify and adapt. Theorists were fighting method wars while the profession's own training resources were already treating fixed methods as a starting point rather than a destination.

Consider a concrete example. Ms. Reyes is working with teenage beginners in a large class with limited resources. She uses a chant to fix a grammar pattern, a technique associated with behaviorist repetition drill, and then asks students to use that pattern in a short paired exchange about their own lives.

T: "So you just chanted 'I go to school by bus.' Now tell your partner, is that true for you?"
S: "No, I walk. It is very far."

Ms. Reyes has not chosen a method. She has chosen what works for this group, at this moment, with these materials, and the distinction matters because it reflects how skilled teachers have always operated when left to make their own professional judgments.

What follows traces the main methods in roughly chronological order, notes where theoretical claims outran the evidence, and arrives at the position most reflective practitioners already occupy. No winner will be declared.

## Grammar-Translation and Audiolingualism: The Methods That Came Before

For most of the nineteenth century and well into the twentieth, the dominant way to teach a foreign language in formal schooling was grammar-translation. The method treated language primarily as a body of written text to be decoded. A teacher would present an explicit grammatical rule, students would memorize paradigms and vocabulary lists, and the central classroom activity was rendering a passage from the target language into the learner's mother tongue, or the reverse. For classical languages such as Latin and Ancient Greek, this approach had genuine logic behind it: the goal was access to literary and philosophical texts, not conversation with native speakers. A student who could parse a sentence from Cicero and produce an accurate English equivalent had fulfilled the curriculum's stated purpose.

The practical limits appeared when the same method was applied to living languages with spoken communication as the goal. A student named Carlos might spend three years studying French grammar-translation style, able to identify a subjunctive clause and translate a passage from Flaubert, yet unable to ask for directions in Paris. The method had optimized for one skill, reading, and treated speaking and listening as secondary concerns, if it addressed them at all. Writing, when it appeared, was largely the writing of translations rather than original composition. The integrated treatment of all four skills, reading, writing, speaking, and listening as mutually reinforcing activities, was not part of the design.

Audiolingualism emerged in the mid-twentieth century as a direct response to that gap. Drawing on behaviorist psychology, its theorists argued that language learning was a process of habit formation: expose learners to a stimulus, require a response, reinforce correct responses through repetition, and fluency would follow. The classroom became a site of pattern drills. A teacher would model a sentence structure, students would repeat it in chorus, then substitute items according to a fixed frame.

T: "He walks to school. Repeat."
S: "He walks to school."
T: "She."
S: "She walks to school."

The method had real strengths. Pronunciation received systematic attention, and learners built a degree of automatic control over high-frequency structures. Military language programs during the Second World War, which needed to produce functional speakers quickly, found the intensive oral drill format genuinely useful for that specific purpose.

The limits were equally real. Drills produced accurate repetition without producing flexible use. A student named Yuki could complete a substitution drill on past-tense verbs without error, then fall silent when asked to narrate a personal experience, because narrating required selecting structures rather than substituting within a given frame. The behaviorist model also had no satisfying account of how learners handled novel sentences they had never drilled, a problem that became harder to ignore as cognitive linguistics developed through the 1960s. Krashen's later Input Hypothesis, which proposed that learners acquire language through exposure to comprehensible input rather than through drill, was historically influential as a challenge to audiolingual assumptions, though Gregg (1984) demonstrated that the strong version of that hypothesis was not empirically testable as stated.

Neither grammar-translation nor audiolingualism should be read as the product of careless thinking. Each addressed a real pedagogical problem: one sought to give learners access to a literary tradition, the other sought to build spoken fluency where grammar-translation had failed. The teachers who used these methods were working within the best available theory of their time, and elements of both approaches, explicit grammar explanation and controlled practice of new structures, persist in contemporary classrooms for good reasons. What each method struggled to provide was a classroom environment in which all four language skills developed together, in service of genuine communicative need.

## The Communicative Turn: What Changed and Why

The shift that gathered force through the 1970s and into the 1980s rested on a single reorientation: language is not a set of forms to be memorized and then applied, but a social practice acquired through use. Communicative Language Teaching, or CLT, takes this as its organizing premise. A learner who can recite the paradigm of the present perfect but cannot ask a colleague for directions has learned something, yet has not learned to communicate. CLT redirected classroom activity toward the kind of meaning-making that language serves in daily life.

Several principles follow from that premise. Lessons are built around authentic communication goals, meaning that students are asked to do something with language rather than merely to produce correct forms on demand. A teacher working with intermediate teenagers might organize a lesson around the task of planning a neighborhood event, so that future tense forms arise because students genuinely need to make proposals, not because the textbook has reached Unit 9. Learner-centered activity is a second principle: students spend more time producing and negotiating language than listening to the teacher explain it. The four skills, reading, writing, speaking, and listening, are treated as integrated rather than as separate subjects, because real communication rarely isolates one from the others.

Practitioner materials developed in this tradition show these principles embedded in actual lesson design. The art-based resource *Create to Communicate* states plainly that most activities are "organized around an authentic communication goal and discrete language structures," with the communication goal named first. The observation guides in *Shaping the Way We Teach English* repeatedly describe lessons structured around integrated language skills, where a single theme, such as a unit on sockeye salmon, draws on reading, discussion, writing, and listening within the same class period. These are not theoretical statements; they are design decisions made by teachers planning real lessons for real students.

The intellectual framework for this turn came from several directions at once. Hymes (1972) supplied the founding concept, communicative competence: knowing a language means knowing not only its grammar but when, where, and with whom particular forms are appropriate. Wilkins (1976) translated that insight into syllabus design, proposing that courses be organized around notions and functions, meanings and communicative acts such as requesting or apologizing, rather than around a sequence of grammatical structures. Canale and Swain (1980) then gave CLT its most widely cited framework, analyzing communicative competence into grammatical, sociolinguistic, and strategic components that curriculum designers and test writers could actually work with. Stephen Krashen's contribution belongs to a related but distinct line. His Input Hypothesis, the centerpiece of what became the Natural Approach, proposed that learners acquire language when they are exposed to input slightly beyond their current level, what he called comprehensible input. The classroom implication was significant: a teacher who provides rich, contextualized reading and listening at the right level of challenge is doing more to drive acquisition than one who drills isolated verb endings. Krashen's influence on the profession through the late 1970s and 1980s was substantial, and his ideas gave communicative teachers a theoretical vocabulary for what many had already sensed through experience.

The difficulty is that Krashen's broader Monitor Model, of which the Input Hypothesis is one part, was built on constructs that could not be tested in the way scientific claims require. Gregg (1984) argued in a detailed critique that the model's central distinctions, between acquisition and learning, between the monitor and the acquired system, were not defined with enough precision to be falsified by evidence. A theory that cannot in principle be shown to be wrong offers limited guidance when classroom results are mixed. Comprehensible input matters as a practical principle, but the strong theoretical architecture Krashen erected around it was shakier than its popularity suggested (Gregg, 1984). CLT entered the mainstream carried partly by a framework that was more persuasive than it was empirically secure.

## Grand Theories and Their Limits: An Honest Accounting

The history of language teaching is, in part, a history of confident prescriptions. Audiolingualism promised that habit formation through drilling would produce fluent speakers; the Silent Way insisted that teachers should speak as little as possible; Suggestopedia claimed that the right physical environment could accelerate acquisition. Each approach arrived with a coherent rationale, and each eventually met classroom realities that the rationale had not anticipated. The pattern is not accidental. Grand theories tend to be built on observations made in controlled or idealized conditions, and classrooms are neither controlled nor idealized.

Gregg's critique of Krashen illustrates what it means for a theory to outrun its evidence (Gregg, 1984). The Monitor Model made sweeping claims about how humans acquire language, stated with enough vagueness that no classroom result could decisively confirm or refute them. A teacher whose students improved could credit comprehensible input; a teacher whose students stagnated could blame affective filters. When a theory can absorb any outcome, it has stopped functioning as a scientific hypothesis and started functioning as a belief system. Krashen's influence on communicative teaching was real and, in practical terms, partly productive, but the theoretical foundation was less solid than the confidence of its proponents implied (Gregg, 1984).

The materials that teachers use in classrooms tend to reflect a more cautious understanding of this problem. The observation guides in Shaping the Way We Teach English ask teachers to watch a lesson, evaluate what they see through peer discussion and individual reflection, and then decide what to adapt for their own context. The sequence is deliberate: observation comes before judgment, and judgment comes before adoption. No method is handed down as settled truth. A teacher watching a video of collaborative group work in a bilingual American classroom is not told that collaborative group work is universally effective; she is asked whether it could work with her students, in her room, given her constraints.

The art-based resource Create to Communicate builds the same caution into its architecture. Adaptability is listed as a core design feature, and most activities include modification suggestions precisely because the designers recognized that no single version of an activity fits every learner or every setting. A sculpture project that works well for intermediate secondary students in one country may need substantial revision for beginner primary students elsewhere. The resource treats this as a permanent condition to be managed, not a problem awaiting a final solution.

New TEFL teachers sometimes experience the gap between theory and classroom reality as a personal failing, as evidence that they have not yet mastered the right method. The more accurate interpretation is that the gap is structural. The field has produced genuinely useful knowledge about how languages are learned, but that knowledge does not translate cleanly into universal prescriptions. Carlos, who thrives when asked to negotiate meaning in open-ended tasks, may sit next to Yuki, who finds the same activity disorienting without more explicit structural support. Both responses are real data. A single grand theory is unlikely to account for both, and understanding this early is not a reason for pessimism; it is the beginning of the kind of reflective, evidence-aware practice that the field's best resources have consistently modeled.

## Total Physical Response, Content-Based Instruction, and Other Contenders

By the 1970s and 1980s, several approaches were developing in parallel with the communicative turn, each drawing on a different account of how learners acquire language. Total Physical Response, or TPR, is a technique in which learners respond to spoken commands with physical movement before they are asked to produce language themselves. A teacher working with five-year-olds in a Costa Rican EFL classroom might say "Jump!" or "Touch your nose!" and wait for the children to act rather than speak. The observation guides describe a lesson of precisely that kind, one where movement, repetition, and varied activity types work together to engage very young learners and reinforce target language without demanding premature verbal output. The underlying logic is that physical response reduces anxiety and builds a receptive foundation, much as a child understands speech before producing it.

Content-Based Instruction, known as CBI, organizes language learning around subject-matter content rather than around grammatical sequences or communicative functions. The language remains the learning target, but it arrives through the study of something else. In one observed lesson, eight- and nine-year-old students in a United States ESOL classroom worked on a project centered on the Pilgrims' voyage across the Atlantic, using role-play, pair work, and visual aids to engage with both the historical content and the English needed to discuss it. A teacher in that room might say:

T: What problems did the Pilgrims face on the ship?
S: They had no food. The water was bad.
T: Good. Can you say that as a complete sentence?
S: They did not have enough food.

The exchange functions as a history lesson and a grammar lesson simultaneously, with neither goal sacrificed for the other.

Project-Based Learning, or PBL, extends that logic further by asking students to produce a concrete outcome, a poster, a presentation, or a model, over a sustained period. One observed classroom shows students working in pairs to complete a poster project, with visual aids around the room serving as scaffolding throughout. PBL asks learners to use language for a genuine purpose within the classroom, connecting it to communicative principles while also borrowing the content-focus of CBI.

Viewed together, these approaches reveal that practicing teachers were already combining them well before theorists gave the combination a name. A primary teacher in Costa Rica used TPR alongside songs and games in a single thirty-minute lesson. A bilingual classroom in the United States wove integrated skills, collaborative group work, and content instruction into one coherent sequence. The observation guides document such mixing as ordinary professional behavior, not methodological confusion. A five-year-old responding physically to commands before she can form sentences and a nine-year-old drafting a poster caption about historical migration are both acquiring language through purposeful engagement with meaning; the theoretical traditions behind their activities differ, yet the classroom reality they share remains consistent.

## Principled Eclecticism: Where the Field Landed

Principled eclecticism holds that no single method is universally superior and that effective teachers draw on multiple approaches, selecting techniques according to their learners' needs, their teaching context, and the best available evidence. The conclusion is considered, not evasive. Every major method surveyed in this unit arrived with confident claims and departed, or at least retreated, when classroom reality proved more complicated than the theory had anticipated.

The observation materials examined here are built around a repeating cycle of observe, evaluate, adapt, and act. That structure is not incidental to the resource's design; it is the resource's central argument about what professional teaching looks like. A teacher who watches a video of a bilingual classroom in Costa Rica, reflects on what she sees, and then modifies one technique for her own context in Hanoi is not following a method. She is exercising judgment, and the framework assumes that judgment is both possible and necessary. No script is provided because no script could anticipate every classroom.

The same philosophy operates at the level of individual lesson design in the art-based resource examined alongside the observation guides. Its activities are, in the authors' own words, "designed to be used independently rather than sequentially, giving the teacher the flexibility to choose an activity that best fits the desired topic, language focus, and available art materials of the language classroom." Ms. Reyes, working with low-intermediate students on prepositions of location, selects a collage activity not because a syllabus mandates it but because her students respond well to visual tasks and her classroom has the materials. The resource hands her a principled option; she supplies the contextual reasoning.

The field's grand theoretical moments often outran their evidence. Krashen's Input Hypothesis was historically influential in directing attention toward meaning-rich, comprehensible input, but Gregg (1984) demonstrated that the strong version of the hypothesis is not empirically testable as stated and cannot be confirmed or refuted by classroom data in any straightforward way. A teacher who builds her entire practice on a single untestable theory is not being rigorous; she is being incurious. The honest professional response is to treat theoretical frameworks as lenses rather than laws, useful for directing attention but always subject to revision when Daniel sits in front of you and the predicted outcome does not appear.

Flexibility, systematic reflection, and a genuine willingness to evaluate what works in a specific room with specific students are marks of a teacher who has understood the history of the field and drawn the right conclusion from it. Certainty is not the goal. Calibrated, evidence-informed judgment is.
