# Teaching Techniques and Vocabulary Activities

## Presenting Vocabulary Meaning: Context, Pictures, and Realia

When a new word appears for the first time, learners need some way to connect its sound and spelling to its meaning. Three broad channels serve this purpose: context, pictures, and realia. Context means placing the word inside a meaningful situation, such as a story, a song, or a short skit, so that the surrounding language carries enough information for the learner to infer what the word means. Pictures create a visual anchor that links the printed or spoken form of a word to an image. Realia refers to physical objects or physical models that give learners a tangible referent they can see, touch, and manipulate.

Each channel works differently in the classroom. When a teacher uses a song as the vehicle for new vocabulary, as in the Thailand classroom described in the source material, the melody and the narrative together supply the context that makes word meaning accessible. A student named Carlos, for instance, might not know the word "valley" in isolation, but hearing it in a verse about a journey through mountains and valleys gives him enough surrounding meaning to form a working definition. Context is especially effective when the situation is one the learner finds engaging or personally relevant.

Pictures operate by giving the eye something to hold. A teacher might ask students to draw their own images representing new computer vocabulary, or to label the parts of a diagram showing the inside of a machine. When a student named Priya draws and labels a diagram of a computer's components, she is building a personal visual record that she can return to later; the act of constructing the image does work that passive reading does not. Cutting out pictures from magazines or printed materials serves the same purpose, and the physical act of selecting and arranging images adds a further layer of engagement.

Realia takes the visual anchor one step further by making the referent three-dimensional. Asking students to disassemble a real computer, or to construct a cardboard model of one, places the vocabulary inside a physical experience. A student named Wei who has taken apart a keyboard and named each part in English carries a memory trace qualitatively different from one formed by reading a dictionary definition. Building a model from cardboard achieves something similar when the real object is unavailable or impractical to bring into the classroom.

The source material raises a direct question about why giving students choices about how they learn matters, and the range of options it lists, from writing words in sand to imagining a voice whispering them, signals that no single presentation channel reaches every learner equally well. Variety matters. Offering vocabulary through more than one channel in the same lesson increases the likelihood that each student finds at least one route into the new word. Before selecting a presentation method, teachers can gather information about student preferences and interests through a simple survey or a class discussion, so that the choices they make reflect the actual learners in the room rather than a generic assumption about how vocabulary is best taught.

## Organizing Words: Families, Collocations, and Topic Sets

When learners encounter new words in isolation, those words are easy to forget. Organizing vocabulary into networks gives each new item connections to other items already stored in memory, and three types of network are especially practical for classroom use: word families, collocations, and topic sets.

A word family groups words that share a grammatical relationship, such as all the nouns in a lesson, all the verbs, or all the irregular past-tense forms. The Word Bricks collection described in Activate: Games for Learning American English is built on this principle. The printed list separates items into labeled categories: nouns, regular verbs, irregular verbs, modal verbs, prepositions, conjunctions, and so on. When a student named Wei sorts a handful of bricks and places "ran," "bought," and "ate" together because they are all irregular past-tense forms, she is building a mental category that will help her recognize and produce similar forms later. Teachers who create their own bricks can extend this by adding new vocabulary from each textbook chapter into the appropriate grammatical family, so the collection mirrors the grammar the class is currently studying.

Collocations are fixed or strongly preferred combinations of words, such as "make a decision" or "take a photograph," and learners often struggle with them because they cannot be predicted from the meanings of the individual words alone. The Activate guidance on expanding a Word Bricks collection addresses this problem directly by recommending that teachers add function words alongside content vocabulary: articles, auxiliary verbs, modal verbs, prepositions, and clause markers such as "because," "when," and "so." These are the glue words that bind content items into grammatically complete structures. Without them, a student like Carlos can produce "cat drink water" but not "the cat can drink the water." Keeping a generous supply of function bricks in the collection means that when students build sentences during activities, they are practicing the collocational patterns that make those sentences sound natural.

Topic sets group words by subject matter rather than by grammar, and they are particularly effective when the words come directly from a shared classroom experience. The Create to Communicate scratchboard activity provides a clear model. After students finish their artwork and compile a vocabulary list on the board, the teacher is instructed to emphasize that the collected words all belong to a related group or category. That single instructional move transforms a list into a topic set. A student named Yuki, looking at twelve words about weather that her class has just illustrated and discussed, can see that the words form a coherent field of meaning rather than twelve separate items to memorize one by one.

All three types of network benefit from being kept visible over time. The Activate DIY section recommends posting an ongoing list of new words in the room and adding to it as words arise in discussions or lessons. When that list is organized by family, collocation pattern, or topic set, students can return to it between lessons, notice gaps, and suggest additions themselves. A topic set that begins with five words in week one may grow to fifteen by week four, and each addition reinforces the words already there.

## Active Practice: Chain Stories and Word Poems

Once the vocabulary has been displayed and discussed, students need opportunities to use the words in connected, self-generated language rather than recognizing them on a page. Two structured production activities from *Create to Communicate* serve this purpose well: the chain story and the vocabulary word poem.

The chain story works as a group activity for three or four students. One member opens the story with a sentence, or part of a sentence, that contains one of the vocabulary words from the board. The next student continues, either completing the sentence, extending it, or beginning a new one, and must bring in another vocabulary word as the story moves forward. The group keeps building in this way, each member contributing in turn, until every word on the board has appeared in the story. A group working with weather vocabulary, for instance, might produce an exchange such as: T: "Remember, every word on the board must appear before the story ends." S: "There was a sudden drought, and the farmers stood at the edge of their fields." S: "The humidity had vanished overnight, leaving only cracked earth." The story belongs to the group, and its direction is entirely their own. When a group finishes, they may run through the activity a second time, constructing a different chain story using the same word set; this repetition gives students a second pass at the vocabulary without the exercise feeling mechanical, because the narrative will take a new shape. After both rounds, groups volunteer to re-tell their stories to the class, giving the vocabulary one further airing in a public context.

The vocabulary word poem closes the lesson and can be completed alone, with a partner, or in a small group, depending on the teacher's preference. Each student selects one vocabulary word from the board and writes it vertically down the left margin of the paper, one letter per line. Against each letter, the student writes a word, a phrase, or a complete sentence. The teacher decides which of those three options is appropriate for the class: a lower-level group might write single words, while an intermediate group such as the Thai teenagers described in the *Shaping the Way We Teach English* video context would be ready to write full sentences. A student named Priya, working with the word *river*, might write: *Rushing past the old bridge / Into the valley below / Villagers draw water each morning / Every season it changes color / Reflecting the sky at dusk.* The constraint of the vertical word gives students a clear structure to work within while still leaving the content open.

Both activities place the learner in the position of a producer rather than a receiver. The chain story demands that students hold the full word list in mind, monitor what has already been used, and construct meaning that connects one contribution to the next. The word poem asks for a different kind of attention, focusing on a single item and finding language that genuinely belongs to it. Because the chain story can be repeated with the same words, a teacher who has limited time can run one round and return to a second round in a later lesson, using the same board list without any additional preparation.

## Games from the Activate Bank: Brick Bingo and Word Category Bingo

Brick Bingo and Word Category Bingo are two games drawn from the Activate games bank that give students repeated, low-pressure exposure to vocabulary through a familiar competitive format. In Brick Bingo, each player or small group builds a card by selecting Word Bricks from the class collection and arranging them in a 3×3 grid. The teacher calls out words one at a time, and players remove any matching brick from their card. Winning patterns include a complete row, column, or diagonal, as well as the four corners of the card; because multiple patterns are valid, several teams can call "Bingo!" in a single round before the game finally ends when one team clears every brick from the card and shouts "White Out!" Teachers who want a longer session can extend the card to a 4×4 grid, which increases the number of bricks each team must manage and keeps the activity running for a fuller class period.

Word Category Bingo follows the same calling and matching procedure, but it changes how players build their cards. Instead of choosing any bricks they like, players must find bricks that belong to a single grammatical or lexical category specified before the round begins. In Preposition Bingo, for instance, a student named Priya would search through the collection for items such as "in," "on," and "by," placing only those on her card. The teacher might prompt the search with a brief example: T: "Find me a word that shows where something is, like 'on the table.'" S: "Is 'with' a preposition?" T: "Yes, good find." That short exchange confirms understanding of the category before play begins.

The Activate source distinguishes between basic categories and more challenging ones. Basic categories include nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs, articles and pronouns, prepositions, and words of three letters or fewer. More demanding categories ask players to discriminate within those broad classes: countable nouns versus non-countable nouns, irregular past-tense verbs, abstract words, and words that can function as both a noun and a verb. A teacher working with a lower-level group might run Noun Bingo one week and Verb Bingo the next, while an intermediate class could attempt Abstract Word Bingo, where a student like Carlos must decide whether "freedom" qualifies before placing it on his card.

Because both games draw from the same physical collection of Word Bricks, the category choice is the primary lever for adjusting difficulty. A teacher can run a straightforward round for one group and a more demanding round for another without preparing separate materials. The Activate DIY section recommends keeping a running master list of every word added to the class collection, and this list becomes especially useful for Bingo variants, since the teacher needs to know which words are available before calling them. Without that list, a teacher risks calling a word that no brick in the current collection represents, which stalls the game and confuses players. Maintaining the list takes only a few minutes after each session in which new bricks are added, and it pays back that time every time either Bingo game is played.

## Games from the Activate Bank: Describe and Guess, and Word Category Sprint

Two further games from the Activate bank extend the collection's usefulness by targeting different cognitive skills, and both require nothing beyond the Word Bricks already in the classroom.

Describe and Guess asks players to sit in groups of three to five. Each player in turn selects a Word Brick from the group's pile and describes one of the words on it without saying the actual word aloud. The remaining players try to guess the word from the description alone. A student named Fatima, for example, might pick up a brick showing the word "mountain" and offer a description along these lines:

T: Fatima, go ahead.
S (Fatima): It's something very tall found in nature, somewhere people climb and where snow tends to settle at the top.
S (Daniel): Is it a mountain?
S (Fatima): Yes!

The game continues with the next player selecting a brick and describing a word. Because players must find their own words to explain a concept, the activity pushes them to draw on vocabulary they already know and to think carefully about meaning, context, and distinguishing features. A group of four students can cycle through a substantial number of bricks in a single session, and the competitive element of guessing quickly keeps engagement high throughout.

The Word Category Sprint shifts the demand from production to recognition. The teacher calls out a word category and starts a two-minute timer; teams then search through their set of Word Bricks to find as many words as possible that belong to that category. When the timer ends, each team reports how many matching bricks they found. Categories can range from straightforward ones such as nouns or verbs to more demanding ones such as irregular past-tense verbs or words that can function as both nouns and verbs. Before starting the timer, the teacher may need to give one or two examples of the category, particularly when it is less familiar:

T: The category is irregular past-tense verbs. For example, "ran" and "ate" are both irregular past-tense verbs. Ready? Go.

Running multiple rounds requires no preparation beyond calling a new category after each two-minute sprint. The game rewards students who have internalized grammatical categories, and it gives the teacher a quick informal sense of which distinctions the class has absorbed and which still need attention.

Taken together, the two games cover meaningfully different cognitive ground while drawing on the same physical set of bricks. The Sprint asks students to recognize and sort words by category, a receptive skill that depends on stored grammatical knowledge. Describe and Guess asks students to produce language spontaneously in order to convey meaning without using the target word itself, a productive skill that mirrors real communicative pressure. A teacher who runs both games in the same lesson gives students practice at two distinct levels of word knowledge without preparing any additional materials or reorganizing the classroom between activities.

## Building and Expanding the Word Bricks Collection

A Word Bricks collection does not arrive fully formed on the first day of class. Teachers and students build it together over time, and the process of building is itself a vocabulary activity. The student-led procedure begins with groups of three or four brainstorming a list of ten or more words they genuinely want or need. A group might scan the next chapter of the class textbook for key terms, or they might think about vocabulary connected to everyday events they discuss outside school. Either way, the words belong to the learners in a way that a pre-printed list does not.

Before any bricks are written, the teacher checks each group's list for spelling accuracy and for duplication across groups. One efficient alternative is to have all groups alphabetize their lists simultaneously, then write the words on the board in alphabetical order while groups cross out any words that appear on more than one list. The source notes that this cross-duplication step can become competitive in its own right, as groups race to find words that are both useful and unique to their list.

Once the word lists are clean, the teacher assigns each group a set of function words to include alongside their content vocabulary. Function words are the small grammatical items, such as articles, auxiliary verbs, negative particles, modal verbs, prepositions, and clause markers, that connect content words into complete sentences. Without enough of these items in the collection, a student like Fatima might hold the bricks "cat," "drink," and "water" but have no brick for "the" or "can," leaving her unable to build even a simple sentence. The Activate guidance is explicit that function words must be present in sufficient quantity, and that common items such as forms of "be," prepositions like "in" and "on," and clause markers like "because" and "when" will likely need multiple copies as the collection grows.

Students then write their assigned words neatly and carefully on blank bricks and add them to the class collection. Neatness matters: a brick that becomes illegible through careless handwriting is lost to the collection, and the source acknowledges that some bricks will be lost or become illegible over the life of the collection. The collection is always changing, and that is normal. A teacher who starts a new textbook chapter can add the chapter's key vocabulary to the collection at the same time as introducing the unit, keeping the bricks current with whatever language the class is studying.

The most practical tool for sustaining the collection is a running list posted somewhere visible in the room. When a new word surfaces during a lesson discussion, a student like Daniel calls it out, and the teacher adds it to the posted list rather than letting it disappear. Students can also be encouraged to bring in words they encounter outside the classroom, from a film, a sign, or a conversation with a neighbor. Those contributions keep the collection tied to language as learners meet it in the world beyond the classroom, and they give students a reason to attend to words wherever those words appear.
