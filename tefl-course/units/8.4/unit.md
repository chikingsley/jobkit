# Teaching Writing II: Process Writing and Correction Codes

## Writing as an Iterative Process: From First Draft to Final Version

Writing develops through repeated revision rather than through a single act of composition. When a teacher returns a first draft with written comments and invites the student to revise before a grade is assigned, that act communicates something about the nature of writing itself: that a finished piece emerges through successive attempts, each one shaped by feedback. A student like Priya, receiving marginal notes on her opening paragraph, understands her essay as a work in progress, advancing steadily toward a completed form.

The distinction between formative and summative feedback maps directly onto this sequence of drafts. Formative feedback, meaning feedback given during the drafting process to shape the changes a student makes in subsequent versions, reaches the student while revision is still possible and purposeful. Summative feedback, by contrast, is the teacher's response to a draft or final version that evaluates what the student has produced at a particular stage. A teacher might write formative comments on Priya's second draft, noting that her topic sentences do not yet connect to her thesis, and then provide summative feedback on the final version once those revisions have been attempted.

Students do not receive only the teacher's formative response. The teacher also provides feedback alongside peer and self-editing activity so that students are not relying on student feedback alone. A student like Daniel therefore works with two streams of comment simultaneously, one from a classmate and one from the teacher, both of which inform the next draft he produces.

The iterative quality of the process becomes visible in how students behave during peer editing. When a student notices that a classmate's essay is missing an introduction, that observation prompts a return to their own essay to check whether the same element is present. Reading someone else's work critically feeds back into the writer's own revision. Writing, understood this way, moves through a continuous cycle in which reading, noticing, and revising remain connected across every draft.

Seeing a gap in a peer's essay makes the structural requirement concrete in a way that a teacher's explanation alone may not. The student who spots the missing introduction has, in that moment, internalized a criterion for what a complete essay requires. That criterion travels back to their own page.

## Responding to Student Writing Without Overwhelming It

When a teacher collects a set of first drafts, the instinct to mark every error can feel almost automatic. A page returned covered in red ink may signal thoroughness, but it can also signal to the student that the writing belongs more to the teacher than to the writer. The main challenge of providing feedback before assigning a grade is plain: extra time and effort are required for teachers to provide feedback on drafts and then read and score the revised papers. That cost is real, and it shapes how teachers must think about where their attention goes.

The answer is to position teacher feedback carefully alongside peer input rather than withdrawing it. The teacher provides corrections and comments around the same time that students are working through the peer editing process, so that neither stream of feedback operates in isolation. Fatima, for instance, might receive a peer's checklist noting that her topic sentences are unclear, and find that her teacher has flagged the same issue independently. The two responses reinforce each other without either one carrying the full burden of correction.

Students who rely entirely on peer feedback receive input of uneven quality. A classmate may miss a structural problem that a trained teacher would catch immediately. The module is explicit on this point: teachers should still provide their own corrections so that students are not relying only on student feedback. The teacher's role remains substantive; it is the timing and proportion of that role that shifts.

What changes in this approach is the teacher's position in the feedback sequence. Rather than standing as the single authority who delivers a verdict on a finished piece, the teacher becomes one voice in a process that also includes the student's own self-editing and a peer's response. When Wei reads his teacher's comment alongside the note his partner left, he is comparing two perspectives on the same paragraph, a comparison that asks him to think and evaluate rather than comply. The formative review process allows feedback to shape the changes students make in subsequent drafts, with the teacher's input arriving in time to guide revision rather than only to evaluate a final product.

## Correction Codes: Marking the Margin, Not Rewriting the Line

A correction code is a set of short marginal symbols that tells a writer what kind of error a line contains without supplying the repair. Instead of crossing out "goed" and writing "went" above it, the teacher writes vt in the margin and leaves the line itself untouched. The symbols are compact and conventional: sp marks a spelling error, wo marks a problem with word order, vt marks a verb tense error, ww marks a wrong word choice, art marks a missing or incorrect article, agr marks faulty subject-verb agreement, p marks a punctuation problem, and a caret (^) shows the point where a word is missing. Each mark does two jobs at once: it locates the problem and it classifies it. What the mark deliberately does not do is fix anything.

That restraint is the entire point. When a draft comes back with every error rewritten in the teacher's hand, revision consists of copying; the thinking has already been done by someone else. When the same draft comes back with coded margins, the student must find the faulty form, work out what the code is pointing at, and produce the correction herself. A student like Wei, reading vt beside his sentence about last weekend, performs three operations: locating the error, diagnosing it, and repairing it. Each operation rehearses exactly the knowledge the revision is meant to strengthen, which is why coded feedback pushes writers toward self-correction in a way that a corrected page never can.

The code also fits the drafting cycle this unit began with. Formative feedback shapes the changes a student makes in subsequent versions, and a coded margin is formative feedback in its most literal form: it arrives between drafts, while revision is still possible, and it tells the writer precisely where to work next. A grade or a fully corrected page closes the conversation about a piece of writing. A margin of codes opens the next draft.

Coding does not mean coding everything. A teacher who marks all fourteen errors in a weak draft has produced the same discouraging page that over-correction always produces, only in a different notation. Selective marking applies here as it does everywhere in feedback: code the errors that touch the lesson's target language, the errors that obscure meaning, and the patterns that recur, and let isolated slips pass. A code is also only as useful as the knowledge behind it. If a student stares at agr beside a sentence and genuinely cannot see what to change, the symbol has revealed a gap that marking cannot close, and the teacher's next move is a brief targeted explanation. A code can prompt only what the writer already partly knows.

## Introducing the Code and Reading a Coded Draft

A complete correction code handed out in a single lesson overwhelms more than it helps, so the code should be introduced gradually. Begin with three or four symbols that cover the class's most frequent error types; sp, vt, and the missing-word caret are common starting points. Present each symbol on the board with a sample sentence, mark the sentence together, and have the class produce the repair collectively before anyone's own draft is coded. Add new symbols one or two at a time on later assignments, as the earlier ones become routine, and keep a legend permanently visible: a poster on the wall, or a copy glued inside the back cover of each notebook. By mid-course, students should read the margin as fluently as they read the text beside it.

A worked example shows the system operating. During a lesson sequence on describing past experiences, Daniel submits this first draft:

"Last year I go to Salvador with my brother. We visit the old town and we eat many wonderful foods. The people there was very friendly. I want go back next year."

The teacher returns the paragraph with four marginal marks and no rewriting: vt beside the first sentence, vt beside the second, agr beside the third, and a caret between "want" and "go" in the fourth. Working from the legend, Daniel produces a second version:

"Last year I went to Salvador with my brother. We visited the old town and we ate many wonderful foods. The people there were very friendly. I want to go back next year."

Every repair in that revision is Daniel's own. The teacher supplied categories, not answers, and the second draft is a record of what Daniel could retrieve once the errors were located for him. That record has diagnostic value in both directions. Codes acted on successfully mark knowledge that was present but unmonitored, the kind of slip a reminder can fix. A code that survives into the second draft unrepaired, or repaired incorrectly, marks a structure the student has not yet acquired, and it tells the teacher that a lesson, not a margin note, is needed.

T: "You fixed both vt marks and the agreement. What did the caret ask you to do?"
S: "Something is missing there. I read the sentence again and heard it: 'want to go.'"
T: "Good. You found the word yourself, which is exactly why I don't write it in for you."

The coded draft then slots into the drafting sequence described at the start of this unit. The teacher codes the first draft while revision is still ahead; the student revises; the teacher responds to the new version, often with fewer marks; summative response waits for the final draft. Over a term, the margins themselves become a progress record. When the vt marks thin out because a student has started catching tense errors before submitting, the code has done its deeper work: the categories have moved off the page and into the writer's own habits of review.

## Peer Editing Stations and the Goals They Share with the Code

Coded teacher feedback is one strand of between-draft revision; structured peer review is the other. The station-based Self-Editing and Peer Editing Checklist was set out step by step in the previous unit: ten stations, each dedicated to a single element of essay construction from thesis statement to conclusion-final thought, each with its own colored highlighter and its own checklist questions, with students self-checking and revising, then reaching agreement with a partner before initialing an item and moving on. The mechanics need no repeating here. What belongs in this unit is how the activity behaves as part of the feedback system. The color coding creates a visible record of the review: by the time a student has worked through all ten stations, her essay carries a layered pattern of highlights that shows, at a glance, which elements have been examined and where changes were made. Telling students in advance that the teacher will collect and review the completed peer assessment worksheets gives the exercise real stakes; initials on a checklist mean more when someone will read them.

The activity's five learning goals are the same goals a correction code serves at the sentence level. Students are meant to notice their own strengths and feel the confidence that comes from them; to identify weak or missing elements they had not seen when writing alone; to compare their own choices with a classmate's; to build inner criteria for what makes a strong essay; and to encounter a wider variety of writing than their own drafts can offer. All five converge on learner autonomy, the capacity to take responsibility for one's own learning without depending entirely on the teacher. A student with weaker writing skills benefits in a particular way. When she notices for herself that her topic sentences are missing, rather than being told so by the teacher, she is doing the cognitive work of diagnosis, and that act of noticing is what strengthens the skill. This self-directed noticing is precisely what moves students toward becoming more autonomous writers, because the improvement comes from their own experience of the process rather than from an external correction handed down to them.

## Challenges, Benefits, and Adapting Peer Review Across Contexts

Peer feedback as an alternative assessment approach does ask the teacher to give up a degree of control. In a conventional correction model, the teacher decides what gets flagged and how; in a peer review session, students are making those judgments, and the outcomes vary. Preparation and setup also take considerably more time than collecting a set of essays and returning them with comments. A teacher who runs this activity for the first time will need to design or adapt a checklist, arrange the editing stations, and train students in how to give and receive feedback before the session can run productively.

The benefits, though, are substantial. Students come to feel more in control of their own writing, and they stop treating the teacher as the sole authority on what is correct or effective. When Daniel reads a classmate's essay and spots a weak conclusion, he returns to his own draft and checks it without being prompted. That transfer of attention, from someone else's work back to one's own, is a form of self-regulation that a teacher's written comment rarely produces on its own. Students also learn that their peers can offer feedback worth taking seriously, which changes the social dynamic of the writing classroom.

The activity can be scaled to suit different proficiency levels without abandoning its core structure. For beginning-level students, the writing task itself becomes simpler, perhaps a single paragraph or even a few sentences rather than a full essay. The feedback tools change accordingly: instead of written comments, beginners can use single words or symbols such as plus signs, minus signs, question marks, check marks, or happy and sad faces to indicate their responses to a peer's work. These symbols give even low-proficiency students a way to participate meaningfully in the review process without being overwhelmed by the language demands of written commentary.

More advanced students can be held to correspondingly higher expectations, with more detailed checklists and more specific written feedback. The checklist itself, covering elements such as thesis statements, topic sentences, evidence, interpreting sentences, bridges, and conclusion components, can be extended or refined to match what a higher-level course requires. The structure of the activity remains the same; only the complexity of the criteria shifts.

Taken together, the challenges are real but manageable, and the module is direct on this point: the benefits outweigh them. Students who move through this process become better writers and more autonomous learners, two outcomes that teacher-only correction does not reliably produce on its own.
