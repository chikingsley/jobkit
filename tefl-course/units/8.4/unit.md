# Teaching Writing II: Process Writing and Correction Codes

## Writing as an Iterative Process: From First Draft to Final Version

Writing develops through repeated revision rather than through a single act of composition. When a teacher returns a first draft with written comments and invites the student to revise before a grade is assigned, that act communicates something about the nature of writing itself: that a finished piece emerges through successive attempts, each one shaped by feedback. A student like Priya, receiving marginal notes on her opening paragraph, understands her essay as a work in progress, advancing steadily toward a completed form.

The distinction between formative and summative feedback maps directly onto this sequence of drafts. Formative feedback, meaning feedback given during the drafting process to shape the changes a student makes in subsequent versions, reaches the student while revision is still possible and purposeful. Summative feedback, by contrast, is the teacher's response to a draft or final version that evaluates what the student has produced at a particular stage. A teacher might write formative comments on Priya's second draft, noting that her topic sentences do not yet connect to her thesis, and then provide summative feedback on the final version once those revisions have been attempted.

Students do not receive only the teacher's formative response. The teacher also provides feedback alongside peer and self-editing activity so that students are not relying on student feedback alone. A student like Daniel therefore works with two streams of comment simultaneously, one from a classmate and one from the teacher, both of which inform the next draft he produces.

The iterative quality of the process becomes visible in how students behave during peer editing. When a student notices that a classmate's essay is missing an introduction, that observation prompts a return to their own essay to check whether the same element is present. Reading someone else's work critically feeds back into the writer's own revision. Writing, understood this way, moves through a continuous cycle in which reading, noticing, and revising remain connected across every draft.

Seeing a gap in a peer's essay makes the structural requirement concrete in a way that a teacher's explanation alone may not. The student who spots the missing introduction has, in that moment, internalized a criterion for what a complete essay requires. That criterion travels back to their own page.

## Responding to Student Writing Without Overwhelming It

When a teacher collects a set of first drafts, the instinct to mark every error can feel almost automatic. A page returned covered in red ink may signal thoroughness, but it can also signal to the student that the writing belongs more to the teacher than to the writer. The source material acknowledges directly that the main challenge of providing feedback before assigning a grade is that extra time and effort are required for teachers to provide feedback on drafts and then read and score the revised papers. That cost is real, and it shapes how teachers must think about where their attention goes.

The source proposes positioning teacher feedback carefully alongside peer input rather than withdrawing it. The teacher in the source material provides corrections and comments around the same time that students are working through the peer editing process, so that neither source of feedback operates in isolation. A student named Fatima, for instance, might receive a peer's checklist noting that her topic sentences are unclear, and find that her teacher has flagged the same issue independently. The two responses reinforce each other without either one carrying the full burden of correction.

Students who rely entirely on peer feedback receive input of uneven quality. A classmate may miss a structural problem that a trained teacher would catch immediately. The source is explicit on this point: teachers should still provide their own corrections so that students are not relying only on student feedback. The teacher's role remains substantive; it is the timing and proportion of that role that shifts.

What changes in this approach is the teacher's position in the feedback sequence. Rather than standing as the single authority who delivers a verdict on a finished piece, the teacher becomes one voice in a process that also includes the student's own self-editing and a peer's response. When a student named Wei reads his teacher's comment alongside the note his partner left, he is comparing two perspectives on the same paragraph, a comparison that asks him to think and evaluate rather than comply. The formative review process, as the source describes it, allows feedback to shape the changes students make in subsequent drafts, with the teacher's input arriving in time to guide revision rather than only to evaluate a final product.

## Using Correction Checklists: Structure and Purpose

A correction checklist is a structured form that directs students to examine one specific element of their writing at a time, rather than attempting to assess the whole piece at once. The Self-Editing and Peer Editing Checklist for Writing described in the source covers ten elements in sequence: thesis statement, topic sentences, explaining sentences, evidence, interpreting sentences, closing sentences, bridges, conclusion-restatement, conclusion-summary, and conclusion-final thought. Each element represents a distinct layer of essay construction, and separating them on a checklist prevents students from conflating, say, the quality of their evidence with the clarity of their topic sentences. A student named Yuki, working through the checklist for the first time, may realize she has written strong explaining sentences but has never included an interpreting sentence at all, a gap she would not have named without a form that treats the two as separate concerns.

The checklist serves three distinct groups of users, and its value shifts slightly depending on who is holding it. For the student working alone, it ensures that important points have been addressed and provides a concrete basis for revising a draft before anyone else reads it. For a peer reviewer, it replaces vague impressions with specific, locatable feedback; instead of telling a classmate that an essay "feels incomplete," a student named Wei can point to the conclusion-summary station and explain precisely what is missing. For the teacher, using the same checklist across the whole class produces a more consistent basis for evaluation, because every student's work has been measured against the same set of criteria.

The physical organization of the activity reinforces the checklist's logic. Students move through a series of editing stations, each one dedicated to a single checklist element and each assigned a different colored highlighter pen. Red might mark the thesis statement, blue the topic sentences, yellow the evidence, and so on. The color coding creates a visible record of the review: by the time a student has worked through all ten stations, her essay carries a layered pattern of highlights that shows, at a glance, which elements have been examined and where changes were made. This visual dimension matters because it makes the process transparent to the student, to her peer, and to the teacher who later reviews the completed checklist.

The station-by-station structure also shapes the conversation between peers. When a student named Priya and her partner reach the bridges station together, their discussion is bounded by a single question rather than an open-ended judgment about the whole essay. That narrower focus makes it easier for Priya to give a specific response and easier for her partner to act on it. The checklist functions as a scaffold. By requiring students to locate each structural feature in someone else's writing, repeatedly and in sequence, it trains them to search for those same features in their own.

## Setting Up a Peer Editing Activity: Step-by-Step Procedure

Before the activity begins, the teacher must prepare students to take it seriously. Training means explaining why peer feedback exists: to give writers a reader's perspective at a stage when changes are still easy to make. Telling students in advance that the teacher will collect and review the completed peer assessment worksheets gives the exercise real stakes. A student named Carlos is far more likely to read his partner's thesis statement carefully if he knows his initials on the checklist will be seen by the teacher afterward.

The procedure itself moves through five steps, each tied to a physical station in the classroom. At the first step, students visit an editing station, where they find a colored highlighter pen and a set of directions focused on one specific element of the essay, such as the thesis statement or the topic sentences. Each station uses a different color, so the marked-up essay becomes a visual record of which elements have been reviewed. Students follow the directions at that station and apply the highlighter to the relevant section of their own writing.

At the second step, students turn to the Self-Editing and Peer Editing Checklist and use the questions listed there to self-check that same element of their essay. If a student named Fatima finds that her topic sentence does not clearly state the paragraph's main idea, she revises it before moving on. The checklist question makes the standard explicit; Fatima is not guessing at what a good topic sentence looks like but responding to a concrete criterion.

Once Fatima is satisfied with her revision, she reaches the third step: initialling the checklist beside that item. The initial is a small act, but it carries weight. It signals that she has read the criterion, applied it to her writing, and made any necessary changes, and it creates a record the teacher can trace back to her.

The fourth step brings in the peer. Fatima asks a classmate to check the same section using the same checklist questions, and the two students discuss it together. The classmate may initial the checklist only when both of them agree that the element meets the standard. Disagreement requires further discussion and revision before either student signs off. That mutual agreement is the heart of the step; neither student can defer to the other and move on without resolution.

At the fifth step, once both students have initialled that item, they move together to the next station and begin the sequence again with a new element and a new highlighter color. The process repeats until every station on the checklist has been completed. By the end, the essay carries the marks of both the writer's own scrutiny and a peer's, element by element, in a sequence that mirrors the structure of the essay itself.

## Goals and Learner Autonomy: What Peer Review Achieves

The activity carries five distinct learning goals, each addressing a different aspect of a student's relationship with her own writing. A student named Fatima may sit down at the thesis station and realize, for the first time, that her opening argument is genuinely well constructed; that moment of recognition is itself a goal, because students are meant to notice their own strengths and feel the confidence that comes from them. At the same time, a student like Carlos may reach the evidence station and see that his supporting details are thin, identifying a gap he had not noticed when writing alone. The activity is designed to produce both of these outcomes together.

Two further goals concern what students gain from reading work other than their own. When Carlos reads Fatima's essay, he can compare her approach to his, measuring his choices against a real alternative rather than an abstract standard. Over several such comparisons, students begin to build what the source calls inner criteria for what makes a strong essay, a personal sense of quality that does not depend on the teacher naming every flaw. Encountering different kinds of writing, with different structures and different voices, is the material from which those internal standards are built.

The fifth goal is exposure to variety itself. A student who has read only her own drafts has a narrow picture of what an essay can do; reading a classmate's work, even a weaker one, expands that picture. The checklist keeps this exposure purposeful, directing attention to specific elements rather than leaving students to form vague general impressions.

All five goals connect to a single larger outcome: learner autonomy, which means the capacity to take responsibility for one's own learning without depending entirely on the teacher. Students learn through this activity that they can rely on each other and on themselves, that peer feedback can be genuinely useful, and that they are capable of finding and correcting their own errors. A student with weaker writing skills benefits in a particular way here. When a student named Yuki notices for herself that her topic sentences are missing, rather than being told so by the teacher, she is doing the cognitive work of diagnosis; that act of noticing is what strengthens the skill. The source makes clear that this self-directed noticing is precisely what moves students toward becoming more autonomous writers, because the improvement comes from their own experience of the process rather than from an external correction handed down to them.

## Challenges, Benefits, and Adapting Peer Review Across Contexts

Peer feedback as an alternative assessment approach does ask the teacher to give up a degree of control. In a conventional correction model, the teacher decides what gets flagged and how; in a peer review session, students are making those judgments, and the outcomes vary. Preparation and setup also take considerably more time than collecting a set of essays and returning them with comments. A teacher who runs this activity for the first time will need to design or adapt a checklist, arrange the editing stations, and train students in how to give and receive feedback before the session can run productively.

The benefits, though, are substantial. Students come to feel more in control of their own writing, and they stop treating the teacher as the sole authority on what is correct or effective. When a student named Daniel reads a classmate's essay and spots a weak conclusion, he returns to his own draft and checks it without being prompted. That transfer of attention, from someone else's work back to one's own, is a form of self-regulation that a teacher's written comment rarely produces on its own. Students also learn that their peers can offer feedback worth taking seriously, which changes the social dynamic of the writing classroom.

The activity can be scaled to suit different proficiency levels without abandoning its core structure. For beginning-level students, the writing task itself becomes simpler, perhaps a single paragraph or even a few sentences rather than a full essay. The feedback tools change accordingly: instead of written comments, beginners can use single words or symbols such as plus signs, minus signs, question marks, check marks, or happy and sad faces to indicate their responses to a peer's work. These symbols give even low-proficiency students a way to participate meaningfully in the review process without being overwhelmed by the language demands of written commentary.

More advanced students can be held to correspondingly higher expectations, with more detailed checklists and more specific written feedback. The checklist itself, which in the source covers elements such as thesis statements, topic sentences, evidence, interpreting sentences, bridges, and conclusion components, can be extended or refined to match what a higher-level course requires. The structure of the activity remains the same; only the complexity of the criteria shifts.

Taken together, the challenges are real but manageable, and the source is direct on this point: the benefits outweigh them. Students who move through this process become better writers and more autonomous learners, two outcomes that teacher-only correction does not reliably produce on its own.
