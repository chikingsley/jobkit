# The World of TEFL: Markets, Learners, and Contexts

## What Is TEFL and Who Is It For?

Teaching English as a Foreign Language, commonly abbreviated as TEFL, is the professional field concerned with teaching English to learners in countries where English is not the dominant language of daily life. EFL, or English as a Foreign Language, refers specifically to this context: a student in Costa Rica learning English in a local school is an EFL learner, because Spanish surrounds her the moment the lesson ends. The neighboring term ESL, or English as a Second Language, describes learners acquiring English in a country where it is the dominant language, so a teenager in a bilingual classroom in the United States whose home language is Spanish is an ESL learner, not an EFL one. The difference is not academic hairsplitting. An ESL learner meets English on the street, at the supermarket, and on television; an EFL learner may meet it nowhere but in class. In the EFL setting the classroom is often the primary place where learners encounter English at all, and that fact shapes everything from the materials a teacher selects to the expectations placed on students.

The field serves a wide range of practitioners. Programs such as *Shaping the Way We Teach English: From Observation to Action* are designed explicitly for both new and experienced EFL teachers and teacher trainers, a description that signals how broad the professional community is. A first-year teacher working through her initial lessons with five-year-olds and a seasoned trainer preparing colleagues for a professional development session are both participants in the same field, with overlapping concerns and shared professional goals. The range is considerable.

Entering TEFL means joining a global community of educators whose classrooms appear in many different national and institutional settings. The videos in the *Shaping* materials feature English language classrooms from around the world, reflecting the international character of the profession. A teacher in one country can watch a colleague manage a vocabulary lesson in another and find techniques directly applicable to her own students. Cross-context exchange of this kind is central to how the field develops its practitioners.

What holds this diverse community together is a shared orientation toward professional growth. The *Shaping* materials describe a cycle in which teachers observe classroom practices, evaluate what they see through peer discussion and individual reflection, adapt ideas to suit their own contexts, and then act on the insights they have gained before beginning the cycle again. Mr. Osei, watching a video of a primary classroom abroad, might notice a transition technique he has never tried, discuss it with a colleague, and then modify it for his own class of eight-year-olds the following week. That process of observation, evaluation, adaptation, and action is the professional habit that TEFL, as a field, asks its practitioners to cultivate from the very beginning of their careers.

## The Range of Learners: From Very Young Children to Adults

English language learners are not a single, uniform population. The field organizes them into four broad groups based on age, and each group brings a different set of characteristics that shapes what a classroom looks, sounds, and feels like. A new teacher who understands these distinctions will be far better prepared to step into any assignment, wherever in the world it takes them.

Very young learners are children aged five and six. At this stage, the classroom itself is a teaching tool. In one example drawn from a bilingual English and Spanish setting in the United States, children move freely between learning stations, choosing where they want to work and play while their teacher delivers a science lesson about plants and assesses their understanding through multiple informal techniques. A teacher watching that classroom for the first time might be struck by how much of the learning happens through physical movement and choice rather than through seated instruction. The arrangement of the room, the presence of visual aids, and the management of transitions between activities are all central concerns when the learners are this young.

Young learners occupy the wider primary years, from age six to twelve. This is a substantial span of development, and a child of six and a child of twelve are very different language learners, yet both benefit from activity-based approaches that keep them engaged and on task. A beginner class of five and six-year-olds in Costa Rica, for instance, uses puppets, songs, and games to teach vocabulary, with the teacher providing positive feedback and managing off-task behavior throughout. A six-year-old sitting in that room is learning English through repetition and movement rather than through grammar explanation. The teacher's role is to sustain energy and attention while keeping the target language present at every moment.

Teenagers at secondary level present a different challenge entirely. They are capable of more sustained attention and more abstract thinking than younger children, but they bring social pressures, varying levels of motivation, and a sharper awareness of how they appear to their peers. A student like Yuki, fourteen years old and sitting in the back row of an EFL class, may disengage quickly if the material feels irrelevant or if the classroom dynamic does not feel safe. Classroom management at this level is less about physical transitions and more about creating conditions in which adolescents are willing to participate.

Young adults and adults, the post-secondary group, arrive with life experience, established learning habits, and often a clear sense of why they are studying English. They may be university students, working professionals, or community learners, and their goals vary considerably. Fatima, attending an evening English class after work, has different priorities from a nineteen-year-old in a university language program. What unites adult learners is that they can reflect on their own learning in ways that younger students cannot, which opens up possibilities for discussion, self-assessment, and peer feedback that would be less productive with a class of six-year-olds. Recognizing which of these four populations sits in front of you is the first practical step any teacher takes before planning a single lesson.

## Contexts and Settings: Where English Is Taught

The classroom in which a teacher works shapes almost every decision about materials, pacing, and technique. A bilingual English-Spanish primary classroom in the United States, for instance, operates under conditions quite different from an English as a Foreign Language classroom in Costa Rica, even though both serve children aged five and six. In the bilingual setting, English shares space with Spanish throughout the school day, and students move between the two languages as a matter of routine. In the Costa Rican EFL classroom, English arrives as a subject in its own right, and the teacher must build exposure to the target language almost entirely within the lesson period itself.

These two settings illustrate a broader reality: TEFL happens across a wide range of contexts around the world, from public primary schools to secondary programs to post-secondary institutions. A teacher working in a secondary school in one country faces a different set of constraints from a colleague who leads an adult conversation class at a community college. The learners differ, the institutional expectations differ, and the role English plays in daily life outside the classroom differs. Treating all these situations as interchangeable leads to lessons that fit none of them well.

Physical conditions vary just as much as institutional ones. Some classrooms are large and well-equipped, with dedicated learning stations, visual aids, and ample floor space for movement activities. Many teachers, however, work in rooms that offer far fewer resources, and a technique designed for a spacious, well-furnished environment cannot be transplanted unchanged into a smaller or less-equipped space. A teacher watching a video of an elaborate station-based lesson and thinking "I could never do that" is asking the wrong question; the more productive question is which elements of the approach can be adapted to the resources actually available.

Adaptation is a core professional skill rather than a workaround. If a lesson design calls for printed handouts and a classroom has no photocopier, the teacher might draw the same content on a chalkboard or ask students to copy a short text into their notebooks. If a song-based activity assumes a sound system, a teacher with a strong voice and a willingness to sing can achieve a comparable effect. The *Shaping* materials were designed with this in mind: teachers are encouraged to observe classroom practices, compare them to their own situations, and then adopt or adapt what they see to fit their local needs.

Recognizing the specific context in which one teaches is not a preliminary step that precedes real teaching; it is an ongoing act of professional judgment. A new teacher placed in a bilingual program, a rural EFL school, or an urban secondary classroom will encounter a distinct set of conditions on the first day and will continue refining their understanding of that context throughout their career. The variety of settings in which English is taught is the normal condition of the profession.

## What Differs Across Contexts: Age, Level, and Environment

The age of learners shapes almost every practical decision a teacher makes, from how the room is arranged to how long a single activity can last. A class of five and six year olds in a bilingual primary school requires routines that give children a degree of choice and movement, such as rotating between learning stations or responding physically to language through songs and games. Managing the shift from one activity to the next is a particular challenge at this level; as one set of observation materials notes, transitions between activities can be difficult, especially when working with very young learners. A teacher responsible for thirty first-graders might spend as much time planning how to move students from a vocabulary game to a science activity as she spends planning the activities themselves.

Post-secondary learners bring a different set of conditions entirely. An adult student like Carlos, attending an evening English course at a college, arrives with prior learning experience, a clearer sense of his own goals, and a tolerance for sustained attention that younger learners cannot be expected to match. The classroom structures that serve him well, such as extended discussion tasks, written reflection, or peer feedback on a draft, would be inappropriate for a child who needs physical engagement and frequent changes of pace. Each group requires its own approach to time, space, and interaction, shaped by conditions that run deeper than a single scale of ability or maturity.

The physical environment adds another layer of variation. One classroom may be large and well-equipped, with space for multiple learning stations and a range of visual materials on the walls. Another may be a small room with limited furniture and no technology. A teacher who watches a video of a well-resourced bilingual classroom and concludes that learning stations are impossible in her own setting has stopped one step too early. The more productive question is how the underlying idea might be adapted to whatever resources are available.

Practical planning tools make these variables visible in a concrete way. A lesson plan template that asks a teacher to record the number of students, the proficiency level, the institution, and the lesson length is not asking for administrative detail. Each field represents a condition that will shape what is possible in that session. A class of twelve intermediate adults meeting for eighty minutes calls for different decisions than a class of thirty beginner children meeting for forty. A teacher filling in such a template before her first lesson at a new school is in effect conducting a preliminary audit of her context before she has taught a single minute.

T: So before we start, can anyone tell me what we did last class?
S: We learned the names of animals.
T: Good. And how did we practice them?

That brief exchange, ordinary as it seems, already reflects a set of contextual choices: the age of the learners, the level of the class, and the amount of shared classroom history the teacher can draw on. Awareness of those conditions is what allows a teacher to make the exchange productive rather than merely routine.

## What the Job Involves: Core Professional Practices

Teaching English as a foreign language involves a set of recurring professional tasks that new entrants to the field sometimes underestimate. The *Shaping the Way We Teach English* materials identify five core areas of pedagogical skill that teachers develop through sustained practice: classroom management, language teaching techniques, encouraging critical and creative thinking, lesson observation skills, and adapting activities and lesson planning. These areas are interlocking demands that arise within a single class period, and a teacher managing a transition between activities is simultaneously applying classroom management knowledge and making a real-time adaptation of the lesson plan.

They also present a four-stage professional cycle as central to how teachers grow: observe, evaluate, adapt, and act. A teacher watches what happens in a classroom, whether her own or a colleague's on video, then evaluates what she sees against her own context, adapts the techniques to fit her students, and acts by trying something new. The cycle then begins again. For a teacher like Ms. Reyes, working with beginner teenagers in a large state school, a lesson she observes in a professional development session serves as a starting point for a series of deliberate adjustments, one she will reshape before it reaches her own classroom.

Reflection is built into this cycle as a routine professional obligation, not an optional extra. The materials encourage teachers to keep notes or an informal reflective log recording what went well and what they would do differently next time. That kind of record-keeping transforms individual lessons from isolated events into a cumulative body of professional experience. Without it, the same difficulties tend to recur without resolution.

Lesson planning is the other side of this reflective practice. The lesson plan template included with those materials has a structured evaluation section that asks teachers to answer specific questions after each class: what did students learn, and how does the teacher know; which parts of the lesson were most and least successful, and why; whether the lesson finished on time; and what changes, if any, the teacher will make going forward. These questions are substantive professional tools. They require a teacher to distinguish between what she intended to teach and what students demonstrably took away, a distinction that shapes every subsequent planning decision.

Consider Mr. Osei, who has just run a vocabulary lesson using songs and movement with a group of six-year-olds. After class he notes in his log that the song activity produced strong participation but that the transition into the next task took nearly five minutes and lost several students' attention.

T: Everyone sit down now, we're going to do something new.
S: Can we sing again?
T: We will later. First, look at the board.

That small moment of friction, recorded and examined, becomes the basis for a better transition routine in the following week's lesson. Professional practice in TEFL is the accumulation of these small adjustments, each one modest on its own, each one compounding across a career.

## Learning from Other Classrooms: Observation as a Professional Tool

Watching another teacher work is one of the oldest forms of professional learning available, and in TEFL it carries particular weight. A teacher who observes a colleague's class, or watches a recorded lesson from a different country, gains access to decisions and routines that no methodology textbook can fully describe. Course material underlying this unit, *Shaping the Way We Teach English: From Observation to Action*, frames this experience precisely: observation can help teachers better understand how they approach their own students, curricula, and classroom routines.

The framework moves through defined stages rather than leaving the observer to watch without direction. Teachers first activate their prior knowledge before viewing, drawing on their own classroom experience to set a context for what they are about to see. They then watch with specific focus areas in mind, attend to particular teaching concepts and classroom management techniques, and finally move toward action by planning how to apply what they have observed. This sequence transforms passive watching into a structured professional activity.

Central to the framework is the principle that teachers should compare observed practices to their own classes and then decide whether to adopt or adapt what they have seen. Consider a teacher in a large secondary classroom in Morocco who watches a video of a bilingual primary class in the United States where students rotate between learning stations. The station model cannot transfer directly to her context, but the underlying idea of giving students structured choice within a lesson can. She adapts the concept by designating three areas of her room for different task types on certain days, keeping the principle while fitting it to her space and her learners.

T: Today you can start at the reading corner or the writing table. Choose one.
S: Can we move later?
T: Yes, after twenty minutes you will switch.

That exchange illustrates an observed idea reshaped for a specific context, which is the outcome the observation cycle is meant to produce.

This outward-looking, reflective habit is not reserved for experienced teachers attending formal training events. The *Shaping* materials are addressed to new and experienced EFL teachers alike, and the observation cycle they outline, moving from watching to evaluating to adapting to acting and back again, is one that a teacher can begin on the first day of professional life. Developing the instinct to look at other classrooms, ask what is working and why, and then carry that thinking back into one's own teaching is a defining characteristic of the TEFL professional at every stage of a career.
