# What Makes an Effective Teacher

## From Traits to Behaviours: What Effectiveness Means

Experienced teachers are sometimes described in terms of personality: they are patient, enthusiastic, or naturally authoritative. These descriptions may be accurate, but they offer a trainee very little to work with. Patience cannot be practised in the same way that a questioning technique can. The more productive approach is to examine what effective teachers do in the classroom, because behaviour is observable, measurable, and open to change.

The source material behind this unit takes that position. The introduction to *Shaping the Way We Teach English: From Observation to Action* describes a cycle of professional development built around four stages: observe, evaluate, adapt, and act. A teacher watches a lesson, assesses what is working and what is not, considers alternative approaches, tries something different, and then begins the cycle again. The cycle assumes that teaching quality is located in specific, repeatable actions rather than in fixed character.

What counts as an observable action? Consider a teacher who notices that only one student, Marta, is answering every question. The teacher adjusts by throwing a soft ball to a different student, who must answer and then throw it to a classmate for the next question. That adjustment is a behaviour. It can be described, demonstrated, and copied. Another teacher, working with very young learners who have short attention spans, builds movement into the final stage of the lesson so that children leave the room having been physically active rather than restless. That decision, too, is a behaviour with a clear rationale behind it.

This unit examines actions of that kind across three phases of teaching. Before a lesson, effective teachers make deliberate choices about objectives, seating arrangements, and the variety of activities they plan to include. During a lesson, they manage transitions, respond to off-task students without disrupting the flow of the class, and check whether students have understood new material. After a lesson, they reflect on what went well and identify what they would do differently. A teacher named David, for instance, might finish a vocabulary lesson and note that his student Omar produced almost no spoken language; the reflection prompts David to plan a think-pair-share activity for the following session.

None of these actions requires a particular personality type. They require knowledge, attention, and a willingness to keep returning to the observe-evaluate-adapt-act cycle. Returning to that cycle is the foundation on which this unit is built.

## Preparation and Clear Objectives

Preparation sits at the centre of effective teaching because it determines what happens in the classroom before a single student arrives. A teacher who has prepared thoroughly knows what students should be able to do by the end of the lesson, and that knowledge shapes every activity, question, and transition in the session. Without a clear sense of the intended outcome, a lesson can drift, and neither the teacher nor the students have a reliable way to measure whether learning has taken place.

Lesson objectives are statements of what students will be able to do, produce, or demonstrate by the time the lesson ends. A teacher named Sara, preparing a vocabulary lesson for young learners, might set the objective that students will correctly identify and name five items of classroom furniture. That single statement tells Sara which activities to include, how long to spend on each one, and what a successful student response looks like. Vague intentions, such as "covering" a topic, give the teacher no comparable standard against which to measure the lesson.

Knowing the objective also means planning how to find out whether students have met it. Assessment in this sense does not always mean a formal test; it includes any moment in the lesson when the teacher gathers information about student understanding. A teacher might ask a concept-checking question such as "T: Miguel, can you show me the window?" and observe whether Miguel points to the correct object. That brief exchange tells the teacher something concrete about what Miguel has learned, without interrupting the pace of the lesson.

Comprehension checks serve a similar purpose during instruction. When a teacher gives directions and then asks "T: How long do you have to answer the questions?" she is collecting evidence that students have processed the instruction, not rehearsing the directions for her own benefit. If a student named Yuki answers incorrectly, the teacher has immediate information and can rephrase before the class moves on. Preparation makes these checks possible because the teacher has thought in advance about where misunderstanding is likely to occur.

When no formal assessment is built into a lesson, a prepared teacher still has options. She might ask students to write a sentence on a slip of paper, read the sentences aloud, and invite the class to evaluate them, as described in the source materials. She might observe student performance during a game or movement activity and note which students respond accurately and which hesitate. The teacher has decided, during preparation, that she will look for this evidence and has created a moment in the lesson where it can appear. Preparation and assessment are not separate concerns; they are two parts of the same act of planning.

## Clarity in the Classroom: Instructions, Questions, and Checking Understanding

Clear communication separates teachers who move through lessons without losing time from those who find themselves repeatedly backtracking to repair confusion. When a teacher gives directions, pace, vocabulary, and physical gesture all carry meaning simultaneously. A teacher working with lower-level learners might say "Open your books," hold up her own book, and pause for three full seconds before continuing, giving students like Miguel and Fatima time to process one instruction before the next arrives. Rushing through a sequence of steps, even in plain language, leaves some learners behind before the activity has begun.

Comprehension-checking questions offer a direct way to confirm that directions have landed. These are questions designed to verify whether students have understood an instruction, as distinct from questions that invite a reflexive "yes" regardless of actual understanding. A question such as "How long do you have to answer the questions?" requires a student to demonstrate understanding rather than guess at the expected answer. When a teacher poses this to the whole class and waits, she learns immediately whether the group is ready to proceed or whether the directions need repeating.

Having students repeat directions back serves two purposes at once. As students say the steps aloud, the teacher can write them on the board, creating a visible record students can consult throughout the activity. A brief exchange might look like this: T: "So what do you do first, Carlos?" S: "We read the paragraph." T: "Good. And then?" S: "We answer the questions with our partner." The board then shows "1. Read. 2. Answer with partner," and students who lose track mid-activity have a reference point without needing to interrupt.

Concept-checking questions serve a related but distinct function. They verify whether students have understood the meaning of a word or phrase, not merely whether they can repeat it back. Rather than asking "Do you know what 'exhausted' means?", a teacher might ask "If someone is exhausted, do they want to go dancing or go to sleep?" A student who answers correctly has demonstrated understanding; a student who hesitates signals that the word needs more attention. Directing such questions at the whole class, with students responding simultaneously by raising hands or holding up cards, gives the teacher a full picture rather than a conclusion drawn from one confident voice.

That last point applies to any checking activity. One student's answer tells the teacher only what that one student knows. Techniques that invite the whole group to respond, through gesture, written response, or choral reply, produce a far more accurate reading of where the class stands. A teacher who sees half the room hesitate over a concept-checking question has learned something she could never have learned by calling on the student who always raises a hand first.

## Rapport and Fairness: Building an Inclusive Classroom

Physical proximity is one of the most immediate signals a teacher can send about the kind of classroom she intends to run. When a teacher sits on the floor with very young learners rather than standing at the front of the room, she reduces the physical distance between herself and her students in a way that changes the atmosphere of the lesson. A child like Mia, who might feel intimidated looking up at an adult standing over her, is far more likely to attempt an answer when the teacher is at eye level, close enough to make quiet, encouraging contact. The source materials note that this closeness carries particular weight with very young learners, though the underlying principle applies across age groups: a teacher who moves toward her students signals that she is accessible rather than remote.

Proximity alone, however, does not guarantee that every student feels included. A classroom where the same three students answer every question is not an equitable one, even if the teacher is warm and approachable. The source identifies this as a deliberate management concern, asking how a teacher gives all students a chance to display their knowledge rather than relying on just one student to answer. One practical response is the think-pair-share model, in which students first form their own ideas, then test those ideas with a partner before the pair shares with the class. A student like Omar, who rarely volunteers in open discussion, has already rehearsed his answer with a classmate by the time the class reconvenes, making speaking aloud considerably less daunting.

Other techniques distribute participation in ways that feel less like a formal procedure. The source describes a teacher throwing a small soft ball to one student, who answers and then throws it to another student for the next question. The ball makes the selection visible and somewhat unpredictable, so students stay attentive because any of them might receive it next. A related approach has one student call on another, shifting some of the authority for managing participation away from the teacher and toward the group itself.

T: "Good. Now, Leila, you choose the next person."
S: "Um... David."
T: "David, what word did we use for the animal's home?"
S: "Den?"
T: "Exactly right."

This kind of exchange does more than distribute turns. It builds a small social contract in which students are responsible to one another, not only to the teacher.

Shyer students require particular attention within these structures. The source raises the specific challenge of encouraging very young or reticent learners to produce oral language, and the answer is rarely to call on them without warning in front of the whole group. Pair work, puppet activities, and movement-based tasks all create lower-stakes moments in which a quieter student can attempt language without the full weight of the class watching. When a teacher notices that a student like Priya has been silent for most of the lesson, the appropriate response is to engineer a situation where Priya can speak to one person first, building enough confidence to contribute more broadly. Fairness in a classroom is not the same as treating every student identically; it means giving each student the conditions in which she can participate on her own terms.

## Adaptability: Managing the Classroom and Responding to Learners

Adaptability in the classroom is best understood as a collection of specific, practised behaviours rather than a vague disposition toward flexibility. A teacher who manages transitions poorly, allows off-task behaviour to spread unchecked, or ignores the physical layout of the room will lose instructional time regardless of how willing she is to change course in principle. The behaviours that matter are concrete and learnable: signalling attention, redirecting individual students, sequencing activities so that movement is built in, and arranging seating to match what a given task requires.

Managing students who drift off task is one of the most immediate tests of these behaviours. Very young learners have short attention spans, and moments when a child is standing when she should be sitting, talking out of turn, or staring at the ceiling are predictable rather than exceptional. The teacher's goal is to redirect that student without breaking the rhythm of the lesson for everyone else. A quiet word, a hand placed briefly on a shoulder, or a look directed at the student while the lesson continues aloud are all ways of addressing the individual without making the disruption larger than it already is. When a teacher stops the whole class to correct one child named Marco, she has handed Marco's inattention to every other student in the room.

Transitions between activities are a separate but related challenge. Moving a group of young learners from a seated listening task to a pair activity, or from a game back to whole-class instruction, creates a gap in which off-task behaviour tends to multiply. Specific signals close that gap. A teacher might say "ABC, eyes on me" or use a content-linked phrase such as "Bear, Cat, Deer, eyes up here," substituting vocabulary from the current lesson topic. The signal works because students have been trained to respond to it; the training itself is a classroom routine, and routines reduce the cognitive load on both teacher and student during moments of transition.

Classroom routines serve a broader purpose beyond managing transitions. When students know what is expected at the start of a lesson, how to move between stations, and what the signal for quiet sounds like, they begin to manage some of these steps themselves. A student like Yusuf, who has learned the routine for collecting materials at the beginning of a station activity, does not need the teacher to direct him each time. That small degree of self-management, multiplied across a class, gives the teacher more room to attend to students who genuinely need her attention.

Seating arrangements are another variable that a teacher can adjust to suit the activity rather than leaving the room configured in one permanent way. A storytelling lesson may call for students to sit on the floor close to the teacher, reducing the physical distance between them and making it easier for the teacher to maintain attention and check understanding. A discussion task works better when students sit in a circle or square so that they can see one another. In a classroom where desks are fixed, a teacher can ask two students from the front row to turn around and work with the two students directly behind them, creating a group of four without moving any furniture.

Adaptability also means working squarely with the classroom environment that exists. A teacher who has seen learning stations used in a large, well-equipped room may assume the approach is unavailable to her in a smaller or less resourced space. The more productive question is what version of the idea is possible given the available conditions. Stations can be simplified, combined, or run sequentially rather than simultaneously. Movement can be incorporated into an activity through a chant, a gesture sequence, or a simple instruction to stand when answering, even when there is no space for students to circulate freely. The constraint is real, but it rarely eliminates the underlying principle entirely.

## Reflection as a Professional Habit

Reflection is a deliberate act, not a passive response to how a lesson felt. The source materials frame it as a structured set of questions: what went well, what a teacher might do differently, and what alternative approaches could be used in the future. A teacher who asks only the first question is doing something useful but incomplete. The second and third questions are where genuine professional development begins, because they require a teacher to move from observation to analysis and then to a concrete plan.

The "observe, evaluate, adapt, act" cycle described in the source's introduction gives that process a repeatable shape. A teacher observes what happens in the classroom, evaluates those events against what she intended, adapts her approach in response, and then acts on that adaptation in a subsequent lesson. Consider a teacher named Marta who notices that her student Paulo consistently stops participating during pair work. She observes the pattern, evaluates whether the task design or the pairing is contributing to it, adapts by trying a think-pair-share structure with a different partner, and acts on that change in the next class. The new lesson generates new observations, and the process begins again.

The source is unambiguous on one point: reflection is not a one-time event. The end of one lesson is simultaneously the beginning of the next round of professional thinking. A teacher who reflects once after a difficult class and then returns to unchanged habits has treated reflection as a remedy rather than a practice. Improvement in concrete classroom behaviour accumulates through repetition, not through single moments of insight.

Maintaining the habit requires consistent intention. After a lesson in which a teacher named James tried using a soft ball to manage student responses, he might spend five minutes noting which students engaged more readily, whether the pace of the activity held the class's attention, and what he would change about the instructions next time. That brief, focused review is sufficient to feed the next cycle. The questions do not need to be elaborate; they need to be honest and specific enough to produce a usable answer.

Over time, teachers who maintain this habit build a practical record of what works with particular groups, particular content, and particular conditions. That record is not abstract knowledge about teaching in general. It is knowledge about their own classrooms, their own students, and the specific adjustments that have produced better outcomes in their specific context. Effective teaching, in this sense, is not a fixed state a teacher reaches and then maintains. It is the ongoing product of a cycle that any teacher can begin after the very next lesson.
