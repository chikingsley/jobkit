# Culture and Its Implications

## Why Culture Matters in the EFL Classroom

Cultural awareness in the EFL classroom begins with a practical question: whose norms are shaping the lesson? A teacher who assumes that students will respond to praise, movement, or group work in predictable ways may find that a technique effective in one setting produces confusion or silence in another. The gap between expectation and reality is not a failure of method; it is information about context. Attending to that gap is the first step toward teaching that fits the room.

The source materials underlying this unit were designed around classrooms spanning very young learners aged five and six through young adults, drawn from settings as different as a bilingual school in the United States and an EFL classroom in Costa Rica. No single arrangement of desks, no single way of calling on students, and no single approach to classroom management proved universal across those settings. A teacher moving between such contexts carries assumptions formed in one place and must test them carefully in another.

The "observe, evaluate, adapt, act" cycle described in those materials offers a useful frame here. A teacher watches what happens in a classroom, considers what the observation means, adjusts materials and techniques to suit local needs, tries the adjustment, and then begins watching again. The cycle treats the teacher as a learner of context rather than an authority who arrives with finished answers. Cultural awareness fits naturally into that posture: the teacher notices, questions, and revises rather than imposes.

Consider a concrete example. A teacher named Marta moves from teaching teenagers in a large urban secondary school to a primary class of six-year-olds in a rural setting. She plans a lesson using the same call-and-response routine that worked well before.

T: "Who can tell me the word for this?" (holds up a picture of a plant)
S: (silence; several children look at the floor)

Marta's instinct might be to repeat the question more slowly, assuming a language difficulty. The more productive response is to ask what the silence means in this particular room, with these particular children, in this particular community. The answer may involve age, may involve local norms around speaking to adults, or may involve something else entirely. The point is that the question must be asked before the technique is adjusted.

Culture is not a background fact about students; it is present in the moment a teacher decides how to arrange chairs, how to signal a transition, or how to give feedback on a wrong answer. Treating those decisions as culturally neutral is itself a cultural assumption, and one worth examining. Bringing a questioning stance to every topic that follows is what the remainder of this unit asks of you.

## Classroom Expectations and Physical Space Across Contexts

Walk into any classroom and you are already reading a cultural text. The height of the ceiling, the direction the chairs face, the presence or absence of a carpet, the way wall space is used or left bare: each of these features carries meaning about what learning is supposed to look like and who is supposed to direct it. A teacher trained in one context will often experience a different arrangement as wrong, when what they are encountering is a different set of assumptions made physical.

The source materials for this unit include a warm-up activity that makes this visible in a direct way. Teachers are asked to draw a picture of an ideal classroom for very young learners, attending to all the parts of the room, including ceilings, floors, and walls, as well as the arrangement of desks, chalkboards, visual aids, and other resources. The follow-up questions then ask why a teacher would arrange a room in that particular way, and how the features chosen help students learn and support classroom management. The exercise is deceptively simple. Most teachers find that their picture looks very much like the classrooms they themselves attended as children, and that this resemblance did not feel like a choice until the drawing made it visible.

One classroom described in the source materials is a bilingual English and Spanish setting in the United States, serving five and six year olds. It is described as large and well-equipped, with defined learning stations, each stocked with materials designed to teach or reinforce specific skills. Students in that classroom choose which station to work at, a design that places learner autonomy at the centre of the physical layout. The room itself communicates an expectation: children here are trusted to make decisions about their own activity. A teacher entering that space from a context where whole-class instruction is the norm might read the arrangement as chaotic before a single student has spoken.

The source materials acknowledge this gap directly. Teachers are asked what they would do if their own classroom environment is not like the one in the video, and how they might use available resources to adapt the idea of learning stations for their own teaching context. The question matters because it shifts the frame from replication to adaptation. The goal is not to reproduce a particular physical arrangement but to understand what that arrangement is trying to achieve, and then to ask whether the same aim can be pursued with different means.

Consider a teacher named Amara, working in a single-room school with fixed benches and no storage space. Watching a video of a well-resourced bilingual classroom, she might conclude that learning stations are beyond reach in her setting. That conclusion closes the inquiry too early. The more productive question concerns what the stations are doing: giving students a degree of choice, allowing different activities to run at the same time, and reducing the pressure on the teacher to direct every moment. Amara may find that rotating small groups between tasks written on the board achieves something comparable, even without a dedicated physical space for each activity.

The habit of observation that this requires is the same habit described elsewhere in this unit. A teacher who enters a new classroom context and immediately begins rearranging furniture to match a familiar model is making an assumption about what the space should do. Pausing matters. A teacher who first asks why the room is arranged as it is, who sits in the students' seats, who notices what is on the walls and what is not, is gathering information before drawing conclusions. Physical space is one of the clearest places where cultural expectations about learning become concrete, and it is therefore one of the clearest places to begin the work of understanding a new context rather than overwriting it.

## Participation Norms and Learner Autonomy

Participation norms are the unspoken rules that govern who speaks in a classroom, when they speak, and in what form their contribution is expected to arrive. In some contexts, students wait to be addressed directly before offering an answer. In others, calling out is accepted and signals engagement. Neither pattern is inherently more productive than the other; each reflects a set of cultural assumptions about the relationship between learner and teacher, and about what respectful attention looks like.

The source material defines learner autonomy as the ability of students to make decisions about and direct their own learning. This definition is specific and bounded, and it does not require students to be loud, spontaneous, or visibly assertive. A student who selects which learning station to work at, decides the order in which to complete tasks, or chooses how to record what she has understood is exercising autonomy, even without drawing attention to herself. A teacher who conflates autonomy with a particular style of vocal participation may misread a reserved student as passive when she is, in fact, directing her own work.

The source raises a practical question about classroom management: how can teachers call on students to share information, and how can they get more than one student to answer each question? These are questions about structure. Consider a teacher, Mr. Osei, who asks a question and then waits only for the first hand to go up. His student Amara, who needs a moment to formulate her answer, never gets the chance to speak. A simple adjustment, such as asking students to write a response before anyone shares aloud, or directing the same question to three different students in sequence, changes who participates without requiring anyone to perform a kind of confidence they do not yet have.

Learning stations, as described in the source, are defined spaces in a classroom equipped with materials to teach or reinforce specific skills, designed so that learners can experience target content in a variety of ways. The structure itself distributes choice across the group. When a student named Carlos selects the drawing station over the writing station, he is making a decision about how he learns best. The teacher has not imposed a single path; she has built a set of paths and allowed students to enter them. Autonomy becomes concrete here, embedded in the lesson design rather than dependent on students negotiating their preferences in front of the whole class.

The source also notes that establishing routines can help younger students become more well-behaved and autonomous learners. Predictability matters. When students know what the structure of a lesson will be, they can move through it with less dependence on the teacher for direction at every step. A teacher introducing student-directed activity into a context where whole-class, teacher-led instruction has been the norm should expect some disorientation, because students in that setting have learned, over years, that the teacher holds the floor and that their role is to respond when invited. Asking them to direct their own work without preparation can feel less like freedom and more like abandonment.

The practical implication is that such approaches are introduced gradually. A teacher might begin by offering two options rather than open choice, or by building one short station activity into an otherwise conventional lesson. After the lesson, she reflects on what she observed: Did students seem uncertain about what was expected? Did some engage more than they had before? The source's recommendation to keep a reflective log of what went well and what to do differently applies here directly. Adaptation is not a single decision made before the lesson begins; it is a process that continues through and after it.

## Attitudes to Authority, Feedback, and Error

Feedback, as the source defines it, is the information that lets learners know how they are doing, and it can come from the teacher or from other learners in either oral or written form. That definition sounds straightforward, but the way a learner receives feedback depends heavily on what authority and correction have meant in every classroom she has sat in before yours. A student named Fatima, who has spent six years in a setting where the teacher's word was final and public error brought shame, will experience even a gentle correction very differently from a student named Carlos, who has been encouraged since primary school to treat mistakes as part of the learning process.

The Costa Rica classroom in the source materials offers a useful point of reference. The teacher there gives positive feedback when students answer correctly and manages off-task behaviour directly, stepping in when students are distracted. Both moves are forms of feedback, and both carry an implicit message about who holds authority in the room. In many cultural contexts, that directness reads as caring and clear. In others, a student like Minh, accustomed to a classroom culture where the teacher's correction signals personal failure rather than academic guidance, may withdraw after a public redirect, even a kind one.

The source describes encouragement as pointing out areas for improvement in a supportive way. The word "supportive" is doing real work in that phrase. Supportive does not mean the same thing in every classroom culture. For some learners, a teacher who praises effort before naming an error feels warm and trustworthy. For others, the praise feels like a preamble to humiliation, because in their experience, positive remarks before a correction were a social ritual that made the correction sting more. A teacher who assumes her tone of encouragement will be read as she intends it may be surprised when a student like Yusuf goes quiet after what felt, to the teacher, like a perfectly balanced piece of feedback.

Peer feedback introduces a second layer of cultural complexity. When another learner, rather than the teacher, points out an error, the social stakes shift. In a classroom where the teacher is the sole legitimate source of knowledge, a peer correction can feel presumptuous or even disrespectful to the student receiving it. The same exchange, in a classroom culture built around collaborative learning, feels entirely normal. Before asking students to give each other feedback on a spoken answer or a piece of writing, a teacher benefits from observing how students already interact: whether they defer to one another, whether they laugh off mistakes together, or whether they look immediately to the teacher for confirmation.

The practical implication is that a teacher should observe before deciding how directive or collaborative her feedback approach will be. Watching how a student like Ana reacts when her answer is corrected in front of the group, or how a student like Tariq responds when a classmate offers a suggestion, gives the teacher information she cannot get from a lesson plan. The reflective log recommended in the source materials is as useful here as it is in any other area of adaptation: noting which feedback moments seemed to land well and which produced silence or discomfort is the beginning of a more culturally informed practice.

## Adapting Without Stereotyping: The Reflective Teacher

Reflection is not a single event that happens after a difficult lesson. It is a repeating cycle, and the source materials describe it precisely in those terms: observe, evaluate, adapt, act, then begin again. A teacher who treats cultural awareness as something acquired once during an orientation week will find that her assumptions calcify rather than sharpen. The cycle keeps those assumptions in motion, subject to revision each time a new student arrives or a familiar student surprises her.

The risk the cycle guards against is not ignorance alone. A teacher who has read about collectivist cultures may assume that Mei, a student from Shanghai, will always prefer group tasks and feel uncomfortable with individual praise. That assumption, applied without observation, is a stereotype wearing the clothes of cultural knowledge. The observe-evaluate-adapt-act sequence insists that the teacher watch Mei specifically, in this classroom, on this task, before drawing any conclusion. General cultural knowledge provides a starting hypothesis, not a verdict.

The reflective log is the practical instrument that makes this possible over time. The source materials recommend keeping notes or an informal log recording what went well and what a teacher would do differently next time. Applied to cultural adaptation, this means writing down specific moments: the afternoon when Carlos fell quiet after a whole-class correction, the morning when Fatima's group produced far more language than she had during individual writing time. A log entry does not need to be long.

T: "Carlos, can you tell us what you wrote?"
S: [long pause, looks at desk]
T: [later, in log] "Direct nomination in front of group — Carlos shut down. Try pair-share first tomorrow."

That exchange, recorded and returned to, becomes the basis for a genuine adaptation rather than a guess.

The source materials also note that the video viewing guides they describe can be completed in any order and are designed for both new and experienced teachers. The same principle applies to cultural reflection. A teacher in her first year and a teacher in her fifteenth are both capable of noticing something they had not noticed before, and both are capable of acting on it. Experience does not graduate a teacher out of the cycle; it gives her more data to bring into it. A newer teacher may notice a pattern after three lessons; an experienced teacher may recognise one after a single exchange because she has seen something similar before and recorded it.

The section of the source materials addressed to trainers makes the same point in a professional development context, recommending that facilitators keep a reflective log of training events and consider what they would do differently next time. The habit is the same whether the subject is a training session or a Tuesday morning class with twelve teenagers. Noticing, recording, and returning to the record is what separates adaptation from improvisation.

To close this unit with something concrete: take a piece of paper or open a blank document and write one sentence describing a cultural difference you have already noticed in your classroom. It may be a pattern in how students respond to correction, how they address you, or how they work alongside peers from different backgrounds. Write what you observed, write what you did, and write one thing you might try differently. That entry is the first page of a reflective log, and the cycle has already begun.
