# Task-Based Language Teaching: What the Evidence Says

## What Makes Something a 'Task'? Defining the Core Concept

In task-based language teaching, a task is an activity in which learners use language to achieve a real, communicable outcome, one that exists independently of the language forms required to reach it. A student completing a fill-in-the-blank exercise on the present perfect is demonstrating knowledge of a grammatical structure; a student persuading a classmate to choose one apartment over another is doing something with language. The distinction matters because the goal of the activity shapes how learners attend to meaning.

Course material on student-centered learning describes a classroom arrangement in which "students then work together to practice, using the language for real communicative purposes." That phrase captures the defining feature of a task: the communicative purpose is genuine, not simulated for the sake of display. Consider a classroom exchange in which Ms. Reyes has asked two students to agree on which of three local events to recommend to a new classmate. Priya asks, T: "Which event would our new classmate enjoy?" Daniel replies, S: "Probably the food festival, because our classmate mentioned enjoying cooking." They are not performing a grammar point. They are solving a shared problem, and the language is the instrument, not the object.

A task also carries what the module calls a rationale, defined as "a justification or purpose for doing something" that helps students understand how an activity supports their learning and keeps them motivated. In a conventional exercise, the rationale is external and often invisible to the learner: practice this form because the teacher assigned it. In a task, the rationale is built into the activity itself. Students can see why they need to speak, read, or write, because a concrete outcome depends on it.

This built-in motivation connects directly to the concept of a driving question, described in the module as an open-ended question that focuses project work and around which students "see the need to gain knowledge, understand concepts, and apply skills." A task operates on the same principle at a smaller scale. When a teacher asks a group to decide which candidate should receive a community scholarship and to justify their choice to the class, the question is not answerable by retrieving a memorized form. Student Yuki cannot conjugate her way to an answer. She must gather information, weigh it, and express a reasoned position, one that requires genuine cognitive and communicative work to produce and would be recognizable as meaningful to someone outside the classroom.

## How a TBLT Lesson Is Structured: Pre-Task, Task Cycle, and Language Focus

A widely used TBLT framework moves through three phases: pre-task, task cycle, and language focus (Willis, 1996). The task cycle itself contains the task, planning, and report stages. This sequence keeps meaning-focused work central while reserving a deliberate place to examine language that emerged from the task.

The pre-task phase introduces the topic and the task, activates useful knowledge, and makes the outcome clear without preteaching every form students might need. A teacher might open a lesson on urban planning by showing a short video of a neighborhood dispute over a proposed development, then asking students to write down two questions they would want answered before making a decision. Carlos, who contributed little in previous lessons, writes: "Who lives there now?" That question is a genuine information gap, and it orients him toward the task ahead. The pre-task phase does not need to be long, but it must make the coming work feel purposeful rather than arbitrary.

The task cycle is where most of the communicative work happens. Learners first complete the task in pairs or groups while the teacher monitors rather than directs. They then plan how to explain their result clearly, which creates a natural reason to improve organization and accuracy, and finally report the result to the class or another audience. In a lesson on environmental issues, one group led by Priya might build an argument around local water-quality data while another focuses on air-pollution statistics. Both groups pursue the same outcome, but the paths they take reflect genuine decision-making.

The report stage serves two related purposes: communicative reporting to an audience and pedagogical debriefing for comprehension. Groups present their findings or decisions to an audience beyond the peers who completed the task with them. A brief debrief then helps the teacher check comprehension and ask learners to articulate the reasoning behind their choices. After Priya's group presents, the teacher might ask:

T: You said the river data was the strongest evidence. Why did you choose that over the air quality figures?
S: Because it affects more people in our area directly.

That exchange does more than confirm comprehension; it pushes students to articulate the reasoning behind their choices. The lesson then moves into language focus. Learners analyze useful forms, recurring problems, or effective phrases drawn from their own reports, followed by focused practice where needed. Willis (1996) places this analysis-and-practice work after the task cycle; Ellis (2003) likewise treats post-task attention to form as compatible with meaning-focused task performance. The teacher might place two anonymous sentences from the reports on the board, ask the class to compare them, and then practice the form that caused difficulty. Language focus responds to evidence from the task rather than replacing the task with a preselected grammar drill.

## Scaffolding and Assessment Inside a TBLT Classroom

Scaffolding, as defined in course material, is an instructional approach in which teachers prepare, present, and guide students through a series of progressively more complex steps to complete an activity or communicate a target language concept. In a TBLT classroom, the teacher builds the conditions under which students can succeed independently, rather than assigning a task and stepping back. A teacher working with an intermediate group might begin by modeling a short version of the target interaction, then move students through paired practice before asking them to attempt the full task in groups.

The module describes this role as being the "guide on the side" rather than the "sage on the stage," and the distinction matters practically. When Wei's group is preparing a presentation on local transport problems, the teacher circulates, listens, and asks a clarifying question such as:

T: You mentioned the bus schedule. Can you say more about who is affected?
S: Mostly older people and students who don't have cars.

The teacher has not supplied the content or the language. A well-placed question opens a gap that Wei's group fills with their own thinking, and that kind of targeted intervention keeps the communicative work where it belongs, with the students.

Assessment in a TBLT classroom needs to capture what students can do with language, not only what they know about it. A rubric, defined in the module as a scoring guide or template clearly linked to learning objectives, is well suited to this purpose because it makes performance criteria visible to students before the task begins. When students know in advance that their group presentation will be assessed on participation, vocabulary use, pronunciation, and the quality of audience questions, they can direct their preparation accordingly. The sample project rubric from the module illustrates this concretely: a group earns full marks on vocabulary use by incorporating five or more target words, on pronunciation by making those words clearly intelligible, and on audience engagement by preparing two questions for the class at the end. Each criterion corresponds to a communicative behavior, not an abstract linguistic category.

Checklists serve a related but distinct function. Where a rubric assigns scaled scores, a checklist gives students a concrete list of requirements they can verify as they work. A student like Fatima, reviewing her group's draft presentation, can confirm that the group has used the required vocabulary, that each member has a speaking role, and that the audience questions are written out. The module notes that checklists make expectations clear, allow students to work at their own pace, and reduce the need to ask the teacher for constant direction.

Peer assessment extends this principle by making students active participants in the evaluation process. When Fatima's group listens to another group's presentation and uses the same rubric criteria to give feedback, they are practicing the analytical attention to language that the task itself demands. The module is clear that peer feedback works best when teachers model the kind of response expected and remain available to supplement what peers may miss. Together, rubrics, checklists, and peer assessment form a coherent system in which the standards for success are shared openly, and students develop the capacity to monitor their own progress against those standards.

## The Research Base: What Meta-Analyses Tell Us

Bryfonski and McKay (2019) conducted a quantitative survey of task-based language teaching, drawing together 52 individual studies into a single meta-analysis. A meta-analysis is a statistical procedure that combines the results of many separate studies into one overall estimate, expressed as an effect size, a number that indicates how much better the group receiving a treatment performed compared to a group that did not. Bryfonski and McKay reported an effect size of d = 0.93, a figure that, by conventional benchmarks, sits in the large range and suggested that learners in task-based classrooms were outperforming those in comparison conditions by a substantial margin (Bryfonski & McKay, 2019).

That figure, however, did not go unchallenged. Xuan, Cheung and Liu (2025) returned to the same body of evidence and conducted a re-analysis, arguing that the original meta-analysis applied loose inclusion criteria, admitting studies whose designs were not sufficiently comparable. After applying tighter inclusion criteria, they analyzed a different study pool and arrived at a more modest effect size of g = 0.61 (Xuan, Cheung & Liu, 2025). The difference between the two estimates therefore reflects a substantive difference in which studies were included, and g = 0.61 is the figure this unit treats as the current best estimate.

An effect size of g = 0.61 represents a moderate positive advantage for learners taught through task-based methods over those in a comparison approach. That is a meaningful benefit, even if it falls short of the dramatic picture the original estimate painted.

The disagreement between the two analyses concerns magnitude, not direction. Both Bryfonski and McKay (2019) and Xuan, Cheung and Liu (2025) find that task-based instruction produces better outcomes than the conditions it is compared against. A teacher reading this evidence can therefore proceed with reasonable confidence that designing lessons around communicative tasks, rather than relying solely on form-focused drills, is likely to benefit learners. The honest position is that the size of that benefit remains a matter of ongoing methodological debate, and anyone citing the original d = 0.93 without acknowledging the revised estimate is presenting an incomplete picture of what the research currently supports.

## Why the Numbers Differ: Inclusion Criteria and What They Mean for Teachers

The gap between d = 0.93 and g = 0.61 reflects a substantive disagreement about which studies should have been included in the analysis. Xuan, Cheung and Liu (2025) argue that Bryfonski and McKay (2019) applied loose inclusion criteria, so some studies counted as TBLT research may not have implemented the approach in a recognizable or consistent form. When a meta-analysis draws on studies that only partially resemble the method under investigation, the resulting effect size reflects a blended picture rather than a clean test of that method. Tightening the criteria, as Xuan et al. (2025) did, removes that noise and produces the more conservative estimate of g = 0.61.

This disagreement carries a transferable lesson about reading research. An effect size is only as trustworthy as the studies feeding into it. A teacher who encounters a headline figure such as d = 0.93 in a professional development workshop should ask what counted as evidence for that number, rather than accepting it as settled fact. The same scrutiny applies in the other direction: a lower revised estimate does not mean the original researchers were careless, only that methodological choices shape outcomes in ways that later analysts can legitimately contest.

Understanding this distinction has direct classroom relevance. Course materials used throughout this unit emphasize that providing a rationale for an activity helps students understand why they are being asked to do it and reduces resistance to unfamiliar approaches (americanenglish.state.gov, Shaping the Way We Teach English). A teacher who grasps the difference between a settled directional finding and a contested magnitude is far better placed to give that rationale honestly. Consider Ms. Reyes explaining a group task to her adult ESOL class.

T: "We know from several large studies that working through tasks like this one tends to help you learn more than form-focused drills alone."
S: "How much better?"
T: "Researchers are still debating the exact size of the improvement, but every analysis points in the same direction."

That exchange is more credible than a confident citation of a single figure, and it models the kind of intellectual honesty that builds student trust over time.

The position that Xuan, Cheung and Liu (2025) arrive at alongside Bryfonski and McKay (2019) is therefore the defensible one: the direction of the effect is settled, and the magnitude remains contested. A corrected estimate of g = 0.61 still represents a meaningful, positive outcome for learners. Teachers can act on that finding with confidence while remaining open to further revision as the evidence base matures.

## Putting It Together: Applying TBLT Principles in Your Classroom

Designing a TBLT lesson begins with the material, not the grammar point. A teacher working with intermediate adults might notice that her students, including Fatima and Carlos, consistently bring up housing costs when chatting before class. That shared concern becomes the entry point: a task in which small groups research local rental listings, calculate what a shared apartment would cost, and present a recommendation to the class. The content carries weight because it connects to decisions the students will face outside the classroom, satisfying the first criterion on the Buck Institute's Project-based Learning Essential Elements Checklist (americanenglish.state.gov), which requires that projects be grounded in knowledge and skills that matter beyond the classroom.

Once the material is chosen, the teacher maps the integrated language skills the task will require. Fatima will need to read the listings, discuss options with her group, and speak clearly enough for an audience to follow her reasoning. Carlos will need to write a short justification for the group's final choice. Planning for those four skills together, rather than in separate lessons, reflects the module's observation that "skills are reinforced through meaningful repetition" when a single theme provides continuity (americanenglish.state.gov). The teacher can then design a sequence of pair and group activities that build toward the final product: first a paired comparison of two listings, then a group negotiation, then a rehearsed presentation.

The defined end product is what gives the sequence its shape. Without it, group work can drift, and students like Carlos may disengage once the novelty fades. A rubric tied to clear criteria, covering vocabulary use, participation, and the quality of the group's recommendation, tells students in advance what success looks like and gives the teacher a consistent basis for feedback. The checklist criterion of revision and reflection means that groups receive peer comments after a rehearsal and adjust their presentations before the final delivery. A brief exchange such as:

T: "What would make your recommendation clearer for someone who hasn't seen the listings?"
S: "Maybe we should explain why we ruled out the cheaper one."

illustrates how teacher prompting during the revision stage keeps students focused on communicative purpose rather than surface correction alone.

The public audience criterion completes the cycle. When Fatima's group presents to students from a parallel class rather than only to their own teacher, the stakes feel real, and the communicative purpose of the task becomes genuine. That shift from performance-for-assessment to performance-for-communication is the mechanism underlying the positive effects reported by both Bryfonski and McKay (2019) and, at a more conservative estimate, Xuan, Cheung and Liu (2025).

Teachers who follow this planning sequence can adopt TBLT with reasonable confidence. A corrected effect size of g = 0.61 is a meaningful result, and the direction of the evidence is not in dispute. What the meta-analyses cannot capture is the texture of a specific classroom: the mix of proficiency levels, the institutional constraints on time, the cultural expectations around public speaking that may make a student hesitant to present to an unfamiliar audience. Those factors belong to the teacher's professional judgment. The evidence provides a sound foundation; the adaptation is the teacher's work.
