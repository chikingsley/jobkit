# Modal Verbs

## What Makes a Modal Verb? Form and Core Features

Modal verbs form a closed set of auxiliary verbs in English. The set contains nine members: can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, and would. No new verbs join this group, and none leave it. Alongside these nine sit the semi-modals, a separate category that includes expressions such as have to, need to, be able to, and ought to. Semi-modals carry similar meanings but behave differently in grammar, and that contrast will matter throughout this unit.

All nine core modals share three formal properties that distinguish them from ordinary verbs. The first is the absence of a third-person singular inflection. Where a standard verb adds an ending in the present tense, a modal does not. A student named Yuki might write "She musts leave by six," but the correct form is "She must leave by six," with the modal staying the same regardless of the subject.

The second property is that a modal is always followed by a bare infinitive, meaning the base form of the verb without the particle to. "He should to call the office" is incorrect; the correct sentence is "He should call the office." A teacher might write both versions on the board and ask a student named Carlos to identify the error. Removing the to produces the grammatical form.

The third property is that modals form negatives and questions without do-support, the grammatical process by which ordinary verbs borrow the auxiliary do to form questions and negatives, as in "Do you understand?" or "She does not agree." Modals handle this independently. The question form is "Can you hear me?" not "Do you can hear me?", and the negative is "She cannot stay" rather than "She does not can stay." A student named Fatima producing "Do you will come tomorrow?" has applied the rule for ordinary verbs to a modal; the correction is "Will you come tomorrow?"

Semi-modals do not share these three properties. They behave more like ordinary verbs, requiring the full infinitive marker and do-support where appropriate. "She has to leave" takes the third-person singular has, not have. "Does he need to register?" uses do-support in the way a question about an ordinary verb would, not in the way a question about can or must would. A teacher working with a class that has just learned the core modals will need to present semi-modals as a related but distinct pattern, since applying the rules for can or must directly to have to produces errors.

## Ability and Permission: Can, Could, and Be Able To

The verb *can* carries two distinct meanings that learners must keep separate from the beginning. In one sense it expresses ability, the capacity to do something: "She can speak three languages" describes a present skill. In the other sense it expresses permission, the granting or requesting of leave to do something: "Can I leave early today?" is an informal request addressed to someone in authority. Both meanings are common in everyday speech, and a learner who conflates them may produce sentences that are grammatically correct but socially odd.

*Could* functions as the past tense of *can* when the meaning is ability. "He could swim before he started school" locates the skill in a completed period of the speaker's life. The same form also appears as a more tentative or polite alternative to *can* in present and future contexts: "Could I borrow your pen?" feels less direct than "Can I borrow your pen?" and is therefore preferred in more formal situations. This double role is a genuine source of confusion, because the same word does two quite different jobs depending on context. A teacher can make the distinction concrete by writing both sentences on the board and asking a student, Fatima, to say which one she would use when speaking to a close friend and which to a new employer.

A common error at this stage is the insertion of *to* after *can*. A student named Wei might write "I can to drive" by analogy with semi-modals such as *have to* or *be able to*. The correction is straightforward: *can* is a core modal and takes a bare infinitive, so the target sentence is "I can drive." Pointing out that *can* never takes *to* is usually sufficient, though the error tends to reappear when students are tired or working quickly.

*Be able to* is a semi-modal that covers the grammatical territory where *can* has no form. Because *can* exists only in the present and past, a speaker who needs a future or perfect construction must turn to *be able to* instead. "She will be able to attend the meeting" supplies the future that *can* cannot. "He has been able to manage the workload" supplies the present perfect. In a classroom exercise, a teacher might ask students to rewrite "I can finish by Friday" using a future time marker, prompting them to produce "I will be able to finish by Friday" rather than the impossible "I will can finish."

One further confusion to address is the overlap between *could* as past ability and *could* as a polite present request. When a student, Priya, writes "Could you help me yesterday?", she has applied the polite-request meaning to a past time reference, which produces an unacceptable sentence. Past ability takes *could* freely, but past permission or requests require different constructions entirely. Separating these uses early prevents the error from becoming habitual.

## Possibility and Probability: May, Might, and Could

The three modals *may*, *might*, and *could* all express epistemic possibility, meaning the speaker is making a judgment about how likely something is rather than stating a fact. They are not equivalent in the degree of certainty they signal. Recognizing these differences allows learners to match their language to their actual meaning with much greater precision.

*May* presents a situation as a genuine open possibility, one the speaker considers reasonably plausible. A sentence such as "It may rain this afternoon" suggests the speaker sees rain as a real and fairly balanced prospect. The speaker is not dismissing the idea, nor treating it as a remote chance. In classroom terms, if a student named Carlos writes "The director may attend the meeting," he is signaling that attendance is a live option, not a long shot.

*Might* shifts the balance toward something more tentative or remote. "I might come to the party, but I'm not sure" conveys that the speaker leans toward uncertainty. The possibility exists, but the speaker is distancing themselves from it. If a student, Yuki, writes "There might be a delay," she is suggesting the delay is conceivable but not something she is ready to treat as likely.

*Could*, in its epistemic use, presents one possibility among several that the speaker is weighing. "That noise could be the neighbors" does not commit to that explanation; it places it on a list of candidates rather than endorsing it. A parallel set of sentences makes the contrast visible: "The keys may be in my bag" (a real, open possibility), "The keys might be in my bag" (a more doubtful guess), "The keys could be in my bag" (one of several places worth checking). Students who see these three sentences side by side begin to feel the gradient rather than treating the words as synonyms.

A common learner belief is that *may* and *might* are completely interchangeable, and in informal spoken English the boundary between them has certainly blurred. For writing, and for precision in speaking, the distinction remains useful. A teacher can address this directly in class. T: "If I say 'I may resign,' how does that feel compared to 'I might resign'?" S: "The first one sounds more serious, like you are genuinely considering it." T: "The second sounds more like a vague possibility you have not yet committed to weighing." That contrast, once heard, tends to stay with learners.

An additional complication is that *may* carries a second, entirely separate meaning: formal permission. "Students may use dictionaries during the test" is a permission statement, not a possibility claim. This creates genuine ambiguity in sentences like "You may leave early," which could mean either that leaving early is possible or that it is permitted. Learners who encounter *may* only in possibility contexts are often caught off guard when they meet the permission sense in formal written English, so flagging both meanings early prevents later confusion. The modals *might* and *could* do not share this permission function in standard contemporary usage, which makes them unambiguous in possibility contexts.

## Obligation and Necessity: Must, Have To, and Should

English uses three main structures to express obligation, and they are not interchangeable. Each signals a different source or degree of compulsion, and learners who treat them as synonyms will produce sentences that are grammatically acceptable but pragmatically wrong.

The modal *must* expresses strong obligation originating with the speaker, or logical necessity the speaker has reasoned out. When a teacher says to a student, "You must hand in your essay before noon," the obligation comes from the teacher's own authority. The same modal appears in logical deductions: if a student named Wei has been in class since eight in the morning and it is now five in the afternoon, a teacher might say, "Wei must be exhausted." In that second sentence, *must* signals a conclusion the speaker has reached, not a rule imposed from outside.

*Have to* expresses obligation that comes from an external source, such as a rule, a law, or a circumstance the speaker did not create. A student named Priya might say, "I have to wear a uniform at my school," meaning the school's policy requires it, not that Priya herself has decided this. The distinction matters in the classroom because a teacher who says "You must read chapter three" is using personal authority, while a teacher who says "You have to read chapter three" implies the syllabus or examination board is the source of that requirement. Both sentences are grammatically correct; the choice between them changes who is responsible for the obligation.

*Should* and *ought to* express a weaker degree of obligation, closer to advice or recommendation than strict necessity. A teacher might tell a student named Carlos, "You should review your vocabulary notes before the test," meaning it would be beneficial, not that failure to do so carries a penalty. *Ought to* carries the same meaning and is slightly more formal, though it appears far less frequently in spoken English. Neither *should* nor *ought to* implies that the action is compelled by an external authority or by the speaker's direct power.

One of the most persistent difficulties at intermediate level involves the past tense of *must*. The modal has no past tense form for obligation, so learners must use *had to* in its place. A student who wants to say that she was required to stay late at work yesterday cannot say "I must stay late yesterday"; the correct form is "I had to stay late yesterday." Teachers can anticipate this gap by presenting *had to* explicitly alongside *must* rather than waiting for the error to appear.

The contrast between *must not* and *don't have to* causes equally persistent confusion. *Must not* expresses prohibition: the action is forbidden. *Don't have to* expresses the absence of obligation, meaning the action is not required. The following exchange illustrates the error:

T: "Can you see the difference between these two sentences? 'You must not use your phone in the exam' and 'You don't have to use a pen.'"
S: "The first one means it's not allowed?"
T: "Exactly. And the second one?"
S: "Maybe... it's also not allowed?"
T: "No, it means a pen is not required. You can use one if you want."

Learners map both forms onto prohibition because both contain a negative element, but the meanings are entirely distinct. The error is predictable. Addressing it directly, with a paired example like the one above, gives learners a concrete contrast to hold onto rather than an abstract rule to misapply.

## Willingness, Prediction, and Politeness: Will, Would, and Shall

The modal verb *will* carries two main functions that teachers should distinguish clearly from the start. In its predictive use, *will* expresses what the speaker believes to be true about the future: *It will be cold tomorrow* reflects a judgment about a future state. In its volitional use, *will* expresses willingness or a spontaneous decision made at the moment of speaking. The offer *I'll carry that for you* is formed in the instant, not derived from prior reasoning about the future. A student named Fatima might write *I will carry that for you* in a formal letter and *I'll carry that for you* in a spoken role-play; both are correct, and the contraction signals informality rather than a shift in meaning.

*Would* is the past form of *will* in reported speech, and this is often the first context in which learners encounter it. When a speaker reports what someone said earlier, the tense shifts back: *She said she would call* reports an original statement of *I will call*. Beyond reported speech, *would* functions as the modal of hypothetical and polite contexts. In a conditional sentence such as *I would move abroad if I could*, *would* signals that the situation is imagined rather than real. In a polite offer or request such as *Would you like some coffee?* or *Would you mind closing the door?*, it softens the directness of the utterance. A teacher can demonstrate this contrast by asking a student named Carlos to compare *Do you want coffee?* with *Would you like coffee?* and to describe which feels more formal; most learners identify the difference immediately, even if they cannot yet explain it grammatically.

*Shall* presents a narrower teaching challenge. In contemporary English it is largely restricted to formal British usage, appearing mainly in first-person offers and suggestions: *Shall I open the window?* and *Shall we begin?* are natural in that register. Learners from many language backgrounds encounter *shall* rarely in the spoken or written input available to them, so it can feel archaic or puzzling when it does appear. A student named Yuki, working through a formal British text, may ask why *shall* appears where *will* or *should* might seem equally possible; the honest answer is that *shall* in these contexts is a convention of register rather than a grammatical necessity.

Two persistent errors deserve direct attention in the classroom. The first is the use of *will* inside the *if*-clause of a first conditional sentence. Learners frequently produce *If it will rain, we will cancel the trip* by analogy with the main clause, but the standard form requires a present simple in the condition: *If it rains, we will cancel the trip*. The second error involves *would* for past habit, as in *When I was young, I would walk to school*. Correct. However, learners sometimes confuse this pattern with *used to*, which covers both past habits and past states. Because *used to* is treated separately in the unit on aspect, noting the overlap here is sufficient, and learners can be told that the distinction will be addressed in full later.

## Teaching Modal Verbs: Typical Learner Difficulties and Classroom Strategies

Four structural errors account for the great majority of mistakes learners make with modal verbs, and recognizing them allows a teacher to plan targeted responses rather than reteaching entire paradigms from scratch.

The first error is inserting *to* directly after a modal, producing forms such as *She can to swim* or *He must to leave*. Modal verbs in English take a bare infinitive, meaning the base form of the verb without *to*. A teacher can address this quickly by writing a correct and an incorrect sentence on the board and asking learners to identify the difference. When a student named Yuki writes *We should to practice more*, the correction is immediate and the rule is easy to state: the modal itself carries the meaning, and *to* adds nothing.

The second error follows a similar logic. Because most English verbs add *-s* in the third-person singular present, learners extend that pattern to modals. A student named Carlos may write *She cans drive* or *He musts finish*. Modals do not inflect for person or number, so the form stays identical across all subjects. Drilling short substitution exercises, where learners swap subjects while keeping the modal unchanged, tends to correct this quickly.

The third error is more serious because it involves meaning rather than form. *Must not* and *don't have to* look parallel but express opposite ideas. *Must not* signals prohibition: the action is forbidden. *Don't have to* signals absence of obligation: the action is optional. Minimal-pair sentences make the contrast visible. A teacher might present these two sentences side by side: *You must not park here* and *You don't have to pay for parking on Sundays*. A short classroom exchange reinforces the point:

T: "So if I say 'You must not use your phone in the exam,' what does that mean?"
S: "It's forbidden."
T: "And 'You don't have to bring a dictionary'?"
S: "I can bring one, but it's not necessary."

The fourth difficulty is subtler. Learners often assume that one modal in their first language maps cleanly onto one modal in English. A student named Fatima, whose first language uses a single form for both permission and possibility, may use *can* and *may* interchangeably without noticing any difference in register or formality. Presenting modals grouped by meaning field, such as ability, obligation, and permission, rather than teaching each modal in isolation, helps learners build a network of contrasts. When *can*, *could*, *may*, and *might* appear together in a permission cluster, the distinctions between them become visible in a way that a single-modal lesson cannot achieve.

Short dialogues give learners a sense of how modals behave in real exchanges:

T: "Can you stay after class, Daniel?"
S: "I might be able to. I have to check my bus time."

T: "Should we submit the report today?"
S: "We could wait until tomorrow. We don't have to send it before Friday."

Because modals carry no inflection and resist reduction to a single rule, isolated grammar drills give learners very little to hold onto. Repeated encounters with modals inside meaningful, contextualised exchanges, whether in reading texts, listening tasks, or short conversations like those above, build the kind of flexible recognition that drills alone cannot produce.
