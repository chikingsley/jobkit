# Components of a Lesson Plan

## What Is a Lesson Plan and Why Use One?

A lesson plan is a written document that a teacher prepares before a class session to organize what will happen during that session. It records the objectives students are expected to meet, the skills and content the lesson addresses, the sequence of activities, the materials required, and the procedures the teacher will follow. Because all of these elements appear in one place, the teacher can move through the session with a clear sense of direction rather than making decisions on the spot.

The blank Lesson Plan Template described in the source text illustrates this range of information well. Teachers use it to specify lesson objectives, skill and content focus areas, lesson sequencing, materials, and procedures. The template can also be modified to suit the needs of a particular teaching context, so a teacher working with thirty-six adult learners at a community college and a teacher working with a small group of teenagers in a secondary school can each adapt the same basic form to fit their circumstances.

A completed plan serves two distinct purposes. Before the lesson, it functions as a preparation guide: the teacher thinks through each stage, anticipates what students will need, and gathers materials in advance. After the lesson, the completed document becomes a record of what was planned, one the teacher can compare against what occurred in the classroom.

Looking at a filled-in example is one of the most direct ways to understand what a lesson plan contains. The example plan in the source shows a teacher at Eastern College preparing an eighty-minute session for intermediate adult learners. The plan records background information about the students, numbered objectives such as the ability to identify main ideas in a listening text, a time-coded sequence of procedures, and notes on materials and rationale for each stage. A new teacher reading that example can see concretely what level of detail is expected and how the various components fit together.

Consider a teacher named Maria who is preparing her first formal lesson plan. Without a written plan, she might arrive in class knowing she wants to practise past continuous tense but uncertain how long to spend on each activity. With a completed plan, she has already decided that the warm-up runs five minutes, the listening task runs fifteen, and the pair interview activity runs twenty, leaving her free to focus on her students rather than on logistics during the session itself.

## Background Information and Lesson Objectives

The background information section of a lesson plan records the contextual facts a teacher needs before writing a single objective or planning a single activity. In the lesson plan example from the source text, this section captures the course name ("Introduction to English 102" at Eastern College), the total session length of two and a half hours, and the specific eighty-minute block the plan addresses. It also records that the class contains thirty-six intermediate-level students between the ages of twenty and forty-five, with a sixty-to-forty female-to-male ratio. Details like these shape every subsequent decision, from the complexity of a listening text to the amount of time allocated for pair work.

The description of students goes beyond age and number. In the example, the teacher has noted that most learners hold or are seeking jobs in service industries such as retail and hospitality, or in technical fields such as cable repair and mechanics. Their motivations include communicating at work, securing better employment, and helping their children with schoolwork. A teacher reading this background section immediately understands why a lesson built around road safety and traffic accident vocabulary is relevant to this particular group, rather than being an arbitrary topic choice.

Lesson objectives follow directly from that context. An objective is a statement of what students will be able to do by the end of the lesson, written in observable, measurable terms. The example provides five such statements. Students will orally identify common causes of traffic accidents; they will determine main ideas in a listening text about post-accident police interviews; they will identify specific details in the same listening text; they will use unit vocabulary and past continuous tense to interview a classmate about a traffic accident; and, time permitting, they will orally report details about a traffic accident to another student. Notice that each statement names a skill, a language form, or both, and specifies the context in which students will perform it. A vague objective such as "students will practise listening" gives a teacher no way to judge whether the lesson has succeeded; the objectives in the example do not have that problem.

The field asking how the lesson connects to previous and subsequent sessions gives the objectives their place in a larger sequence. In the example, the preceding class was a grammar lesson on using the past continuous to report events, so students arrive already familiar with the target structure. The following class will be a writing lesson in which students draft a letter to city officials about a dangerous traffic area. The lesson in between therefore serves a clear bridging function: it consolidates the grammar introduced earlier and builds the content knowledge students will draw on when they write. Imagine a student named Carlos who struggled with past continuous forms in the previous session. Because the teacher has mapped this connection explicitly, she knows to build in a brief review before the listening task, rather than discovering mid-lesson that Carlos and several classmates are still uncertain about the structure. That kind of anticipation is only possible when the background and objectives sections are completed with care.

## Skills, Content Focus, and Timing

The specific skills and content focus component gives the teacher a concise record of what the lesson covers, distinct from the broader objectives. In the example lesson from Eastern College, this section lists four items: a grammar review of the past continuous tense, listening for gist and detail, asking about and reporting events, and theme-based content on road safety and auto care. Each entry names something concrete and teachable. A teacher reading the plan at a glance can identify a focused session built around a particular grammar structure, a particular skill, and a particular topic.

The grammar and skills entries work together rather than in isolation. The past continuous review, for instance, is not an end in itself; it gives students the language they need to complete the listening tasks and the pair interview activity. Listening for gist means identifying the main idea of a text, while listening for detail means locating specific information within it. Both are named separately because they require different kinds of attention from the student. When a teacher named Priya plans her board work and comprehension questions, she can use this section to check that she has addressed each skill type rather than inadvertently spending the whole lesson on one.

Theme-based content appears as a distinct entry because the topic of road safety carries its own vocabulary and background knowledge. Students in the example class are adults with jobs in service and technical industries, and road safety is a subject many of them encounter directly. Naming the theme in the skills and content section reminds the teacher that content knowledge is part of what is being taught, not merely a backdrop for grammar practice.

Timing is recorded in a separate column that runs alongside the procedure table. In the example, each stage is marked with a time stamp written as elapsed minutes from the start of class: the lesson opens at :00 and the warm-up activity begins at :05. Short. Precise. This format tells the teacher how many minutes into the session each stage should begin, rather than tying the plan to a fixed clock time. The example covers an 80-minute lesson that forms the first half of a 2.5-hour session, so the time column also signals where the lesson ends relative to the full class meeting. A teacher named James, glancing at his plan mid-lesson, can see at once whether he is running ahead or behind without having to calculate from a fixed start time. The timing column records the teacher's intentions before class and provides a reference point during it.

## Procedure and Materials

The procedure column records, step by step, what the teacher and students will do at each stage of the lesson. A well-written procedure entry is specific enough that a substitute teacher could follow it without additional explanation, yet concise enough to be read at a glance during class. Specificity is the standard.

The warm-up stage from the example lesson illustrates this clearly. At the zero-minute mark, the teacher greets the students and displays four pictures on the whiteboard: a woman applying makeup while behind the wheel, a man talking on a cell phone while driving, a woman texting, and a man yawning in the driver's seat. The procedure entry does not say "show pictures about driving." It names the specific images, because the choice of image carries instructional intent. A teacher named Maria, reading this entry before class, knows which pictures to prepare and the context in which they will appear.

The materials and rationale column sits alongside the procedure and serves two distinct functions recorded in a single field. The first is logistical: it lists what the teacher needs to carry out that step. For the warm-up described above, the column records "printouts of pictures, tape," telling the teacher precisely what to gather before the session begins. The second function is explanatory; it states why each element is present. The same entry notes that the activity is intended to "create positive, welcoming atmosphere" and to "create interest; activate prior knowledge related to road safety," preventing the plan from becoming a bare sequence of actions with no pedagogical logic attached.

Keeping the rationale visible inside the plan has a practical benefit. When a teacher named David reviews his lesson after class and finds that the warm-up ran too long, the rationale column reminds him what the activity was meant to accomplish. He can then judge whether a shorter version would still meet those purposes, or whether a different activity would serve them better. The rationale is a diagnostic tool, not decoration.

The lesson plan template also includes separate, dedicated materials fields that appear outside the main time-procedure table. These fields collect materials information at the lesson level rather than the stage level, giving the teacher a single place to check everything needed before walking into the room. This mirrors the principle described in the source materials for project-based lessons, where each lesson plan lists the specific materials needed to complete a particular project along with suggestions for variations in case different resources are on hand. A teacher working in a school with no color printer can consult that variation note and decide to draw simple sketches on the board rather than abandon the activity entirely. The materials column, whether embedded in the procedure table or gathered in a separate field, is where practical classroom reality meets the plan on paper.

## Assessment, Notes, and Pre-Class Preparation

Three fields in the lesson plan template sit outside the main procedure table yet carry information that shapes how a session runs before, during, and after instruction. Each field has a distinct purpose, and leaving any of them blank reduces the template to a bare schedule of activities.

The Assessment field is where the teacher records what will be checked or collected to determine whether students have met the lesson objectives. In the example lesson from Eastern College, the objectives are stated in measurable terms: students should be able to identify main ideas in a listening text and use past continuous tense to interview a classmate about a traffic accident. The Assessment field specifies how the teacher will know whether those outcomes were reached. A teacher might write that she will circulate during the pair interview activity and note whether students like Maria and David are producing past continuous forms accurately, or that she will collect written responses to comprehension questions at the end of the listening segment. No formal test is required; a clear statement of what evidence the teacher will gather is sufficient.

The Notes field holds contextual reminders that do not belong in the procedure steps themselves. A teacher might record that one student has a hearing difficulty and should be seated near the front, or that the projector in Room 14 requires a specific adapter that must be requested from the office. These details are not procedural, but forgetting them can disrupt a lesson that is otherwise well planned. The Notes field gives them a designated place so they are visible when the teacher reviews the plan before class.

The Pre-class Preparation field lists tasks the teacher must complete before students arrive. In the Eastern College example, the warm-up requires four pictures displayed on the whiteboard, so the teacher must print or locate those images, bring tape, and arrange the room in advance. Pre-class preparation might also include writing target vocabulary on the board, setting up audio equipment, or photocopying handouts. Recording these tasks in the template allows the teacher to work through them systematically rather than discovering at the start of class that a key material is missing.

T: "Does everyone have a copy of the interview worksheet?"
S: "I don't have one."
T: "Neither do I. I forgot to print them."

That exchange is one the Pre-class Preparation field is designed to prevent. A teacher who has written "photocopy interview worksheet x30" in the template and checked it off before class will not face that moment.

## Lesson Evaluation: Reflecting After the Class

The final section of the lesson plan template is the Lesson Evaluation, a set of four questions the teacher completes after the class has ended. The questions are: "What do you think the students learned? How do you know?"; "What parts were most successful/least successful? Why?"; "Did you finish the lesson on time? If not, why?"; and "What changes (if any) will you make in your teaching and why (or why not)?" Each question targets a distinct aspect of the lesson, and together they form a structured habit of reflection rather than a vague general impression of how things went.

The first question asks the teacher to compare what students appeared to learn against what the lesson objectives stated they would learn. A teacher who set out to have students use the past continuous tense in a role-play interview, as in the Eastern College lesson plan example, might notice that most students managed the grammar in prepared exchanges but struggled when the conversation became spontaneous. Answering "How do you know?" pushes the teacher to point to specific evidence, such as what students said, wrote, or produced, rather than a general feeling that the class went well.

The second question separates the lesson into its parts and asks the teacher to evaluate each one honestly. A warm-up activity using pictures of distracted drivers might have generated lively discussion, while a subsequent listening task may have been too long for the time available. Naming which parts succeeded and which did not, and explaining why, gives the teacher concrete information to carry into the next planning cycle.

The third question addresses timing directly. If the lesson ran over, the teacher needs to identify where time was lost. Perhaps pair-work instructions were unclear and had to be repeated, or students needed more time with the vocabulary than the plan allowed. This question connects back to the timing column in the main lesson plan, where each procedure was assigned a specific number of minutes. A mismatch between planned and actual time signals that the sequencing or the time estimates need adjustment.

The fourth question asks the teacher to decide on concrete changes. Reflection here becomes preparation for the next lesson. The source material's action-plan prompt reinforces this directly, asking teachers to "reflect on what went well and what you might do differently" and to consider "alternative approaches or strategies" for the future. A teacher might decide to shorten the warm-up, replace a whole-class activity with pair work, or choose a different listening text at a more appropriate level.

T: "I noticed most of you stopped using the past continuous halfway through the role-play. What happened?"
S: "I forgot the grammar when I had to think about what to say."

That exchange, noted in the Lesson Evaluation, becomes the starting point for the next lesson plan. The evaluation section closes the loop between planning and teaching, turning each completed lesson into the foundation for a better one.
