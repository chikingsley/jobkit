# Common Learner Errors by First-Language Background

## Why First-Language Background Shapes Learner Errors

Errors in a learner's English are rarely accidental. They follow patterns that a trained teacher can recognize, predict, and address, because most errors arise from the systematic influence of the learner's first language, known as the L1. When a learner produces an English sentence, the grammatical habits of the L1 are already active, shaping every choice from word order to the use of articles to the placement of verbs.

The term for this influence is transfer. Transfer occurs when a learner applies an L1 rule to English, and the outcome depends entirely on whether the two languages agree. Positive transfer happens when an L1 rule and the corresponding English rule work the same way, making acquisition of that feature relatively straightforward. Negative transfer happens when the rules diverge and the learner's automatic application of the L1 habit produces an English error.

A Spanish-speaking student named Carlos might write "He is teacher" instead of "He is a teacher," not because he has ignored the lesson on articles, but because Spanish allows the indefinite article to be omitted after the verb "ser" in certain constructions. His L1 system is functioning as a coherent grammar; the two languages differ on this point. A Japanese-speaking student named Yuki might produce "She yesterday the book read," placing the verb at the end of the clause, because Japanese is a verb-final language and that word order is grammatically correct in her L1. In both cases the error is the product of a rule-governed system applied to a language with different rules.

Recognizing this has a direct practical consequence for classroom preparation. A teacher who knows that a class is composed largely of Mandarin speakers can anticipate difficulty with English tense marking, since Mandarin expresses time through adverbs and context rather than verb inflection. That teacher can prepare explicit explanations and controlled practice before the errors appear in student writing, rather than correcting the same mistake repeatedly after the fact. The exchange below illustrates how awareness of transfer can surface naturally in a lesson.

T: "Yuki, can you tell me what you did last weekend?"
S: "I go to the park with my family."
T: "Good, thank you. Now, because this happened in the past, we need to change the verb. What form do we use?"

The teacher in that exchange already expected the omission of past tense marking and had a ready explanation. That readiness comes from understanding transfer, and it is the foundation on which the rest of this unit is built.

## Article Errors: When English Asks for 'A' or 'The' and the L1 Doesn't

English uses three article choices, and each one carries a specific meaning. The definite article "the" signals that the speaker and listener share an understanding of which particular thing is meant. The indefinite article "a" or "an" introduces a singular countable noun whose identity is either new to the conversation or unspecified. The zero article, meaning the absence of any article, applies to plural nouns and uncountable nouns when the reference is general rather than specific. A sentence such as "Dogs make good pets" uses zero article because it refers to dogs as a category, not to particular dogs the listener already knows about.

Learners whose first language contains no article system face a genuine structural gap. Russian, Japanese, Korean, and Mandarin Chinese all encode definiteness and specificity through other means: word order, context, demonstrative pronouns, or, in Japanese and Korean, grammatical particles. When a speaker of one of these languages produces an English sentence, the article slot is absent from the mental template being applied. That gap is a transfer problem, not a sign of inattention.

The most frequent result is omission. A Russian-speaking student might write "She is teacher" instead of "She is a teacher," because Russian uses no article to introduce an unspecified role or profession. A Korean-speaking student might say "I went to bank this morning" when meaning a specific bank she visits regularly, omitting "the" because Korean would convey that specificity through context alone. In both cases the learner has communicated something, but the English listener loses information that the article would have provided.

Overcorrection produces a different pattern. Having learned that English requires articles, some learners begin inserting "the" where zero article is required. A Mandarin-speaking student named Wei might write "The life is short" or "The music makes me happy," applying "the" to uncountable nouns used in a general sense. In English, those sentences imply a specific life or a specific piece of music already identified in the conversation. The general philosophical statement requires zero article: "Life is short," "Music makes me happy."

A short decision-tree sequence gives teachers a practical way to walk learners through the choice in real time. The first question is whether the noun is countable and singular. If so, the next question is whether the noun appears here for the first time in the conversation or text. A first mention with no specific identity calls for "a" or "an." A noun already introduced, or one where only a single referent could logically exist in context, calls for "the." If the noun is uncountable or plural and the meaning is general, zero article applies. A brief exchange in class might look like this:

T: "Wei, you wrote 'He is teacher.' Is 'teacher' countable and singular here?"
S: "Yes."
T: "Is this her first mention, and do we know which specific teacher she is?"
S: "First time, and not specific."
T: "So which article fits a first mention with no specific identity?"
S: "A. He is a teacher."

Running this sequence aloud several times gives learners an internalized procedure rather than a memorized rule they cannot apply under pressure. The questions become a habit. That habit gradually replaces the L1 default of producing no article at all, because learners are making a conscious choice at each step rather than relying on an instinct their first language never built.

## Word-Order Errors: Subject, Verb, Object and Its Variations

English declarative sentences follow a fixed Subject-Verb-Object sequence, and this order is not negotiable in the way it is in many other languages. In Japanese, Korean, and Turkish, the verb typically appears at the end of the clause, after the object, giving those languages a Subject-Object-Verb pattern. A learner whose grammar is built around that pattern will sometimes carry it into English, producing sentences such as "She the book read" or "I yesterday to school went." The meaning is usually recoverable, but the sentences sound incomplete to a native speaker, who expects the verb to anchor the predicate immediately after the subject.

A teacher encountering this pattern can make the structural mismatch explicit without lengthy explanation. Consider a learner like Yuki who writes "She every morning coffee drinks." The teacher can respond:

T: "Yuki, where is the verb in this sentence?"
S: "Drinks."
T: "In English, the verb comes directly after the subject. What is the subject?"
S: "She."
T: "So what comes right after 'she'?"
S: "Drinks. She drinks coffee every morning."

Pointing to the subject and asking where the verb belongs gives Yuki a positional rule she can apply sentence by sentence, rather than a general instruction to "sound more natural."

Question formation presents a separate set of difficulties, because English requires both an auxiliary verb and subject-verb inversion, a combination absent from many learners' first languages. A speaker of Mandarin, Arabic, or Turkish can form a yes-no question by adding a particle or by using rising intonation on a declarative sentence. The result in English is a sentence like "You are coming tomorrow?" used as a direct question, or "She can drive?" where the auxiliary stays in place but inversion does not occur. These forms are understandable in casual speech, but they mark the learner as someone who has not yet acquired the inversion rule, and they become genuinely ambiguous in writing.

Negatives produce a parallel problem. Many languages form negation by inserting a negative particle directly before the main verb, with no auxiliary required. A learner following that pattern writes "She not likes coffee" or "He not finished the report." In English, negation in the simple present and simple past requires the auxiliary "do" to carry both tense and the negative marker, leaving the main verb in its base form. A learner like Carlos who writes "I not understand the question" needs to see that the structure is "I do not understand," where "do" holds the tense and "not" follows it.

Adjective placement causes a persistent error for speakers of Arabic, French, Spanish, and other languages that place descriptive adjectives after the noun they modify. In those grammars, a phrase meaning "a red fast car" would be constructed with the adjectives following "car." The English equivalent of that pattern produces "I have a car red and fast." English places attributive adjectives before the noun, and when a learner produces the post-nominal form, the sentence reads as unfinished or as a clumsy attempt at emphasis. A brief contrastive note, showing the learner's L1 pattern alongside the English pattern, is usually enough to make the positional rule clear, since the learner already controls the adjectives themselves and needs only to relocate them.

Each of these error types points to a specific structural feature of the learner's first language that conflicts with English grammar. Identifying the module of the mismatch allows the teacher to give targeted, efficient feedback rather than marking the sentence as incorrect and moving on.

## Tense and Aspect Errors: Marking Time in English

English marks time through a combination of tense and aspect, and the interaction between the two creates persistent difficulty for learners from a wide range of first-language backgrounds. Tense locates an event in time relative to the moment of speaking; aspect describes how that event is viewed, whether as complete, ongoing, or connected to the present. Many learners arrive in the classroom with a solid grasp of English vocabulary but produce sentences that feel temporally misaligned to a native speaker, because their first language organizes time differently.

The contrast between the simple past and the present perfect is one of the most reliable sources of error at intermediate level. The simple past locates an event at a specific, identified point in past time: "I saw her yesterday" places the event firmly in a completed, named moment. The present perfect connects a past event to the present moment without specifying when it occurred: "I have seen that film" signals relevance to now rather than a fixed point in the past. Many first languages draw the boundary between these two functions in a different place. Spanish and Italian each have a perfect built from "have" plus a participle, but its territory does not match the English one: a speaker from Madrid can use the Spanish perfect for this morning's finished events, and spoken Italian routinely uses its perfect form as an all-purpose past. Several varieties of Arabic, meanwhile, cover both functions with a single past form. Learners transfer the boundaries of the home system into English and apply the two forms inconsistently. Fatima might write "I have seen her yesterday," attaching a specific time marker to the present perfect, while Carlos might say "I lived in this city for ten years" about the city he still lives in, using the simple past where the present perfect is required because the period continues into the present.

T: Carlos, you said "I lived in this city for ten years." Do you still live here?
S: Yes, ten years, and I live here now.
T: Then we need "I have lived here for ten years." The "have" tells me those ten years reach up to now.

The continuous, or progressive, aspect signals that an action is in progress at a specific moment, as in "She was reading when I arrived." English applies this aspect freely to dynamic verbs but not to stative verbs, a category that includes mental states and perceptions such as knowing, believing, and understanding. The concept of ongoing action is rarely the obstacle: Mandarin, for example, marks an action in progress with the particle "zai" before the verb. What trips learners up is the boundary English draws around the form, and they either omit it where English requires it or stretch it past its limits. Wei might produce "I am knowing the answer" by extending the progressive pattern to a stative verb, while Priya might say "She read when I came in," omitting the progressive entirely and losing the sense of an interrupted ongoing action.

T: Wei, can you say "I am knowing"?
S: Is that wrong? I know it now, at this moment.
T: "Know" describes a state, not an action in progress, so we say "I know the answer."

A separate but related problem affects speakers of languages that carry no grammatical tense marking at all. In Mandarin, time is indicated through adverbs and context rather than verb inflection. Wei may therefore produce "Yesterday I go to the market," leaving the verb in its base form because the adverb "yesterday" already signals the time. The verb ending feels redundant from the perspective of the first language, even though English requires it.

A practical classroom response to all three problem areas is to teach the time expressions that co-occur with each tense form as part of the same lesson. Words like "yesterday," "last week," and "in 2019" belong with the simple past; words like "already," "just," "ever," and "since" belong with the present perfect; phrases like "at that moment" and "while" signal the progressive. When learners see these pairings presented together, the tense or aspect choice becomes less abstract. Matching the right grammatical form to the right temporal signal gives learners a concrete decision procedure rather than an appeal to intuition they have not yet developed.

## Preposition Errors and Pronunciation-Driven Spelling Mistakes

English prepositions are largely idiomatic, meaning their correct use depends on convention rather than on any consistent logical relationship between the preposition and its meaning. A learner cannot reliably predict which preposition English requires by understanding the situation being described. The preposition that works in the learner's first language will often be a different word entirely, and direct translation produces errors that are both systematic and persistent.

A Spanish-speaking student named Carlos might write "I dreamed with my grandmother last night" because Spanish uses "soñar con," and "con" translates literally as "with." A French-speaking student might produce "She depends of her parents," carrying over the French construction "dépend de." A German or Italian learner might write "He is good in tennis," applying a preposition that feels logical in his own language but is wrong in English, where the correct form is "good at tennis." In each case the error is not random; it is a direct reflection of the learner's first-language system.

These errors are among the most persistent in learner writing because there is rarely a rule that resolves them. The teacher cannot explain to Carlos by any principled rule why English speakers dream "about" or "of" someone rather than "with" them; the pairing is a fixed feature of English, and no deeper logic governs it. Prepositions therefore need to be taught as part of fixed collocations and lexical chunks, the term for multi-word units stored and retrieved as single items. A teacher presenting the word "depend" should introduce the full phrase "depend on someone" from the start, so that the preposition is bound to the verb in the learner's memory rather than selected separately each time.

T: "Priya, you wrote 'she depends of her parents.' In English, the verb is always 'depend on.' Say the whole phrase for me."
S: "She depends on her parents."
T: "Good. Write that phrase in your vocabulary notebook exactly like that, with the preposition included."

Chunk-based recording of this kind gives learners a retrievable unit rather than a rule they must apply under pressure.

A second cross-linguistic error type involves spelling that reflects the learner's phonology rather than standard English orthography. When a learner's first language does not distinguish between the vowel sounds /ɪ/ and /iː/, that learner may write "I will leve soon" for "leave," spelling the word as it sounds through the filter of a system that treats the two sounds as one. A learner whose first language permits fewer consonant clusters may reduce "listen" to something like "lis," writing "I like to lis music." These spelling errors are diagnostic, meaning they reveal a specific gap in the learner's phonological awareness rather than a gap in vocabulary knowledge.

The most direct remedy is to address the pronunciation before correcting the spelling. Once a learner can reliably hear and produce the distinction between /ɪ/ and /iː/, the spelling of "leave" versus "live" becomes easier to anchor. A teacher who recognizes the phonological source of a spelling error can target the sound in controlled pronunciation practice, then return to the written form with a learner who now has an auditory model to match. Treating the spelling error in isolation, without attending to the underlying sound, tends to produce only temporary improvement.

## Responding to Predictable Errors: Practical Classroom Strategies

Because L1-influenced errors follow recognizable patterns, a teacher who knows the first-language background of a class can prepare for likely problems before the first lesson begins. Preparation of this kind shapes lesson planning, materials selection, and the sequencing of grammar instruction. Anticipating difficulty is a different posture from waiting for errors to appear and then improvising a response.

The first practical approach is anticipatory teaching. A teacher working with a class of Mandarin-speaking learners knows that English articles will cause persistent difficulty, because Mandarin has no equivalent system of definite and indefinite reference. Rather than waiting for students to write sentences such as "Manager called meeting to discuss problem," the teacher builds article use into the syllabus from the beginning, returning to it at intervals and providing controlled practice before free writing tasks. A teacher with a class of Arabic-speaking learners, knowing that subject-auxiliary inversion in questions is not a feature of Arabic syntax, can drill question formation early and explicitly, before learners have had time to fossilize the pattern "You are coming tomorrow?" as a question form.

The second approach is error analysis during feedback. When a teacher marks a set of written assignments and notices that several students have produced the same structural error, the appropriate response is to identify the rule the learners are applying from their L1 and then explain the English rule that replaces it. This treats the learner as a logical thinker who has transferred a system that works in one language into a context where it does not apply. Marking an error as wrong without naming the underlying pattern leaves the learner with no explanation for why their reasoning failed.

The third approach is contrastive explanation: pointing out clearly and briefly how English differs from the learner's L1 on a specific grammatical or phonological point. A teacher does not need to speak the learner's language to do this. A short exchange can be enough:

T: "Yuki, you wrote 'She suggested to go.' How would you express that idea in Japanese?"
S: "The pattern is different. I think I chose 'to go' because many English verbs use it."
T: "That is a reasonable hypothesis. With 'suggest,' English uses an -ing clause here: 'She suggested going.' Add that phrase to your verb-pattern notes."

The exchange treats the learner's choice as a hypothesis without inventing a claim about Japanese grammar. Contrastive explanation is useful only when the teacher has checked the relevant L1 pattern or invites the learner to supply it; otherwise the safe move is to teach the English pattern and help the learner record it accurately.

A final point deserves emphasis. L1 background is a useful diagnostic tool, not a fixed description of any individual learner. Carlos, whose first language is Spanish, may have already mastered English article use through extensive reading, while a classmate with the same background struggles with it daily. Individual variation in exposure, aptitude, and prior instruction means that L1 predictions are starting points for a teacher's attention, not conclusions about what a particular learner can or cannot do.
