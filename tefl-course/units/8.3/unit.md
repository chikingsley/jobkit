# Teaching Writing I: From Sentence to Paragraph

## Building Blocks: From Words to Sentences

A sentence is a unit of meaning built from smaller parts, and one of the clearest ways to make that construction visible to learners is to give them something physical to move around. Word Bricks are small tiles, each carrying a word on either side, that students arrange on a flat surface to form sentences. Because the words are separate objects, a student like Carlos can pick up a noun, set it next to a verb, and immediately see whether the combination produces something grammatical. The physical act of placing and replacing tiles mirrors the cognitive work of deciding how words relate to one another.

Before any game or activity begins, the teacher demonstrates two features of the materials that students might otherwise overlook. First, each brick carries a different word on each of its two faces, so turning a brick over doubles the available vocabulary without adding more tiles. Second, the set includes special bricks that attach to base words to produce alternate forms: the -s brick, the -ed brick, the -ing brick, and the -ly brick, along with a Wild Card that a student can label with any word she needs. Fatima, for instance, might have the word "walk" but need the past tense; she reaches for the -ed brick and places it beside "walk" to produce "walked." The teacher should show this process explicitly before students begin working independently.

The difficulty of any Word Bricks task adjusts naturally to the level of the class. Beginning students work with a small set of tiles and aim to produce short phrases or simple sentences, such as "The cat sleeps." Intermediate and advanced students receive more bricks and are expected to produce longer, more structurally varied sentences. Adding one or two extra Wild Cards raises the ceiling for any group, because a student who knows the word she wants but cannot find it on any tile can write it on the Wild Card and continue building. That single adjustment means the same core activity can run in a mixed-level classroom without requiring the teacher to prepare entirely separate materials.

The connection between handling the tiles and understanding grammar is direct. When Wei tries to place two verbs side by side and the sentence sounds wrong, he is encountering subject-verb structure as a felt problem rather than an abstract rule. At that point, the teacher's role is to draw attention to the location of the difficulty, perhaps by resting a finger on the problematic brick while asking Wei to read the sentence aloud again. Students who build many sentences in this way begin to notice recurring patterns, and those patterns form the practical foundation on which more formal instruction in sentence structure can later be built.

## Controlled Writing: Sentence Patterns and Boundaries

Controlled writing is a form of scaffolded instruction in which the teacher specifies precise conditions for what students must produce, leaving content open while constraining form. A student working without any constraint may write whatever comes to mind, producing fluent but structurally careless sentences. When the teacher sets a boundary, such as requiring a sentence of five words, the student must attend to meaning and to the architecture of the sentence at the same time.

The Sentence Boundaries game makes this principle concrete. Teams receive a sequence of five requirements, each defining a different structural target: a sentence with five words, one with eight words, one containing two verbs, one containing an adjective and an adverb, and so on. Each requirement is listed on the board in order, and teams must satisfy them in sequence before moving to the next. Fatima, working with her group, might draft "She runs to the park" for the five-word round, count the words carefully, and then read the sentence aloud to check that it is grammatically sound before calling the teacher over.

The counting itself is not trivial. A team that writes "She run to the park" has met the word-count requirement but failed the grammatical one, and the teacher's response is to return the sentence to the team rather than supply the correction. The teacher records no point and asks the team to keep working, which keeps the cognitive work with the students, who must identify what is wrong and revise accordingly, rather than receiving a corrected model to copy.

The variety of patterns across the five rounds means that students encounter different structural demands in quick succession. A round requiring six words and a separate round requiring seven words asks students to consider how adding or removing a single word changes both the count and the grammar. A round specifying one adjective and one adverb pushes students to recall those categories and find words that fit them; attending to grammatical category in this way differs from producing a sentence that merely sounds acceptable. Daniel, who writes "The small dog barks loudly," has satisfied both the adjective and adverb conditions and produced a grammatically correct sentence, so his team earns the point and moves to the next pattern.

What these constraints accomplish, taken together, is a gradual narrowing of the gap between open-ended production and accurate, purposeful construction. Students are not asked to write freely and then be corrected afterward; they are asked to meet a specific target and to keep revising until they do. The teacher's role during this process is to evaluate correctness and to redirect, not to model or explain. By the time a team has worked through all five patterns, each member has made multiple decisions about word choice, word order, and grammatical form, and those decisions have been tested against a clear standard.

## Guided Writing: Sentence Frames and Word Charts

Guided writing sits between controlled practice and free production. In controlled work, students manipulate language within tight constraints; in free production, they compose without structural support. Guided writing gives students a visible framework they can follow while still making genuine choices about meaning.

A word chart organized by part of speech provides that kind of framework. The teacher prepares a large poster with vocabulary arranged in labeled columns: adjectives in one column, nouns in another, then verbs, adverbs, and prepositions or adverbs of place. In a rainforest-themed lesson, the adjective column might include words like "good," "bad," and "small," while the noun column lists "toucans," "jaguars," and "monkeys," and the verb column offers "run," "scratch," and "sleep." Students move across the columns in sequence and string together a wide variety of sentences, all of which follow the same underlying pattern.

Color-coded ink makes the grammatical structure of each sentence visible at a glance. When the teacher writes a model sentence on the board, each part of speech appears in a different color, so a student can see immediately that the blue word is a noun, the red word is a verb, and the green word is an adverb. This visual separation does the work that a grammar explanation might otherwise do, without requiring students to hold abstract rules in memory while they are also trying to compose.

Before students write anything independently, the teacher moves through a sequence of whole-class activities built around the same sentence. Students read the sentence aloud together, then sing it, then act out the individual words physically, each step brief and purposeful. When Daniel acts out "slowly" by dragging his feet across the front of the room, he is encoding the meaning of that adverb through movement rather than definition, and because all students participate at the same time, the sentence pattern is rehearsed repeatedly without any single repetition feeling mechanical.

The individual writing task follows as the natural endpoint of this preparation. Students open their journals and write three sentences of their own, selecting words from the chart's columns and following the model practiced as a group. Priya, working from the rainforest chart, might write "Small jaguars run quickly in the canopy," choosing one item from each column. The chart remains visible throughout, so she is not retrieving vocabulary from memory alone; she is making decisions about which words to combine. The teacher circulates and asks questions rather than correcting directly.

T: Priya, can you point to the adverb in your sentence?
S: "Quickly."
T: Good. Now read the whole sentence aloud and check that the verb matches your noun.

The exchange keeps the student doing the analytical work. The journal entry is a record of choices made within a structure the class has practiced together, and it gives the teacher concrete evidence of where each student is ready to move toward less supported production.

## The Topic Sentence: Anchor of the Paragraph

A topic sentence is the sentence that states the main idea of a paragraph and controls everything that follows it. Every other sentence in the paragraph exists to develop, support, or explain that central claim. When Carlos writes "Jaguars are skilled hunters," that sentence commits the paragraph to a single, manageable idea, and every sentence after it must stay within that boundary.

The connection between a topic sentence and the thesis statement of a longer piece of writing is one of the first things students need to see clearly. A topic sentence should use language similar to the thesis, so that a reader can trace the paragraph back to the essay's central argument. If the thesis states that rainforest animals have adapted to survive in a competitive environment, a topic sentence about jaguars should echo that idea of adaptation and survival rather than drifting toward a general description of the animal's appearance. The Self-Editing and Peer Editing Checklist used in station-based peer review asks students to check precisely this relationship, treating it as a concrete, verifiable feature of the paragraph rather than an abstract quality.

Once the topic sentence is in place, explaining sentences follow it by adding more information about the main idea. They do not yet offer proof; they expand the claim so that the reader understands its scope. After Carlos writes his topic sentence about jaguars, he might add that jaguars use stealth and speed together, and that their spotted coats make them difficult to see in dappled forest light. These sentences deepen the idea without yet asking the reader to accept it on the basis of specific fact.

Evidence sentences then provide the specific examples that support the main idea. Fatima, working on the same paragraph type, might write that jaguars can carry prey heavier than themselves up into trees to protect it from other predators. That detail is concrete and particular; it gives the reader something to hold onto. Without it, the paragraph remains a series of general claims that the reader has no reason to accept.

Interpreting sentences complete the cycle by telling the reader why the evidence matters. They explain the connection between the specific example and the topic sentence, a step that student writers frequently omit because it seems obvious to them. Fatima might add that this behavior shows how jaguars have developed physical and instinctive strategies that give them an advantage over competing animals, which ties the evidence directly back to the idea of skilled hunting. These four sentence types, taken together, form the practical architecture of a basic paragraph: the topic sentence anchors the idea, explaining sentences develop it, evidence sentences ground it in fact, and interpreting sentences close the logical loop for the reader.

## Basic Paragraph Organization: From Opening to Closing

A paragraph does not end with its last piece of evidence or interpretation. The closing sentence, as the station checklist defines it, restates the main point of the paragraph in fresh language. It introduces no new information; it signals to the reader that the paragraph's single idea has been fully addressed. If Fatima's paragraph argues that jaguars are skilled hunters, her closing sentence might read: "Every aspect of the jaguar's physical design serves its role as one of the most effective predators in the rainforest." The idea is familiar, the phrasing is new, and the paragraph feels finished rather than stopped mid-thought.

Once students write more than one paragraph, they face the additional problem of connecting those paragraphs to each other. A bridge is a clause or phrase that reviews the idea just completed in the previous paragraph and previews the idea about to be developed in the next one. Bridges appear at the boundary between paragraphs, either at the end of one or the opening of the next. Carlos, writing a two-paragraph composition about animal adaptation, might begin his second paragraph with a sentence such as: "While the jaguar relies on physical strength, the toucan depends on an entirely different kind of advantage." The first clause glances back; the second opens the new direction. Without that connection, the two paragraphs sit side by side with no logical relationship between them.

The station checklist approach provides a practical way to teach this structure at any level. For a beginning class, the teacher reduces the task to a single paragraph or even two or three sentences, asking students to identify only the topic sentence and the closing sentence. A student at this level might highlight one sentence in green as the opening idea and one in yellow as the restatement. The physical act of highlighting makes the structure visible in a way that abstract explanation does not.

As students advance, the teacher adds stations for explaining sentences, evidence, interpretation, and eventually bridges. A more experienced student like Fatima can move through all ten stations, checking each structural element in turn and initialing the checklist after a peer has reviewed the same section. The checklist scales because the underlying framework stays constant; only the complexity of the writing changes. Beginning students learn that a paragraph has a shape, and advanced students learn to manage that shape across multiple paragraphs simultaneously.

## Peer Feedback as a Writing Scaffold

When students write in isolation, errors and gaps tend to go unnoticed until the teacher returns a marked draft. The station-based peer feedback activity described in the module addresses this by keeping writing active between drafts rather than treating revision as a single corrective event. Each station in the classroom focuses on one component of the essay, and students move through the stations independently, highlighting the relevant part of their own writing at each stop. The process is structured enough to give students clear direction, yet open enough that they choose which classmate to work with at each station.

The mechanics of the activity require students to do three things at every station: highlight the targeted section of their essay, reach agreement with a classmate about whether that section meets the stated criteria, and initial the checklist before moving on. Carlos, for example, arrives at Station Two and highlights his topic sentences. He and a peer read them against the criterion asking whether the language echoes the thesis statement. Only when both students agree does Carlos initial the checklist and move to the next station. Because students are encouraged to work with several different peers across the ten stations, no single classmate's judgment carries all the weight, and Carlos receives a range of responses rather than one person's reading.

One effect of the activity deserves particular notice: its mutually reinforcing quality. When a student like Priya notices that a classmate's essay is missing a closing sentence at Station Six, she tends to return to her own essay and check the same element. Evaluating someone else's writing sharpens attention to the writer's own work, and that sharpening is a built-in feature of the design rather than a side effect. The process is iterative rather than linear precisely because each act of peer evaluation feeds back into the student's own revision.

The practical difficulties should not be understated. The teacher has less control over what happens once students are moving independently among stations, and the preparation and physical setup of ten stations takes considerable time. These are real costs, and a teacher planning this activity for the first time should budget accordingly. The benefits are equally concrete: students feel more in control of their own writing, and they stop treating the teacher as the sole authority on whether a sentence or paragraph works.

T: "Before you initial the checklist, make sure you and your partner actually agree. If you're not sure, that's a reason to talk, not a reason to skip it."
S: "We disagreed about whether my topic sentence matched the thesis. I changed it."
T: "Good. That's exactly what the station is for."

The teacher does not step back entirely. She continues to provide feedback on drafts alongside the student feedback, offering summative feedback on later drafts and the final version. Peer response shapes the revision process; teacher response remains part of it.
