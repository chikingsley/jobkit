# Teaching Reading

## What We Mean by Teaching Reading

Reading is one of the four primary language skills, sitting alongside listening as a receptive skill and alongside speaking and writing as the productive pair. A receptive skill is one through which a learner receives and processes language rather than producing it. This distinction comes directly from the vocabulary framework used in integrated-skills teaching, where listening and reading are grouped together because both involve taking in meaning rather than generating it.

Receptive does not mean passive. The source material for this unit shows students doing active work during a reading activity: one student fills out a worksheet about a peer's written piece while that peer reads it aloud, and the teacher suggests that listeners might also keep a running list of unfamiliar vocabulary or write down one or two questions to put to the reader afterward. A student named, say, Amara who sits with her classmate during the reading is processing, selecting, and recording throughout. That mental and physical activity is the substance of engaged reading work.

A reading lesson is therefore developing more than one thing at once. The primary skill of reading sits at the centre, but sub-skills such as vocabulary recognition, spelling awareness, and the ability to infer meaning from context all feed into how successfully a learner can handle a text. When a teacher asks a student like Tomás to underline words he does not recognise while reading a short article, the lesson is working on both levels at the same time, building the sub-skill base that makes the primary skill function.

The broader goal of language teaching, as the source material makes clear, is for skills to be used together for communication rather than practised in isolation. A student who reads well but cannot connect that reading to speaking, listening, or writing is only partly equipped. This unit concentrates on the reading thread, examining what happens in the classroom when reading is the central focus, while keeping in view the connections to the other skills that give reading its communicative purpose.

## Top-Down and Bottom-Up Processing in Plain Terms

Two distinct processes are at work whenever a reader engages with a text, and understanding both helps a teacher make better decisions about classroom tasks. Bottom-up processing begins at the smallest units of language: individual letters, spelling patterns, vocabulary items, and the sub-skills that the source material lists alongside the primary skills of reading, writing, listening, and speaking. A reader working primarily from the bottom up decodes each piece of the text in sequence, building toward meaning one word or phrase at a time. This process is close to what the source describes as rote memorisation, working through language by repetition without necessarily arriving at deep understanding.

Top-down processing works in the opposite direction. The reader brings prior knowledge, personal interest, and a sense of purpose to the text, using those resources to construct meaning from the whole before attending closely to individual parts. The source illustrates this in the free-write activity, where students chose their own pictures from magazines and wrote about topics that mattered to them personally. One student selected bears because, as the teacher recalled, his previous writing had also been about bears. He arrived at the reading task already carrying knowledge about the subject, and that background shaped what he noticed, what he understood, and how deeply he engaged with the content.

The magazine picture activity shows top-down processing in a second way. Because each student chose a topic of personal interest, the listener in the pair encountered a text with a real communicative purpose: finding out what the other person had written about. The source contrasts this kind of meaningful engagement with the narrower goal of decoding alone, noting that communicative competence requires using language skills together in social interaction rather than treating each skill as a separate, self-contained exercise. A student listening to a classmate read aloud about pirates is constructing a picture of events from her existing knowledge of ships, adventure stories, and narrative structure, not performing a mechanical word-recognition task.

In practice, most reading involves both processes running at the same time, and a teacher's task is to support each one appropriately. When a student like the one in the source activity stumbles over an unfamiliar word in a passage about a topic she knows well, her top-down knowledge of the subject can help her infer the meaning from context. Conversely, when a reader has strong decoding skills but no background knowledge of a topic, bottom-up accuracy alone may not be enough to produce genuine comprehension. The free-write activity, modest as it appears, addresses both sides of this balance: students practise the sub-skills of spelling and vocabulary in writing, then activate prior knowledge and personal interest when reading aloud to an audience.

## Choosing Texts That Work

A text works in the classroom when learners have a genuine reason to read it. The clearest starting criterion for selection is whether the material qualifies as authentic, meaning it consists of tasks, activities, and materials that native speakers use in real life for actual communicative needs. A magazine article about wildlife, an e-mail written to a friend abroad, a poem composed for a school project: each of these circulates outside the classroom for real purposes, and that origin gives it a communicative weight that a passage written solely to practise the past tense does not carry.

The range of text types that appear across the source activities is wider than many teachers initially consider. In the free-write segment, students cut pictures from magazines and wrote descriptions or stories to accompany them, producing texts that were then read aloud to a peer. In the iEARN projects, learners read library books and magazines during jigsaw research sessions, composed written summaries with citations, exchanged e-mails with partner classes in other countries, built web pages, wrote poems, and kept journals recording a teddy bear's travels through Thailand. Each of these formats places different demands on the reader, and a course that draws on several of them gives students practice with the variety of texts they will encounter outside the classroom.

Authentic materials do not require an authentic setting to be useful. The source notes explicitly that they can be used in simulated classroom situations; a teacher can bring in a real travel blog or a genuine e-mail exchange and frame a reading task around it without needing to replicate the original context. Consider a student named Priya, reading an e-mail that a Thai peer originally wrote for an American class. She is engaging with language produced for a real communicative purpose even though she is sitting in a classroom in a different country entirely. The setting is artificial; the language is not.

Student-chosen topics add a further dimension to text selection. When learners select their own pictures from a pile of magazines and write about what interests them, they arrive at the reading stage with a personal investment in the content. The source describes one student writing about bears because that subject had already appeared in his previous work, and the teacher noticed the connection. That kind of continuity, where a learner returns to a topic that matters to him, increases the likelihood that he will read carefully and listen attentively when a classmate reads aloud.

Text choice also needs to account for the range of learners in the room. The iEARN classes described in the source included students of mixed ages, grade levels, and language abilities, with more experienced students mentoring those with less experience. A single text pitched at one narrow proficiency band excludes part of the group. Magazine pictures with short captions can serve a beginning reader like Carlos while a longer library article on the same theme challenges an advanced reader like Mei sitting beside him; both are working with material on a shared topic, which makes subsequent discussion possible.

Internet access expands the range of available authentic texts considerably, but its absence does not prevent this kind of teaching. The source makes the practical point that class-to-class exchange projects have succeeded through postal mail for many years, and that library books, printed magazines, and other text-based resources serve the same function as digital materials. A teacher working without reliable internet can build an equally varied reading programme from physical library collections. The governing principle is that texts should be chosen because they carry genuine communicative purpose, not merely because they happen to be on hand.

## Staging a Reading Lesson: Pre-, While-, and Post-Reading

A reading lesson gains considerably when it is divided into three distinct stages, each with its own purpose. The pre-reading stage prepares learners to meet the text; the while-reading stage gives them a structured way to engage with it; and the post-reading stage extends what they have understood into further language use. Treating these stages as separate events, rather than moving directly from handing out a text to asking comprehension questions, allows learners to build meaning progressively rather than encountering the text cold.

Pre-reading work centres on activating what learners already know about a topic before they read a single sentence of the text itself. One reliable technique, drawn from the kind of warm-up used before integrated-skills activities, is to ask learners to recall a personal experience connected to the subject matter. If the class is about to read a short article on community service, a teacher might say: T: "Think of a time you did something to help someone outside your family. What did you do?" A student, Maria, might respond: S: "I helped clean the park near my school." That brief exchange surfaces vocabulary, sets expectations, and gives Maria a personal stake in what she is about to read. Brainstorming, defined as suggesting many ideas quickly without giving each one extended thought, usually in groups and in preparation for a future activity, works alongside this recall technique. The teacher can ask the class to call out everything they associate with the topic while writing responses on the board, giving all learners a shared bank of words and ideas before the text is opened.

The while-reading stage replaces passive reading with structured tasks that keep learners actively processing the text as they move through it. Three techniques serve this purpose well. The first is a short worksheet that directs a student like Ahmed toward specific information, giving him a concrete target rather than an open-ended instruction to read. The second is keeping a running list of unfamiliar vocabulary encountered during the reading; this gives learners a concrete record of new language rather than allowing unknown words to pass unnoticed. The third is writing one or two questions to ask about the content afterward. When a student, Priya, knows she will need to pose a question to a classmate at the end, she reads with a different quality of attention, looking for what is interesting or unclear rather than decoding words as an end in itself. These tasks make the reading an active event.

Post-reading work transforms the text from a reading exercise into a springboard for further language production. The simplest move is to use the text as a stimulus for conversation: the teacher asks pairs or small groups to discuss what they found surprising, familiar, or worth questioning. A student who has written two questions during the while-reading stage can now put those questions to the class, shifting the dynamic toward learner-generated discussion rather than a teacher-led comprehension check, and doing so with a focus and economy that a conventional question-and-answer sequence rarely achieves. For learners who are ready for extended work, the text can go home as the basis for a longer writing assignment, perhaps a personal response or a short summary in the learner's own words. A single text, handled this way, generates speaking, listening, and writing activity, treating reading as one part of a connected communicative event rather than a skill practised in isolation.

## Reading Tasks That Check Comprehension Without Killing Interest

Student choice is one of the most direct ways to keep comprehension work from feeling like an examination. In the free-write activity described in the source material, learners browsed magazines, selected a picture that interested them personally, and then wrote either a description or a creative story based on that image. A student named Marco, drawn to a photograph of a shipwreck, produced a short piece of fiction about pirates; another student, Yuki, chose a picture of a bear and wrote a factual paragraph. Because each student had selected a subject that genuinely engaged them, the subsequent reading aloud was purposeful rather than mechanical.

Comprehension in that activity was checked through a response worksheet that the listening partner completed while the other student read. Yuki, for instance, recorded her understanding of the main events on paper as Marco read his pirate story aloud. The teacher could then ask a few follow-up questions, such as asking Yuki to explain what happened to the ship's crew, and the answers revealed how well she had followed the text. This approach treats comprehension as a social act between two people, not a private transaction between a learner and a marking scheme.

The jigsaw technique, used in the iEARN Good Deeds project described in the source, extends this principle to a whole class. Students divided into two groups, each reading different library materials on the same broad topic, and then came together to combine what they had found. A student named Priya, who had read about community volunteering, could explain her material to a partner named Daniel, who had read about school-based good deeds; each depended on the other to complete the full picture. The task structure makes comprehension necessary for communication, because a student who has not understood the text cannot contribute meaningfully when the groups reconvene.

Graphic organizers, defined in the source as visual maps of information such as graphs, semantic webs, timelines, and story maps, give learners a structured way to process a text before they are asked to discuss or write about it. After reading their library materials, the Good Deeds students could have mapped the sequence of events on a timeline or arranged key ideas in a semantic web before writing their summaries. A learner who struggles to articulate what a text means in speech often finds that filling in a story map first gives them the language they need to talk about it.

Displaying finished student writing on classroom walls or doors, as the source recommends, turns the product of a reading and writing sequence into something with a genuine audience. When Marco's pirate story is posted on the door, other students read it as they enter the room. They read it not because a teacher has assigned it but because it is there and it is interesting. That small shift means comprehension becomes a real communicative act, the kind the source describes as authentic use of language, and the motivation to read carefully comes from curiosity rather than obligation.

## Adapting Reading Activities for Different Class Sizes and Contexts

The free-write and read-aloud activity described in the source works with two students, but the same principles scale to a full class with modest adjustments. Magazines can be placed in several locations around the room, preventing students from crowding a single table while choosing their pictures. Once students have written, they can rehearse their reading in pairs before presenting to a larger group, giving quieter students like Ana a chance to hear their own voice and adjust their pace before facing the whole class. Listeners in each group can be asked to prepare one question for the reader, a technique the source calls active listening, which keeps every student engaged even when someone else is speaking.

The chain story is a related activity that the source describes as working well with all ages and as capable of producing genuinely enjoyable results. Groups of three or four students co-author a single story by passing it around in a circle, with each writer contributing one section. In a group of four, every student writes an introduction, a middle section, and a conclusion at different points in the rotation, so no one is only a beginner or only an ending writer. When Kenji reads back the full story his group has assembled, the reading task carries real interest because no single student knows what the others have written.

Giving a series of reading texts a unifying theme across several lessons is what the source defines as content-based instruction: structuring curriculum or lessons around central themes or topics. A teacher running a two-week unit on the natural world can sequence a magazine picture activity, a chain story, and a read-aloud session, with each one building on shared vocabulary and ideas from the previous lesson. Students arrive at each lesson with some prior knowledge of the topic, which reduces the cognitive load of decoding new text and allows attention to shift toward meaning.

None of these activities depends on internet access or specialist equipment. The source notes explicitly that class-to-class exchange projects have run successfully by post for many years, and that organisations such as Global SchoolNet can help teachers set up print-based exchanges when computers are unavailable. A class in a school with limited technology can still send a handwritten story to a partner class in another city, receive one in return, and read it with the same communicative purpose that an emailed version would carry. The underlying principle, that reading gains meaning when it serves a real communicative need, holds regardless of the medium through which the text arrives.
