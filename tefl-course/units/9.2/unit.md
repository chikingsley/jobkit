# Learner Feedback in Practice

## Three Domains of Feedback: Linguistic, Communicative, and Affective

Feedback in the language classroom operates across three distinct domains, each serving a different purpose in the learner's development. Linguistic feedback addresses the accuracy of the language itself, covering areas such as grammar, vocabulary, and pronunciation. Communicative feedback focuses on the task or the content of the message, asking whether the learner has conveyed meaning successfully. Affective feedback attends to the learner's emotional relationship with the language, encouraging attempts to communicate and building the confidence to take risks.

Module 5 states plainly that "incorrectness is not the only standard teachers can use to assess learning," and this principle explains why all three domains belong in a balanced feedback routine. A teacher who responds only to linguistic error may leave a learner like Priya feeling that her carefully constructed message about a weekend trip was judged a failure, when she communicated the content with clarity and showed genuine willingness to speak. Attending to the communicative and affective dimensions alongside the linguistic one gives a fuller and more accurate picture of what the learner can do.

Consider a short classroom exchange. Daniel says, "Yesterday I go to the market and buy many vegetable." The teacher might respond:

T: Yesterday you go to the market?
S: I went. I went to the market and I bought many vegetables.
T: Good, you caught that yourself. What did you find there?
S: Tomatoes and onions.

In this sequence the teacher addresses the linguistic error through a prompt, repeating the error with rising intonation so that Daniel produces the repair himself, confirms the communicative content by engaging with the message, and signals approval of Daniel's self-repair as an affective response. No single exchange needs to cover all three domains explicitly. A teacher who holds all three in mind, however, can make deliberate choices about where to direct attention at any given moment, rather than defaulting to error correction alone.

The module's primary concern is formative feedback, defined as continuous feedback given at all stages of the learning period to show learners what they have achieved and what still needs work. Summative feedback, by contrast, arrives at the end of a unit or project and typically takes the form of a grade or a final report. Formative feedback is the more immediate tool for shaping learning as it happens, and it is the kind most directly connected to the three domains described above. Research confirms that corrective feedback of this kind supports acquisition (Lyster and Saito, 2010; Li, 2010), and the practical techniques explored in the sections that follow are grounded in that understanding.

## General Dos and Don'ts: Installing a Feedback Mindset

Before a teacher can respond usefully to a student's error, she needs to understand what produced it. The module draws a clear distinction here: an error, in the technical sense, reflects incomplete or incorrect learning, while a mistake results from inattention or carelessness at a moment when the student already knows the correct form. These two causes call for different responses. A student who writes "he go to school yesterday" because she has not yet acquired the past tense needs something different from a student who writes the same thing because she was rushing.

Once the cause is clearer, the teacher must also identify the type of error. A grammatical slip, a wrong word choice, and a pragmatically inappropriate utterance each point to a different gap in the learner's knowledge. Consider Daniel, who tells a shop assistant, "Give me the bread," when the context calls for "Could I have the bread, please?" The form is grammatically correct, but the pragmatic register is wrong. Treating that as a grammar problem would send Daniel in the wrong direction entirely.

After diagnosing both cause and type, the teacher must match the feedback to the context of the task. A fluency activity, such as a role-play or a storytelling exercise, is not the right moment to stop the class and address a minor pronunciation detail. The module is explicit on this point: teachers should avoid trying to correct every error and must judge whether an error prevents understanding or whether, for the current task, keeping communication moving matters more. A teacher who interrupts a student mid-story to correct a verb ending may gain a corrected verb and lose the student's confidence.

Over-correction is one of the most consistent risks in feedback practice. Too much negative feedback, particularly the kind that singles students out or signals failure, can reduce motivation and make learners reluctant to attempt new language at all. The recommended practice is for teachers to use techniques that allow learners to self-correct, individually or with peers, rather than supplying corrections directly whenever possible. Research supports the value of this approach: prompts that push students toward self-correction produce stronger and more durable learning outcomes than recasts, in which the teacher restates the correct form without requiring the learner to do so (Lyster and Saito, 2010).

The classroom atmosphere a teacher builds around error matters as much as any individual correction technique. One teacher described in the module told her students directly and explicitly that making mistakes was a natural part of learning. That single statement, consistently backed up by her behavior, created an atmosphere of acceptance in which students felt safe enough to take risks with the language. The result was more practice and better practice. The statement alone, however, is not sufficient; if a teacher says mistakes are welcome and then responds to every error with visible correction, students will trust the behavior over the words. A teacher such as Ms. Reyes might open a speaking activity by saying to her class, "Try it. If it comes out wrong, that's useful information for all of us," and then respond to a student's hesitant attempt with:

T: Good, keep going. What happened next?
S: He, uh, he go to the market.
T: He went, yes. And then?

That brief, forward-moving response acknowledges the error, supplies the correct form without drama, and returns attention immediately to the student's message. Over time, that pattern teaches students that errors are workable, not shameful.

## On-the-Spot and Delayed Oral Feedback: Practical Routines

When a student speaks, the teacher faces a choice about when to respond to an error, and that timing decision shapes both the quality of the correction and the student's willingness to keep talking. On-the-spot feedback, sometimes called immediate feedback, is a response given at the moment the error occurs. Delayed feedback is held back until after the communicative task is finished, then addressed to the individual or the whole class. Each option serves different purposes, and skilled teachers learn to choose between them based on what the activity demands.

The story-retelling lesson described in the Video Segment 2 answer key illustrates how on-the-spot feedback can stay focused on communication rather than form. In that lesson, two students stood at the front of the class and acted out a story while their classmates supplied sentences from the narrative. The teacher's role was to keep the retelling moving: when a student faltered, she used a question-word cue such as "When?" or "Where?" to prompt the next idea, and when a student produced a sentence successfully, she clapped or said "Very good" or "Uh huh." When a speaker stalled mid-sentence, she supplied the next phrase so the story could continue. At no point did she stop the activity to address a pronunciation slip or a grammar error. Her feedback was calibrated entirely to the communicative goal of retelling the story with enough clarity that the actors could understand and respond, and the actors' physical responses served as natural confirmation: if they acted correctly, the speaker knew the message had landed.

A brief exchange from that kind of lesson might look like this:

T: He bought the fish, and then what happened?
S: He, uh — he buyed the fish.
T: Good. He bought the fish. What did he do after that?

The teacher restates the correct past-tense form without interrupting the flow, and the student's attention stays on the story rather than on the grammar point. It is worth naming what this move is: a recast, not a prompt. Lyster and Saito (2010) found in a meta-analysis of fifteen classroom studies that prompts pushing students toward self-correction produced stronger acquisition outcomes than recasts, in which the teacher restates the correct form, though both types of oral corrective feedback showed significant and durable effects, and Li (2010) corroborates in a separate meta-analysis of thirty-three studies that corrective feedback of this kind supports acquisition. The teacher in the retelling lesson is not ignoring that evidence; she is deliberately trading some of the prompt's acquisition advantage for momentum, because the goal of this task is a complete, fluent retelling in front of an audience. In a moment where flow mattered less, a brief prompt, such as 'he buyed the fish?' repeated with rising intonation, would draw the correct form from the student himself, keeping the cognitive effort of retrieval with the learner rather than the instructor.

Delayed feedback becomes the appropriate choice when stopping to correct would break the communicative purpose of the task entirely, or when several students are making the same error and addressing it once for the group is more efficient than pausing for each speaker individually. The answer key for Video Segment 2 suggests a practical routine: the teacher takes brief notes during the activity, then after it concludes writes the patterned errors on the board and works through them with the whole class. Suppose Mr. Osei notices during a group discussion that four students have used the simple present when the past was required. Rather than correcting each one mid-sentence, he jots the forms down, lets the discussion run its course, and then opens a five-minute slot at the end: "I heard something a few times today. Look at these sentences with me." Students see the error in context, discuss what went wrong, and produce the corrected form themselves. The answer key also suggests that audio or video recordings of the activity can be replayed so students locate one or two recurring errors and attempt to self-correct before the teacher confirms the right form.

Delayed feedback need not be confined to the classroom period in which the error occurred. The answer key for Video Segment 1 describes a teacher who wanted more student participation but could not generate it through in-class techniques alone. She raised the issue during parent conferences, and after the parents began encouraging their children to speak up, participation increased. That example is a reminder that the feedback loop can extend well beyond the lesson itself, and that the teacher's judgment about who receives the feedback, and when, is as consequential as the content of the correction.

## Feedback on Written Work: Techniques and Formats

Written production introduces a different set of demands on the teacher's feedback decisions, because the physical record of a student's work allows for more deliberate and varied responses than spoken language typically permits. The materials for Video Segment 3 organize writing activities along a spectrum from accuracy-focused tasks to those where fluency is the primary concern, and the feedback technique recommended for each task follows directly from that purpose.

At the accuracy end of the spectrum, tasks such as writing the alphabet or copying a text call for a concrete external standard against which the student's work can be measured. The recommended approach is to provide a model and then have a partner compare the finished work against it. For copying tasks, the comparison becomes slightly more active: a partner reads the finished copy aloud while the other student follows the original model. Fatima, for instance, might read her copied paragraph sentence by sentence while her partner tracks the source text, noting any word that was dropped or altered. The spoken comparison slows the checking process enough that small discrepancies become visible.

Tasks in the middle of the spectrum, such as writing a letter, combine accuracy and fluency concerns. One recommended sequence is to provide a correct letter format at the outset and then send the finished letter to another student for a genuine response. When Carlos receives a letter from a classmate and writes back, the exchange itself becomes the feedback mechanism: if the original letter was unclear, the reply will reflect that confusion. The teacher's role shifts from corrector to facilitator of a real communicative transaction.

At the fluency end, tasks such as writing a story heard or read, or synthesizing information for a report, require a different approach. The source recommends supplying guidelines that tell students in advance which points will be evaluated, and then confining correction to those stated points. Yuki, writing a summary of a short story she has read, should know before she begins whether the teacher will focus on paragraph organization, verb tense consistency, or both. Correction confined to the stated criteria prevents the kind of exhaustive marking that can make a returned page look more red ink than text, and that can cause a learner to feel that improvement is impossible.

This principle of focused feedback connects directly to the broader guideline stated in the module's introduction: feedback should fit both the kind of error and the context in which it occurred. A checklist of errors for self-checking a report extends that principle further, because it transfers part of the evaluative responsibility to the student. When Fatima works through a checklist before submitting her report, she is practicing the same diagnostic thinking the teacher would otherwise perform alone. This self-checking step is not a shortcut for the teacher but a deliberate strategy for building learner independence, identified in the module as a goal of effective feedback practice.

## Peer and Self-Assessment: Building Independent Learners

One structured approach to peer feedback appears in the module's account of a teacher who grouped students according to learning style. Each group designed a set of activities, passed them to another group to complete, then collected the finished work and checked it themselves. The checking step is the feedback event. Students in the originating group had to decide what a correct response looked like, compare it against what their peers had written, and form a judgment. That process requires the same analytical attention that a teacher brings to error correction, but it distributes that attention across the class.

The module identifies two simultaneous effects of this arrangement. Peer feedback can reduce the anxiety that learners feel about making mistakes, because the evaluative relationship is between equals rather than between a student and an authority figure. At the same time, the act of checking someone else's work causes students to focus and work out for themselves what is correct. Wei, who might freeze when a teacher points to his error on the board, may engage quite differently when he is the one deciding whether his classmate's sentence is right. The two effects reinforce each other: lower anxiety creates the conditions in which genuine cognitive engagement becomes possible.

The module adds a caveat that carries real practical weight. The teacher must monitor peer-checking work closely to ensure that the feedback students give each other is both correct and helpful. If Wei's group marks a grammatically acceptable sentence as wrong, the peer process has introduced a new error into the learning environment rather than resolved one. Close monitoring is not a sign that the teacher distrusts the procedure; it is the condition that makes the procedure reliable. Research supports the value of corrective feedback in general, with Lyster and Saito (2010) finding significant and durable effects across fifteen classroom studies, and Li (2010) corroborating those findings across thirty-three studies, so the quality of the feedback students receive, whether from a teacher or a peer, matters for acquisition.

Peer correction connects directly to a goal the module states in its action plan: helping students learn to self-correct in order to become more independent learners. Checking a peer's work is a rehearsal for checking one's own. A student who has spent several lessons deciding whether another person's verb agreement is correct begins to apply that same scrutiny to her own writing. The module supports several habits that extend this process. Students can work through error checklists before submitting written work, review audio or video recordings of their own spoken production, and look for patterned errors rather than isolated slips. A student who listens back to a recorded role-play and notices that she consistently drops the past tense marker in fast speech has identified a pattern; that identification is the first step toward correction. Each of these strategies shifts the responsibility for noticing errors gradually from the teacher toward the learner, which is the direction the module consistently points.

## Praise That Names the Behavior and Keeping Feedback Encouraging

Affective feedback, as this module defines it, is feedback whose explicit purpose is to build confidence rather than to evaluate accuracy. It sits alongside feedback that rewards correct production and feedback that addresses linguistic form, and all three types are needed for effective learning. A teacher who uses only corrective feedback, however carefully delivered, leaves the affective dimension unattended, and students in that classroom may become reluctant to attempt language they are not already sure of.

The distinction between vague praise and praise that names the behavior is practical and consequential. When a teacher says "Good" after a student completes a task, the student receives a positive signal but no information. When the same teacher says "Good, you kept going even when you lost the word," or repeats the student's response clearly to the whole class, the praise does two things at once: it confirms that the attempt was successful and identifies what the student did that made it so. The Video Segment 2 answer key records a range of such techniques used by one teacher in a story-retelling activity, including clapping for student performance, saying "Very good" and "Keep going," thanking students, and repeating their responses so the whole class could hear them clearly. None of these interrupted the communicative task; all of them told students that their effort was recognized and that continuing was worthwhile.

Take Carlos, who hesitates mid-sentence during a retelling activity. A teacher who waits, then says "Keep going, Carlos" and later repeats his completed sentence to the class has given him two pieces of affective information: that pausing is acceptable and that his finished utterance was worth sharing. Contrast that with a teacher who corrects the grammar of the hesitant sentence, thereby shifting focus from fluency to accuracy at a moment when fluency was the point of the task. The choice is small in execution and significant in effect.

The source states a principle that connects affective feedback to everything else a teacher does: the teacher's behavior must be consistent with any stated belief about mistakes. If a teacher tells students that making mistakes is a natural part of learning but then responds to errors with frequent direct correction, students will not believe the original statement. Classroom behavior carries more weight than declaration. A teacher who announces on the first day that errors are welcome but who visibly winces at mispronunciation sends two contradictory messages, and students will act on the one they observe rather than the one they heard.

The overarching guideline is to encourage student learning through positive feedback and to help students be successful and confident. That guideline is not a separate technique to be added at the end of a lesson; it is the thread that holds every practical routine in this unit together. Decisions about when to correct, whether to delay feedback, how to structure peer review, and how to design self-correction checklists all serve the same end: a classroom in which students see attempting the language as safe, and in which the teacher's responses, verbal and non-verbal alike, consistently confirm that belief.
