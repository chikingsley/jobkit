# Prepositions

## What Prepositions Do: A Teacher's Overview

A preposition is a word that links a noun or pronoun to another element in the sentence, showing the relationship between them. That relationship may involve time, place, movement, manner, cause, or several other meanings. In the sentence "The book is on the table," the word *on* connects *book* to *table* and tells the reader where the book is located. Without the preposition, the grammatical and semantic connection between the two nouns would be lost.

English prepositions form a closed lexical class, meaning the set of members is fixed and new prepositions are almost never added to the language. This contrasts with nouns or verbs, which speakers coin freely. The practical consequence for learners is that the list of prepositions to be learned is finite, though the range of meanings each one carries can be wide. A single word such as *on* appears in "the meeting is on Monday" (time), "the cup is on the shelf" (place), and "she jumped on the bus" (movement), and each use must be understood on its own terms.

This unit organizes prepositions into three main categories: time, place, and movement. Time prepositions indicate when something happens, as in "Daniel arrived at noon" or "the course runs from September to December." Place prepositions locate a person or thing in space, as in "Fatima sat beside the window." Movement prepositions describe direction or path, as in "the children ran across the field." Each category receives its own section, with explanations of the most common items and the distinctions learners regularly find difficult.

A fourth area, dependent prepositions, is also covered in this unit. These are prepositions that are required by a particular adjective, noun, or verb and cannot be predicted from general rules. Carlos, for instance, might say "I am interested about history" by analogy with "I am curious about history," not knowing that *interested* takes *in*. The exchange below illustrates how this confusion surfaces in class.

T: Carlos, can you tell me about your hobbies?
S: Yes, I am very interested about history and old buildings.
T: Good topic. We say "interested in" — the preposition is *in* with that adjective.
S: Ah, interested in. Is it always *in*?
T: Not always. That is exactly what we will look at in the dependent prepositions section.

Keeping these four areas distinct gives both teacher and learner a clear map of the ground to be covered.

## Prepositions of Time: At, On, In and Beyond

Time prepositions in English sort themselves by a single underlying principle: the narrower the time reference, the more specific the preposition. "At" marks a precise point on the clock or a fixed moment treated as a point. A student writing "The meeting starts at 9:15" or "She called at midnight" is using "at" correctly because both references identify an exact instant rather than a stretch of time. Fixed expressions such as "at noon," "at dawn," and "at night" follow the same logic, even when no clock time is stated. The weekend is a special case worth flagging for learners: American English treats it as a bounded unit and says "on the weekend," while British English says "at the weekend," and both appear in authentic materials.

"On" moves one step outward to cover days and dates, units that are wider than a moment but still bounded and specific. A learner who writes "on Friday," "on 14 June," or "on New Year's Day" is treating each as a named, discrete unit of the calendar. The preposition does not change when a day carries an adjective: "on a cold Tuesday morning" still uses "on" because the anchor is the named day.

"In" covers the broadest time containers: months, years, decades, centuries, and the general parts of the day. Sentences such as "She was born in 1998," "The factory closed in the 1980s," and "He prefers to study in the morning" all use "in" because the time reference is an extended period rather than a point or a named day. The phrase "in the morning" often surprises learners who have just learned "at dawn," but the distinction holds: "dawn" is a point, while "the morning" is a several-hour span.

The specificity framework is a reliable starting point, yet it does not account for every time preposition in English. "During" indicates that something happens within the boundaries of a named period, with the period itself as the focus: "The power went out during the concert." "For" measures a duration from a quantity of time: "Fatima has lived in Lyon for three years." "Since" anchors a period to its starting point and runs to the present or to another reference moment: "She has worked here since 2018." "By" sets a deadline, meaning no later than a specified time: "Please submit the form by Thursday." "Until" marks the endpoint of a continuing situation: "The library is open until nine o'clock." "Within" specifies that something happens inside a time boundary without reaching its outer edge: "You will receive a reply within two working days."

Learner errors cluster around the core trio in predictable ways. Students whose first language uses a single general preposition for time often produce "in Monday" or "in Friday," applying the broad container logic of "in" to a named day. Others say "at the morning" by analogy with "at noon," not recognizing that "the morning" is a span rather than a point. The classroom exchange below shows how quickly the framework can redirect a learner.

T: Yuki, when do you usually exercise?
S: I exercise at the morning, before work.
T: Nearly right. "The morning" is a long period, so we use "in." At the morning is a common slip.
S: In the morning. So "at" is only for exact times?
T: For clock times and fixed points, yes. Days take "on," longer periods take "in."

The framework helps because it gives learners a question to ask themselves before choosing: am I naming a point, a day, or a longer period? That question resolves the majority of cases. It does not resolve "during," "for," "since," "by," "until," and "within," because those prepositions express relationships such as duration, deadlines, and starting points rather than the size of the time container. Each of those six requires its own focused practice, and treating them as a separate group prevents learners from forcing them into the at-on-in pattern where they do not belong.

## Prepositions of Place: Locating People and Things

The same three prepositions that organize time also organize place, but the underlying logic shifts from size of a time period to scale of a physical space. "At" marks a point or address, a location treated as a single spot on a map rather than a space you move around inside. "On" marks a surface or a line, such as a road, a floor, or a shelf. "In" marks an enclosed or bounded space, somewhere with edges that contain the person or thing. Fatima is at the bus stop, on Platform 3, or in the waiting room, and each choice reflects a different way of conceptualizing the same general area.

The distinction between "at" and "in" becomes particularly useful when the location is a building. Both prepositions can appear with the same noun, but they carry different meanings. "At the hospital" tells you where someone is in a general sense, the way you might give directions or explain why you cannot meet for lunch. "In the hospital" tells you the person is physically inside it, and in everyday English it often implies they are a patient or are otherwise enclosed within the building. A teacher might draw this out in class with a short exchange:

T: Where is Carlos's father?
S: He's at the hospital.
T: Is he a doctor there, or is he sick?
S: He's sick.
T: Then we say he's in the hospital. He's inside, as a patient.

That brief correction anchors the contrast more firmly than a rule stated in isolation.

Beyond "at," "on," and "in," a second set of prepositions describes the spatial relationship between one thing and another. "Above" and "below" describe vertical position without implying contact: the lamp hangs above the table, the pipes run below the floor. "On" already covers contact with a surface, so "above" is reserved for cases where the two objects do not touch. "Underneath" is close in meaning to "below" but tends to suggest that one thing is directly under another and often covered or hidden by it: the cat is underneath the bed, not merely below it.

"Beside," "next to," and "opposite" all describe horizontal proximity, but they are not interchangeable. "Beside" and "next to" both mean immediately adjacent, and in most contexts they can substitute for each other without a change in meaning. "Opposite" means facing, with a gap between the two reference points: the pharmacy is opposite the post office, meaning the two buildings face each other across a street or space.

"Behind" and "in front of" describe position along a front-to-back axis. The bakery is behind the bookshop means the bakery is at the rear, further from the viewer's assumed vantage point. "In front of" places something between the viewer and the reference object.

The pair that causes the most persistent confusion is "between" and "among." "Between" locates something in relation to two clearly identified reference points: the café is between the bank and the library. "Among" places something within an undifferentiated group where individual members are not separately named: she found her keys among the papers on the desk. A learner who writes "the cottage was among the lake and the forest" has applied the wrong frame; two named landmarks call for "between." Giving learners a simple test, two named things or an unnamed group, resolves most errors with this pair.

## Prepositions of Movement: Direction and Path

Movement prepositions describe a trajectory, a path that something or someone travels along, rather than a fixed position in space. A place preposition answers the question "where is it?" while a movement preposition answers "where did it go?" or "which way did it travel?" This distinction matters in the classroom because learners who have studied place prepositions first will sometimes transfer them incorrectly to sentences describing motion.

The preposition "to" marks a destination: she walked to the park. "Into" marks entry from outside to inside a bounded space: she walked into the building. Fatima may write "she walked to the building" when she means that the character entered it; the sentence with "to" only confirms arrival at the building's location, not passage through the door. Contrasting the two sentences side by side, and asking Fatima to decide whether the character is now inside or merely nearby, usually resolves the confusion quickly.

"Onto" works in the same way as "into" but for surfaces rather than enclosed spaces: the cat jumped onto the table. "Out of" is the reverse of "into," describing exit from an enclosed space: he ran out of the room. "Away from" describes movement that increases distance from a reference point: the dog ran away from the noise. These three prepositions are worth presenting together because they share the idea of a boundary being crossed or a gap being widened.

"Across" and "through" form another pair that learners conflate. "Across" describes movement over a surface from one side to the other: they swam across the river. "Through" describes movement that passes inside a three-dimensional space: they hiked through the forest. Wei, who writes "they hiked across the forest," has treated the forest as a flat surface rather than a space with interior depth. Asking him whether the hikers were moving over the top of the forest or inside it, among the trees, usually produces the correction.

"Along" describes movement following the length of something: she cycled along the canal. "Past" describes movement that takes a traveler beyond a reference point without stopping: the bus drove past the school. "Around" describes movement that follows the perimeter of something or changes direction to avoid it: the children ran around the fountain. "Up" and "down" describe movement on a vertical or inclined path: he climbed up the stairs; she slid down the hill.

One genuine exception requires explicit attention. With "home" as a destination, English drops the preposition entirely: she went home, he arrived home. Priya, applying the rule she has just learned, will confidently write "she went to home," and the correction can feel arbitrary without a clear explanation. The word "home" in these constructions functions as an adverb rather than a noun, so no preposition is needed. Flagging this pattern directly, rather than waiting for the error to appear, saves time and prevents the learner from doubting the "to" rule she has correctly understood.

## Dependent Prepositions: Verbs and Adjectives That Demand a Partner

Some prepositions attach themselves to particular verbs and adjectives so firmly that the combination behaves almost like a single unit. A dependent preposition is one that a specific verb or adjective requires, and whose selection cannot be worked out from any general principle. The pairing has to be learned whole. Carlos may know every rule about place and direction covered earlier in this unit and still write "he insisted to finish on time," because nothing in those rules tells him that *insist* takes *on*.

The verb group alone contains enough variety to make the point. A speaker *apologizes for* a mistake, *relies on* a colleague, and *agrees with* a suggestion. The same verb can shift its meaning entirely by changing its preposition: to *argue with* someone describes a conflict between people, to *argue about* something identifies the subject of that conflict, and to *argue for* a position means to advocate in its favor. These three sentences are not interchangeable. The preposition carries the distinction.

T: "Can you argue with a decision?"
S: "Yes, if you disagree with it."
T: "And argue for a decision?"
S: "That means you support it, I think."
T: "Exactly. Same verb, different preposition, different meaning."

Adjectives follow the same pattern. A person is *afraid of* spiders, *responsible for* a project, *interested in* history, *familiar with* a procedure, *capable of* great precision, and *disappointed with* a result. None of these choices follows from the meaning of the adjective in any predictable way. Yuki, whose first language pairs the equivalent of "interested" with a preposition closer in meaning to "about," will almost certainly produce "interested about history" before she has encountered the correct form enough times to make it automatic.

First-language transfer produces a reliable set of errors in this area. Learners whose languages allow a direct object after the equivalent of *explain* will write "explain me the rule" rather than "explain the rule to me." Those whose languages use a preposition meaning "of" with the equivalent of *responsible* may write "responsible of the project." The errors are not random; they follow the logic of another system applied to English. Pointing this out rarely prevents the mistake, because the learner has no English rule to substitute for the one being suppressed.

That absence of a rule is the central fact about dependent prepositions. No principle makes *consist of* more logical than *consist from* or *consist in*, even though only *of* is standard. A student who tries to reason her way to the correct preposition will find the reasoning leads nowhere. The only reliable path is repeated exposure to the combination in context, enough times that the pairing becomes familiar as a chunk rather than as a verb or adjective waiting for a preposition to be chosen.

## Teaching Prepositions: Chunks, Patterns, and Classroom Practice

Because dependent prepositions resist logical deduction, the classroom approach that serves learners best treats prepositions as parts of fixed phrases rather than as individual words to be selected by rule. A student who learns *interested* and *in* as separate items will pause each time she needs to combine them, searching for a principle that does not exist. A student who has stored *interested in* as a single unit retrieves it the way she retrieves any familiar word. Every practical decision a teacher makes when planning a preposition lesson flows from that distinction.

The first technique follows directly from that principle: present prepositions inside complete phrases from the very first encounter. When a teacher introduces time expressions, she writes *on the weekend*, *on time*, and *in a hurry* on the board as whole units, not as the prepositions *at*, *on*, and *in* followed by a list of nouns they supposedly govern. Carlos, copying *on time* into his notebook as a phrase, is far better placed than one who copies *on + time expressions* and then tries to remember which nouns belong to which preposition. The phrase is the unit of learning.

The second technique uses pattern drills that place contrasting combinations side by side. A teacher might write three sentences on the board and ask the class to complete them: *She is interested ___ history*, *He is good ___ math*, *They are afraid ___ the dark*. When Yuki reads all three aloud in sequence, the different prepositions become salient precisely because the surrounding structure is so similar. Repeating the drill across several lessons builds the kind of automatic retrieval that frees a learner's attention for meaning rather than form.

The third technique asks learners to keep a collocation notebook in which every new verb-plus-preposition or adjective-plus-preposition combination is recorded as a full example sentence. Each entry should be a complete sentence; for *rely on*, a learner writes *We rely on the bus to get to school* rather than the bare phrase alone. A student who reviews her notebook before a writing task is rehearsing real language, not a stripped-down formula. Full sentences keep the combination intact in a way that bare word lists cannot, because bare lists invite learners to treat the preposition as an afterthought.

A simple activity brings these principles together in a single lesson. Give learners a short paragraph, perhaps four or five sentences, with every preposition removed and replaced by a blank. Ask them to predict the missing words before any discussion begins. Predictions will vary. Some will be wrong, and that moment of uncertainty is the lesson. When Wei writes *consist from* and then sees that the original reads *consist of*, the teacher can say:

T: Why do you think the writer used *of* here?
S: I'm not sure. It just sounds right to me now.
T: That feeling of rightness comes from having seen the phrase before. There is no deeper reason.

That exchange makes the arbitrary nature of dependent prepositions visible and gives learners a concrete reason to record new combinations carefully rather than trust that logic will supply the answer next time.

   b. on

   a. 'Among' requires a preposition of movement, not place.
   b. 'Among' is only used with people, not landmarks.
   c. Two named landmarks require 'between,' not 'among.'
   d. 'Among' must follow a verb of location such as 'sit' or 'stand.'

   *Answer: Two named landmarks require 'between,' not 'among.'*
   *Anchor: "two named landmarks call for "between""*

**3. A learner writes 'she walked to the building' when she means the character entered it. Which preposition should replace 'to'?**

   a. through
   b. into
   c. onto
   d. across

   *Answer: into*
   *Anchor: ""Into" marks entry from outside to inside a bounded space"*

   *Answer: 'At the hospital' indicates a general location, as when giving directions, while 'in the hospital' means the person is physically inside it, often implying they are a patient.*
