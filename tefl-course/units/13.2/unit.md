# Alternative Assessment

## What Alternative Assessment Is and Why It Matters

Alternative assessment is a type of evaluation that directly measures learners' ability to use the language. Paper-and-pencil tests reveal what a learner knows about the language; alternative assessment reveals what a learner can do with it. That distinction shapes every practical decision a teacher makes when choosing how to evaluate a class.

The first reason to use alternative assessment is that it places learners in situations where they make real communicative use of English for a genuine purpose. A learner like Fatima, for example, is not filling in a grammar table; she is using the language as a means of communication to accomplish something meaningful. The second reason is that alternative assessment allows learners to demonstrate what they have learned and how well they can apply it. Criteria based on the goals of the class are established in advance, so both teacher and student understand what successful completion looks like before the work begins.

The third reason concerns the learner's role in the process. When students understand how to evaluate their own performance, they can take responsibility for directing some of their own learning. A student like Carlos who has internalized the criteria for a writing task can read his own draft with a purposeful, critical eye rather than waiting passively for a grade. The fourth reason is motivational. When learners use the language for real purposes and see their own progress clearly, their motivation to continue using English may increase. The fifth reason extends beyond the classroom walls: alternative assessment gives learners the chance to display their progress directly to family members, school administrators, and others in their community, making learning visible in a way that a test score alone rarely achieves.

The term "alternative assessment" covers a wide range of evaluation procedures, and teachers should be aware of the full menu before selecting any single approach. The types named in this module are self-assessment, self-record-keeping, peer feedback and peer assessment, portfolios, performance assessment, observations, conferences and interviews, learning logs, and journals. Each serves a different purpose and suits different classroom contexts, age groups, and proficiency levels. The sections that follow examine the most prominent of these in detail, beginning with self-record-keeping and peer feedback.

## Self-Record-Keeping and Peer Assessment

Self-record-keeping is a practice in which students maintain a written account of their own completed work, typically using a chart or checklist that the teacher has prepared and posted on the classroom wall. In a class where students track their reading and homework assignments this way, a student like Yuki can see at a glance which tasks she has finished and which remain outstanding, without waiting for the teacher to tell her. The charts themselves require no special materials; teachers or students can construct them from cardboard, posterboard, or recycled paper. Because the record is visible and belongs to the student, it places the responsibility for monitoring progress directly in her hands.

Peer assessment is a related practice in which learners evaluate each other's work using guidelines set in advance by the teacher. In the classroom example from this module, students are placed in pairs to work on a writing assignment intended for their portfolios. One student, Daniel, reads his partner's draft and gives feedback on the ideas, the organization of the content, and the clarity of the writing; his partner does the same for him. This exchange also gives both students a chance to talk through the assignment itself and share ideas before either has committed to a final version. Because the feedback occurs during the writing process rather than after a final grade has been assigned, this kind of peer work is a form of formative assessment, meaning assessment that takes place during learning and is intended to support further progress.

For pairs to function well in this role, the teacher must supply clear guidelines before the activity begins. Without them, a student like Daniel has no reliable basis for deciding what to look for in his partner's writing, and the feedback he gives may be vague or misdirected. The guidelines tell students which specific features of the work to examine, so their comments are useful rather than general. The teacher's role during the activity shifts accordingly: rather than directing the whole class, the teacher moves among pairs and works with those who need additional support, while the pairs themselves manage the task.

Students in a peer assessment activity carry genuine responsibilities. They are expected to stay on task, complete the work within the time the teacher has set, and treat their role as evaluator with the same seriousness they would bring to their own writing. A student who gives only superficial comments, or who spends the time on unrelated conversation, fails both herself and her partner. When students take the role seriously, peer assessment gives them practice in applying criteria to real language use, which is itself a form of language learning.

## Portfolios: Building a Record of Progress

A portfolio is a systematic collection of a learner's work over a period of time, evaluated according to pre-set criteria. The criteria are what distinguish a portfolio from a simple folder of accumulated papers; without them, the collection has no evaluative purpose. Fatima, for example, might keep every draft of her essays alongside reading response sheets and a short written reflection on what she has improved, all measured against standards she received at the start of the term.

One practical advantage of the portfolio is that it keeps all of a student's work in one place, which allows both the teacher and the student to track progress across weeks or months. A teacher reviewing Carlos's binder in December can compare his October writing samples with his most recent drafts and identify where he has grown and where he still needs support. That kind of longitudinal view is difficult to achieve with a single end-of-term test.

Portfolios also give students direct responsibility for managing and reflecting on their own learning. Rather than waiting for a grade to tell him how he is doing, a student like Wei selects which pieces to include, writes brief reflections on his choices, and monitors his own progress against the criteria the class has agreed upon. This responsibility is not incidental; it is one of the central purposes of using the portfolio as an assessment tool.

The portfolio also makes a student's progress visible to people outside the classroom. Parents, school principals, and program directors can look through a student's collected work and see concrete evidence of what she has learned and how her language use has developed over time. A parent who attends a portfolio review evening can read her daughter's writing from September and then from March, and the growth is apparent without any explanation from the teacher.

The physical form of a portfolio depends on the age and context of the learners. In one classroom shown in the video for *Shaping the Way We Teach English*, the module's source, younger students kept their work in large plastic envelopes stored in bins. Older students used large binders that sat on shelves in the classroom; students picked up their binders when they arrived, so the materials were immediately available for use during the lesson. In both cases, the portfolios remained in the classroom rather than traveling home, which kept the collection intact and organized throughout the term.

Portfolios are suitable for learners at any age or proficiency level, which makes them one of the more flexible tools available to a language teacher. A beginning-level adult class might include vocabulary logs and short writing tasks, while an advanced secondary class might include multiple drafts of analytical essays and reading comprehension activities. Students and the teacher typically decide together what will go into the portfolio for end-of-term evaluation, and they also agree on the criteria by which that collection will be judged. That shared decision-making means students understand from the outset what a successful portfolio looks like, and they can work toward that standard throughout the course.

## Performance Assessment and Rubrics

Performance assessment rests on two requirements: a task for students to perform and a set of criteria that define what a successful performance looks like. During the performance itself, both the teacher and the student audience evaluate what they see against those established criteria. When the performance ends, they offer constructive feedback to the performers, giving the assessment a formative quality even within what might appear to be a summative event.

The range of tasks that qualify as performance assessment is wider than many teachers initially expect. In one classroom example, individual students report the results of their group's research on an academic topic. To do this, a student such as Carlos must read source material, organize the group's findings, write key points on an overhead transparency, and then speak to the class. Four distinct language skills are present in a single assessed event. In a second example, a group of students performs a dramatization of an abridged version of King Lear. A student like Priya must listen carefully to hear the play read correctly, read for comprehension, sustain reading fluency, attend to pronunciation, and deliver her lines with dramatic expression. The two tasks look nothing alike on the surface, yet both meet the definition of performance assessment because each involves a task and a set of evaluative criteria applied in real time.

Criteria and guidelines, as defined in this module, are standards set in advance and shared with the learner that establish how a performance will be evaluated. Advance sharing is what separates performance assessment from informal observation. When a teacher hands Priya a rubric before rehearsals begin, she knows that pronunciation and dramatic reading are being assessed, and she can direct her preparation accordingly.

The specific criteria a teacher selects should reflect the actual goals of the class, because a single performance task can serve many different purposes. A teacher might design the King Lear dramatization primarily to build confidence and inspire creativity, in which case the rubric would weight those qualities heavily. Another teacher using the same text might focus on pronunciation and reading fluency, producing a rubric that looks quite different. Other plausible purposes include practicing oral fluency, developing study skills, learning to give oral reports, and building the language needed to discuss a particular topic. The appropriate choice depends on what the class has been working toward.

The teacher may establish the criteria independently, or may negotiate them with students. Negotiation has a practical advantage: when a student like Carlos has contributed to writing the rubric, he is more likely to understand what each criterion means and to apply it accurately when evaluating a classmate's report. Shared ownership matters. Regardless of how the criteria are developed, students must receive them before the performance begins. Receiving the criteria in advance means that evaluation during and after the task is grounded in transparent, agreed-upon standards rather than impressionistic judgment.

## The Four Defining Characteristics of Alternative Assessment

Any form of alternative assessment, regardless of its specific format, shares four defining characteristics. The first is that it begins with course objectives. A portfolio in an adult literacy class and a dramatized reading in a secondary school are very different activities, yet both must be anchored to what the class has set out to achieve. The second characteristic is that learners receive criteria describing successful achievement before the assessment begins, a principle the preceding section examined in detail. The third is that the assessment evaluates actual student performance, meaning the learner demonstrates what she can do with the language rather than what she knows about it. The fourth is that the student takes part in the evaluation process itself, whether through self-assessment, peer feedback, or a conference with the teacher.

These four characteristics mark a clear boundary between alternative assessment and the discrete-point tests that dominate many traditional programs. A multiple-choice grammar test measures a learner's knowledge about the language: a student like Yuki may correctly identify the past perfect in a sentence without being able to use it in conversation. Alternative assessment shifts the question from "Does Yuki know the rule?" to "Can Yuki use the language to accomplish something real?" This is the difference between testing language knowledge and testing language use, and the distinction carries practical weight when a teacher is deciding which instrument to reach for.

Alternative assessment is the stronger choice in three situations. The first is when the goal is to evaluate genuine communicative use, as in a performance assessment where students report research findings to the class. The second is when a teacher needs to track progress over time rather than capture a single moment; a portfolio kept across a semester shows a student like Fatima moving from halting paragraph drafts to organized essays in a way that no end-of-term test can replicate. The third is when the aim is to involve learners in directing their own learning. Self-assessment and peer feedback, supported by clear criteria, give students like Carlos the tools to identify their own gaps and set their own next steps.

Traditional summative assessment still serves a purpose of its own. Formal testing at the end of a learning period provides a structured measure of what a student has learned overall, and some institutional contexts require that kind of documented, comparable result. Summative and formative assessment serve different functions; neither outranks the other. Formative assessment is relatively informal, occurs during the learning process, and provides feedback that shapes what happens next in the classroom. Summative assessment closes a period of study and records an outcome. A well-designed course will generally include both, using alternative forms to support and document learning as it unfolds, and reserving more formal evaluation for the moments when a cumulative record is genuinely needed.

## Putting It Into Practice: Designing Your Own Alternative Assessment

The first practical step is to select a single language learning objective from a course you are currently teaching or planning to teach. The objective should be specific enough to be measurable; for example, a learner like Fatima might be working toward the ability to give a short oral report summarizing group research on a topic from her academic preparation class. Once the objective is clear, you can decide which form of alternative assessment fits it most naturally. A performance assessment suits Fatima's objective well, since it evaluates her actual use of the language rather than her knowledge about it.

The second step asks you to choose between two design paths. The short-term option involves building a single lesson task around the objective and writing a set of criteria that will be used to assess it. The long-term option involves designing an evaluation plan that spans several class sessions, such as a portfolio that accumulates drafts, peer feedback sheets, and a final reflection. Both paths require the same thing: a written set of criteria that specifies what successful completion looks like. Without those criteria, neither you nor your students have a shared understanding of the target. A student like Carlos, for instance, cannot improve his essay organization through peer feedback if neither he nor his partner knows what organized writing is expected to look like in this class.

The third step is to bring your plan to a small group of colleagues or fellow trainees. You explain the objective, walk through the criteria, and describe how you intend to use them during the assessment. Your peers may notice gaps you have missed; they might point out that a criterion such as "speaks clearly" is too vague to be applied consistently, and suggest replacing it with something more observable, such as "uses complete sentences and maintains a pace the audience can follow." This kind of peer review of your own design mirrors the peer assessment process you are preparing to use with learners.

The fourth step is revision. You take the feedback from your group and adjust the plan before bringing it into the classroom. A criterion that seemed obvious to you may have confused three out of four colleagues, which is a reliable signal that it will confuse students as well. Once the plan is revised, you try it with your class, or, if you are not currently teaching, you ask an experienced teacher or trainer to review the final version.

The thread connecting all four steps is the requirement that criteria be shared with learners before the assessment begins. This principle appears in every form of alternative assessment covered in this unit. Peer assessment depends on it, because students evaluating a classmate's writing need to know in advance what they are looking for. Portfolios depend on it, because students cannot curate their own work meaningfully without knowing the standards against which it will be judged. Performance assessment depends on it, because a student preparing to dramatize a scene or deliver a report needs to understand what a successful performance requires. Designing your own assessment is, in this sense, an exercise in making expectations visible, to yourself first, and then to the learners in your class.
