# Mistakes, Errors, and What They Tell You

## Two Different Problems: Mistakes vs. Errors

The distinction between a mistake and an error determines what a teacher does next. A mistake is incorrect language production that results from inattention or carelessness rather than incomplete learning. An error reflects a consistent pattern showing that the learner has not yet fully acquired a particular feature of the language. The same surface form, a missing verb ending or a misplaced word, can belong to either category, and treating one as the other wastes instructional time.

Consider two students working on a written narrative task. Yuki, writing quickly to finish before the bell, produces the sentence 'he go to the market every day' in an otherwise accurate paragraph where she correctly writes 'she walks' and 'he runs' several times. That single lapse is almost certainly a mistake: the knowledge is present, but attention slipped under time pressure. Daniel, in the same class, submits a draft in which 'he go,' 'she come,' and 'the teacher explain' appear throughout, with no instances of the third-person singular inflection anywhere in the text, a consistent pattern signaling that the rule has not been internalized.

The source material frames the diagnostic step as the teacher's first obligation: understand why the incorrect form occurred, and determine whether the student lacked the knowledge to produce the correct form or made a careless slip. For Yuki, a brief individual note may be sufficient, because the underlying knowledge is already there. Daniel's case calls for focused attention to the rule itself, perhaps through a structured activity or targeted feedback across several lessons, a response proportionate to what the pattern reveals.

A classroom exchange can make the diagnostic process visible. After collecting drafts, a teacher might proceed as follows:

T: Daniel, I noticed you wrote 'he go to school' here, and the same pattern appears in a few other places. Can you tell me the rule for verbs when the subject is 'he' or 'she'?
S: I'm not quite sure. Do you add something to the verb, or change it?
T: Think about 'she walks.' What did we add there?
S: An 's,' so it would be 'he goes'?
T: Yes. Look at your draft with me and find the other places where that applies.

That brief exchange reveals that Daniel has partial awareness but has not consolidated the rule, confirming the error diagnosis. Had Daniel immediately self-corrected and said 'Oh, I just forgot the s,' the teacher would have reasonable grounds to treat the instance as a mistake. The distinction is not always clean, but the exchange makes the reasoning transparent and gives the teacher a basis for choosing a response.

Recognizing the difference also protects students from unnecessary correction. Over-correcting a student who already possesses the knowledge can undermine confidence and create the impression that their language is more deficient than it is. The source material cautions against over-correction and excessive negative feedback, grounded in the principle that not every incorrect form signals a gap in learning. Research supports calibrated corrective feedback: Lyster and Saito (2010) found, across a meta-analysis of fifteen classroom studies involving 827 learners, that oral corrective feedback produces significant and durable effects, with prompts that push students toward self-correction outperforming recasts in which the teacher restates the correct form. The teacher's response should be proportionate to what the form reveals about the learner's current state of knowledge.

## Interlanguage: The Learner's Developing System

When a learner produces a sentence like "She go to school yesterday," the missing past-tense marker reflects the state of a temporary, evolving linguistic system that researchers call interlanguage. Interlanguage is the rule-governed system a learner constructs on the path toward the target language, drawing on knowledge of the first language, partially acquired target-language rules, and hypotheses the learner has formed about how the new language works. It operates as a system in its own right, with its own internal logic at each stage of development, distinct from both the learner's mother tongue and the target language.

The source material defines error as language production that shows "incomplete or incorrect learning," and that definition points directly to what interlanguage errors reveal. When a student named Fatima consistently omits the third-person singular "s" across many different verbs and contexts, that pattern is evidence that her current interlanguage system has not yet incorporated that rule, even though she may be producing otherwise well-formed sentences. Systematic errors, those that recur across learners at similar stages of development, are the visible surface of this internal system and carry diagnostic information a teacher can use.

The source frames trial and error as "learning by trying new or different language, taking risks, and learning from errors or mistakes." Error production is an active, necessary part of acquisition. A student named Carlos who attempts a complex conditional sentence and gets it wrong is doing something more productive than a student who avoids the structure entirely to stay safe, because the attempt generates data, both for the learner and for the teacher, about where the interlanguage system currently stands.

Interlanguage shifts as learners receive new input, notice mismatches between their output and the target language, and adjust their internal rules accordingly. The source stresses that teachers should tell students that making mistakes helps them learn, and it calls for creating a classroom atmosphere in which students see mistakes as part of the process. A learner who understands that each error marks a stage in a developing system, rather than a fixed deficiency, is more likely to take the risks that move the system forward. Lyster and Saito (2010) found that oral corrective feedback produces significant and durable effects on acquisition; the feedback a teacher provides in response to interlanguage errors is therefore one of the forces that causes the internal system to reorganize and develop. Li (2010) corroborates this finding across a broader set of studies, reinforcing the case for consistent, well-timed corrective feedback as a driver of interlanguage progression.

## Errors as Evidence of Learning

Viewing errors as diagnostic data changes what a teacher does with them. When a student named Carlos says "yesterday I go to the market," the unmarked past tense is evidence that Carlos has acquired the verb but has not yet stabilized the past-tense inflection in spontaneous speech. The source material defines an error as a pattern in production that reflects incomplete rather than finished learning. That distinction tells the teacher where instruction needs to go next, not only that something went wrong.

The trial-and-error process described in the source material makes this diagnostic value concrete. Students who take risks with new language, attempting structures they have not fully mastered, generate the errors that reveal the current state of their developing system. A student named Priya who attempts a conditional sentence and produces "if I will study, I pass the exam" is doing something more productive than a student who stays silent to avoid mistakes. Priya's attempt reveals which part of the conditional structure needs attention, and it shows Priya that communication is possible even when the form is imperfect.

Research confirms that acting on those errors through corrective feedback produces measurable gains. Lyster and Saito (2010) conducted a meta-analysis of fifteen classroom studies involving 827 learners and found that oral corrective feedback has significant, durable effects on acquisition, with longer treatments producing larger effects; their analysis also found that prompts, pushing the student to self-correct, outperformed recasts, where the teacher restates the correct form. Li (2010), in a separate meta-analysis of thirty-three studies, corroborates that corrective feedback advances acquisition. Errors are teachable moments with a measurable payoff when a teacher responds to them well.

The source material is explicit that teachers should tell students directly that making mistakes is part of the learning process, and that stated position must be supported by the teacher's actual behavior in the classroom. A teacher who says mistakes are welcome but then responds to every error with immediate, public correction will not be believed. Feedback practices and stated beliefs must align. When they do, a student like Carlos or Priya feels safe enough to attempt the next difficult structure rather than retreating to language already under control.

Alignment between belief and practice underlies the affective domain guidance in the source material, which calls for creating a positive atmosphere for risk-taking so that students feel comfortable trying to use the language. Comfort matters. Confidence and accuracy develop together when students understand that an error is a step in a process rather than a verdict on their ability. The teacher who reads errors as evidence of learning, and who responds with that understanding, gives students both the corrective information they need and the motivation to keep producing language.

## Diagnosing What Went Wrong: Types of Error

Once a teacher has determined that a production problem is a genuine error rather than a careless slip, the diagnostic work begins. The source material defines diagnosing as the process of determining what kind of mistake or error the student is making and what might be causing it. Two obligations follow from that definition: classification and explanation. Understanding why something is wrong shapes every decision that follows, because the nature of the error determines the nature of the response.

The source organizes language learning feedback across three domains, and errors can arise in any of them. Linguistic errors concern the actual use of the language, covering grammar, vocabulary, and pronunciation. Communicative errors affect the task or the content of the message; a student may produce grammatically acceptable language that nonetheless fails to convey the intended meaning. Affective factors, while not errors in the strict sense, can suppress production to the point where a teacher misreads silence or avoidance as an error when the underlying cause is anxiety. Keeping these domains distinct helps a teacher ask the right diagnostic question before reaching for a response.

Within the linguistic domain, the source identifies three specific error types: grammatical, lexical, and pragmatic. A grammatical error involves an incorrect structural form. A student named Wei might say, "She go to school yesterday," applying a present-tense form where the past tense is required; the rule is identifiable, and the cause is likely incomplete acquisition of past-tense morphology. A lexical error involves the wrong word choice rather than a structural problem; if a student named Priya says, "I am very boring in this class," she has selected an adjective that describes the class rather than her own feeling, and the intended meaning is obscured by a confusion between participial adjective pairs.

Pragmatic errors are the most context-dependent of the three types and therefore the most easily overlooked. The source defines pragmatics as the social and cultural aspects of language use; a pragmatic error occurs when a student produces language that is grammatically correct but socially or culturally inappropriate for the situation. A student named Fatima might address a new teacher with, "Hey, tell me the answer," using a register suited to a peer rather than an authority figure. The sentence contains no grammatical fault, yet it signals incomplete learning of the conventions governing polite requests in an academic setting. Identifying the error as pragmatic rather than grammatical is what allows the teacher to respond usefully.

The diagnostic step matters because the error type determines which domain the teacher's response should address. A grammatical error calls for attention to form; a lexical error calls for attention to meaning and word relationships; a pragmatic error calls for attention to context and social convention. Treating all three with the same corrective move risks addressing the surface of the problem while leaving its cause untouched. Research supports the value of well-targeted oral corrective feedback: Lyster and Saito (2010) found, across a meta-analysis of fifteen classroom studies involving 827 learners, that corrective feedback produces significant and durable effects on acquisition, with prompts that push students toward self-correction outperforming recasts in which the teacher restates the correct form. A teacher who pauses to classify before responding is positioned to give feedback that connects to the actual source of the difficulty rather than its symptom.

## Patterns Across Learners: Using Error Analysis to Plan Teaching

Diagnosing a single student's error is one task; reading the same error across twenty students is another, and the second task carries more direct consequences for lesson planning. When a teacher collects written work or listens during an oral activity, certain problems surface repeatedly across the group. Five students omit the third-person singular 's' in present-tense statements. Seven students phrase a request in a way that sounds abrupt to a native speaker. These recurrences signal a shared point in the learners' interlanguage, the developing internal grammar that learners construct as they move toward the target language, and they therefore signal a shared instructional need.

The source material draws a practical distinction between dealing with errors the whole class is making and dealing with individual errors, and that distinction has real consequences for how a teacher allocates time. An error belonging to one student warrants a targeted response, perhaps a written note on returned work or a brief exchange during a task, whereas an error belonging to the group warrants a planned teaching moment. Leaving a class-wide gap unaddressed means the underlying problem persists for most of the room.

Delayed feedback is a natural vehicle for a class-wide response of this kind. Rather than interrupting a fluency-focused activity to correct grammar, the teacher takes notes during the task and returns to the pattern afterward. The source illustrates this directly: if many students had the same language problem or were making mistakes on one part of the content, the teacher could have the whole class work on it after the activity was completed. Consider a role-play in which students practice making requests at a shop counter; during the activity, the teacher notices that several students say 'Give me the blue one' rather than using any softening form, so the communicative task continues without interruption, and the teacher addresses the pragmatic pattern with the whole group once it concludes.

T: 'I heard a few people say, "Give me the blue one." That works, but it can sound quite direct. How could we make it softer?'
S: 'Can I have the blue one?'
T: 'Good. What else?'
S: 'Could I get the blue one, please?'

A sequence like this treats the error as a teaching opportunity and reaches every student who made the same pragmatic misstep. Research supports the value of prompting students toward self-correction rather than supplying the correct form directly; Lyster and Saito (2010) found across fifteen classroom studies that prompts produced more durable gains than recasts, and Li (2010) corroborates the broader finding that corrective feedback advances acquisition. The whole-class delayed format shown above is one practical way to deliver that kind of prompt at scale, and the longer the treatment, the larger the gains tend to be (Lyster & Saito, 2010).

Acting on a pattern still requires the judgment the source describes: the teacher must weigh whether the error matters enough to correct and whether fluency was the priority for the task in which it appeared. A pronunciation variation that does not impede understanding may not warrant class time, whereas a grammatical structure causing consistent miscommunication almost certainly does. At the class level, the teacher applies the same principle that applies at the individual level, asking whether the error affects the communicative goals of the course.

Error analysis, understood in these terms, becomes a planning instrument. What a class consistently gets wrong across several activities is a reliable indicator of what the class has not yet acquired, and a teacher who keeps brief records of recurring errors over two or three weeks will find that those records sketch the outline of the next unit's focus. The errors are not evidence of failure. They are data about where instruction should go next.

## Formative Feedback and the Diagnostic Mindset

Formative feedback, as the source defines it, is continuous and given at all stages throughout the learning period to determine what has been learned so far and what still needs work. Summative feedback arrives at the end of a unit or course to measure what learners have achieved overall. A final test score tells a teacher how a student performed, but it does not explain why a particular structure is still missing from that student's production or what the next lesson should address. Shaping instruction requires information gathered while there is still time to act on it.

The mistake-error distinction feeds directly into formative practice. When a teacher identifies a pattern as an error rather than a careless slip, that identification is a diagnostic act, and the source's action-plan guidance puts the point plainly: the teacher's first task is to determine what the student's problem is. Consider a student named Yuki who consistently writes "He go to school yesterday" across several different writing tasks. The consistent pattern signals incomplete acquisition of past tense, not inattention, and that diagnosis changes what the teacher does next. A gap in learning requires instruction rather than a reminder to be careful.

Diagnosis also requires the teacher to keep the language goal of the session in view. A teacher named Mr. Osei, whose class is practicing fluency in a storytelling activity, interrupts the communicative purpose of the task and shifts learner attention away from meaning if he stops to correct every tense error as it occurs. The appropriate move is to note the recurring errors, let the activity run, and return to the language point in a subsequent focused exercise. Lyster and Saito (2010) found that prompts, which push the student toward self-correction, produce stronger and more durable results than recasts, in which the teacher restates the correct form; that finding reinforces the value of planned, deliberate feedback over reflexive interruption.

Teachers who approach errors with a diagnostic mindset accumulate something more useful than a record of what went wrong: they build a picture of where each learner stands relative to the goals of the course. Li (2010) corroborates that corrective feedback supports acquisition, so the feedback decisions a teacher makes during the learning period have real consequences for what students ultimately internalize. A teacher who understands why an error occurred is positioned to give feedback that moves the learner forward, rather than feedback that marks only a boundary between correct and incorrect.

Diagnostic work carries an affective dimension inseparable from its linguistic one. A teacher who has taken the time to understand an error can respond in a way that treats the error as a natural part of learning. Errors are not failures. When a student named Carlos produces an incorrect conditional form during a speaking task, a teacher who recognizes the error as a predictable developmental stage can respond with a prompt that invites self-correction, keeping Carlos engaged and confident rather than embarrassed. Formative feedback, understood in full, aims to build the kind of classroom atmosphere in which students see their errors as information about their own progress and continue to take the risks that learning requires.
