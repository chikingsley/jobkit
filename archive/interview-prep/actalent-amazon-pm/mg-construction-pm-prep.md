# Interview Prep — Construction PM "Meet & Greet" (multi-site / regional program)

> Same Actalent/Amazon opportunity as `amazon-cm-ssd-interview-prep.md`, next-round interview ("M&G" = Meet & Greet). This round probes architect partnership, MEP, regional→national travel, training ramp, and projects-ready-immediately. Anchor everything to the Pfizer/Seagen Bothell retrofit work. Spoken practice scripts (one per question, written in Chi's cadence and run through the TEFL anti-slop rules) live in `practice-scripts/`.

## Decode: the two terms that threw you

**MEP = Mechanical, Electrical, Plumbing** — a building's core systems. Mechanical = HVAC; Electrical = power/lighting/fire alarm; Plumbing = water/sewer/gas (+ fire sprinkler). You DO have this — Seagen was MEP-heavy (pharma temperature control, electrical service, sprinklers for racked storage). Never say "no familiarity."

**Partnership with Architects** — the architect is the design lead (Architect of Record). They're asking whether you can work the *design* side: design reviews, constructability feedback, RFIs, submittals, resolving design-vs-field conflicts collaboratively. Your hook: you built the existing-conditions/drone survey the design baseline used, and fed field reality back to the design team.

## The questions they sent — your answers

### 1. Budget you're comfortable managing in a calendar year
They want a *dollar figure for annual responsibility* (a portfolio across multiple projects), not one project.
- Honest anchor: *"I've owned a $5M warehouse buildout end-to-end — budget, buyout, and cost tracking."*
- Stretch to the role: *"Across a regional portfolio of concurrent projects I'd be comfortable carrying roughly $10–20M in aggregate annual project value."*
- **Then calibrate:** *"What range does this role typically carry in a year?"* — turns it into a conversation and shows you think in portfolio terms.

### 2. Partnership with Architects
See decode above. Frame it as collaborative, early, RFI-disciplined, protect design intent. Mention you've worked from a design set → permitting → field.

### 3. Knowledge of MEP Systems
See decode above. Lead with the pharma HVAC story — it's a harder MEP problem than a standard building, so it lands strong.

### 4. Difficult situation within construction — dial into SOLUTIONS, not problems
They explicitly want a solutions-orientation. STAR, keep the "problem" to one sentence, spend the time on the fix.
- Strong real one: **permitting friction with the City of Bothell.** Problem (1 line): a permit item threatened the schedule. Action: owned it end-to-end through the MyBuildingPermit.com portal, ran a tight follow-up cadence with the city, kept the submittal moving. Result: issuance held the schedule.
- Or the **remote-senior-CM coordination** story: senior CM was in South Carolina, you were the only set of eyes on site — you ran a standing safety+schedule walk and got anything off-plan to him same-day so decisions didn't wait.
- End every version on: *"My instinct is to show up with the fix and 2 options, not just the problem."* — that's the exact phrase they're fishing for.

### 5. How do you feel about managing budgets
Attitude check. Be affirmatively comfortable + show a system.
- *"Comfortable — it's core to the job. I track committed vs. forecast cost, manage change orders tightly so scope changes get priced and approved before work proceeds, and I'd rather flag a budget risk early than surprise an owner late."*

### 6. Opinion on site safety
Non-negotiable, culture-first, and you have a real habit to point to.
- *"Safety comes before schedule, full stop. On my project I walked the site on a standing checklist with safety as the first item before progress. I believe in stop-work authority — anyone can stop work for an unsafe condition, no penalty. A safe site is also a productive site; incidents are the most expensive schedule hit there is."*

### 7. What questions do you have? (always have 3+)
1. *"What does the project pipeline look like over the next year — new builds, retrofits, or a mix — and what's the typical project size?"*
2. *"How is the architect/design relationship structured — in-house, or external firms I'd partner with per project?"*
3. *"You mentioned aligning projects regionally with possible national travel later — what's the realistic travel expectation in the first 6–12 months?"*  ← (this one matters to you; see note)
4. *"You said you're building out the training program — what does ramp-up look like for someone starting now with projects ready immediately?"*

## Their tells (from what the recruiter told you)
- **"Projects immediately"** → they need someone who can start producing fast. Lean into your readiness.
- **"Aligning regionally, possibly national travel later"** → ask the travel question (Q3 above). Flag against your own location/travel constraints before you accept anything.

## The two questions I stumbled on last time

### A. "What's your criteria for confirming a project is ready for construction?"

The reframe: the schedule isn't the *first* criterion, it's the *proof* that all the others are met. Walk a readiness checklist, then land on the schedule as the integration point. (Industry name: **Construction Readiness Review**, gated before mobilization; the upstream discipline is **front-end planning / scope definition**, formalized as **PDRI — Project Definition Rating Index**, CII's go/no-go tool at "Gate 3." Don't have to name PDRI, but "I'd run a readiness review against a definition checklist" signals program-level thinking.)

The checklist, in order:

1. **Design complete and IFC** ("Issued For Construction," not design-development) **and coordinated across disciplines** (architectural + MEP clash-free). ← ties to the architect + MEP answers.
2. **Scope fully defined** — no open assumptions (the PDRI idea).
3. **Permits in hand or a known path** — building/mechanical/electrical/fire, plus specials (high-pile storage, racking seismic).
4. **Budget approved and contracts executed** — bought out, GMP in place, Notice-to-Proceed ready.
5. **Long-lead procurement secured** — equipment ordered, lead times known and *inside* the schedule.
6. **Existing-conditions and phasing resolved** (retrofits) — occupant downtime windows agreed, phasing/wall-off plan set, utility shutdown/tie-in sequence planned. (The Pfizer lab-move coordination.)

Land it on the real point:

> *"And the schedule is where I integrate all of that. If I can build a credible, resource-loaded schedule with no open assumptions in it, that's my signal we're ready — a schedule with holes is just a list of unanswered questions. When every input has a real date behind it, we're ready."*

Vocab upgrade: the "50% of budget spent by 70% of the timeline" rule is a **cash-flow / spend S-curve** — name it and it reads as discipline, not gut feel.

### B. "Walk me through a project end-to-end"

It came out un-smooth because end-to-end needs a fixed narrative spine. Use these six beats (~20s each, ~2 min total) for ANY project:

1. **Why it existed + your role** (1 sentence business driver, 1 sentence your scope).
2. **Planning & design** — what you defined, how you worked the design team.
3. **Procurement & buyout** — contractors, long-leads.
4. **Permitting / approvals.**
5. **Execution** — put **the one thing that went sideways + how you solved it** here.
6. **Turnover + result + lesson.**

Which project:

- **Lead with the Pfizer lab-suite move** for this question — cleanest full lifecycle you personally drove. Standout beat-5: coordinating directly with scientists for downtime windows and phasing the build so experiments restarted in one area while you walled off and finished the rest. Memorable.
- **Boeing Mesa tanks** = backup for an environmental/regulatory prompt (NPDES renewal deadline → assess three 500k-gal equalization tanks → contingency for blast/recoat/weld repair → coordinate around production testing → execute). Don't force it.
- **Seagen $5M warehouse** = answer for "biggest project / budget owned."

Don't tell all four. Pick Pfizer, run the six beats.

### Ready-to-read Pfizer end-to-end script

> ⚠️ **Number check before using:** don't say "$5M" and "500,000 sq ft" together — that's $10/SF and an interviewer will catch it. A racking+MEP+electrical retrofit is $40–150/SF. Either it's the ~40–50k sq ft Bothell building at ~$5M (≈$100–125/SF, clean), or it was ~500k sq ft and the budget was tens of millions. Lead with sq ft OR dollars, not the ratio. Script below omits the budget on purpose — confirm it first.

Term fixes: the truck-connection plate = **dock leveler**. The sprinkler problem = **sprinkler clearance violation** (NFPA 13 needs **18 in.** min between top of storage and sprinkler deflector); fix = raise/relocate the heads. Closeout certifiers = **AHJ/building dept (C of O)**, **fire marshal** (life-safety), **commissioning agent (Cx)** (MEP), **third-party special inspectors** (steel/welds/concrete, during construction), **QA IQ/OQ/PQ** (pharma). Tools to name: Kahua, Microsoft Project, ServiceNow.

> "More recently I owned an owner's-rep retrofit of a Pfizer distribution warehouse, end to end. The driver was straightforward — Pfizer was scaling up a couple of commercial drugs and needed more storage and shipping capacity — so we converted an existing building: relocating the shipping-and-receiving team into the space, installing storage racking, and building out the electrical and the rest of the MEP to support it.
>
> There was a senior PM who took the lead when we had to pitch executives for funding, but day-to-day I owned every step. I'll walk it through in phases.
>
> In **design**, I was the owner's rep working directly with the design team, and my main job was getting the scope tightly defined — a loose scope is what kills you later. From there I ran the **bid**: pre-qualifying contractors, running the RFP, managing the walkthroughs and the RFI process, then presenting my award recommendation and the budget breakdown to the stakeholders who had to approve the spend.
>
> Through **procurement** I stayed on top of the vendors — racking, dock equipment, long-lead electrical gear — tracking each one against its promised delivery so nothing surprised the schedule. We had a dedicated scheduler, but I owned the schedule; I fed her what went in and managed it in Microsoft Project, with costs and invoices in Kahua.
>
> Once budget and schedule were approved, we mobilized. In **construction** I was on site week to week — walking the job for safety first: OSHA compliance, PPE, stormwater — then tracking progress against stage-gate milestones: foundation, rebar, dock levelers, racking. Anything that slipped — a long-lead rack delivery, a concrete pour, a permit — I flagged to stakeholders as early as possible, because bad news ages badly.
>
> Then **closeout**: final walkthroughs, punch list, and certification — Certificate of Occupancy, fire-marshal sign-off on the life-safety systems, and commissioning of the MEP.
>
> And the honest postscript, which is the part I learned the most from: after turnover we found the ceiling-sprinkler clearances conflicted with the top of the racked storage — an oversized carton could intrude on a sprinkler head. So I ran a follow-up minor-works project to raise the sprinkler lines back into NFPA clearance. The lesson I took was that rack-to-sprinkler clearance has to be locked in design against the *real* carton sizes, not the nominal rack height."

Timeline: ~4 mo design + ~1 mo bid + ~6 mo construction + closeout ≈ "about a year overall."
