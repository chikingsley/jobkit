# "Walk me through a project end to end."

## Say it like this

So more recently I owned an owner's-rep retrofit of a Pfizer distribution warehouse, pretty much end to end. The reason for the project was simple. Pfizer was scaling up a couple of their commercial drugs, so they needed more storage and shipping capacity. We took an existing building and converted it. That meant moving the shipping and receiving team into the space, putting in the storage racking, and building out the electrical and the rest of the MEP to support it.

There was a senior PM who'd take the lead when we had to pitch executives for funding. But day to day I owned every step, so let me walk it through in phases.

In the design phase I was the owner's rep, and I worked directly with the design team. My main job there was making sure the scope was really tightly defined, because a loose scope is what comes back to bite you later. From there I ran the bid. I pre-qualified the contractors, ran the RFP, managed the walkthroughs and the RFI process, and then I presented my recommendation on who to award, along with the budget breakdown, to the stakeholders who had to sign off on the spend.

Through procurement I stayed on top of the vendors. Racking, dock equipment, the long-lead electrical gear. I tracked each one against when it was promised, so nothing snuck up on the schedule. We had a dedicated scheduler on this one, but I owned the schedule. I told her what went into it, I managed it in Microsoft Project, and we tracked cost and invoices in Kahua.

Once the budget and schedule were approved, we mobilized. During construction I was on site week to week. I'd walk the job for safety first, so OSHA compliance, PPE, stormwater, and then I'd track progress against our stage-gate milestones. Foundation, rebar, the dock levelers, the racking. If anything slipped, like a long-lead rack delivery or a concrete pour or a permit, I'd flag it to the stakeholders as early as I could, because bad news only gets worse the longer you sit on it.

Then we got into closeout. Final walkthroughs, the punch list, and certification. So the Certificate of Occupancy, the fire marshal sign-off on the life-safety systems, and commissioning of the MEP.

And honestly the part I learned the most from came after turnover. We found the ceiling sprinkler clearances were conflicting with the top of the racked storage, so an oversized carton could clip a sprinkler head. I ran a follow-up minor-works project to raise the sprinkler lines back into NFPA clearance. The lesson I took from it was that you have to lock the rack-to-sprinkler clearance in during design, and you check it against the real carton sizes, not just the nominal rack height.

## If they dig in

- **"What was the budget?"** Give the figure you've confirmed. Do not say "$5M" and "500,000 sq ft" together (that's $10/SF, too low; a racking+MEP retrofit is $40–150/SF). Lead with the dollars or the square footage, not the ratio.
- **"Whose fault was the sprinkler issue?"** Don't blame the designer. "It was a clearance oversight against the real carton sizes, and as owner's rep catching that is on me too. We fixed it fast, and now I check rack-to-sprinkler clearance against actual product dimensions at design review."
- **Timeline:** about a year overall (roughly 4 months design, a month to bid, 6 months construction, then closeout).
